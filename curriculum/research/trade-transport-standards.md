## 1. Incoterms 2020 (ICC Publication No. 723)

- Incoterms 2020 is a set of **eleven three-letter trade rules** published by the International Chamber of Commerce, in force since **1 January 2020**, defining seller/buyer tasks, costs and risk in contracts of sale. They are recognised by UNCITRAL as the global standard and are available in over 30 languages. (source: ICC, https://iccwbo.org/business-solutions/incoterms-rules/)
- The rules do **not** govern transfer of ownership/title, payment terms, or breach remedies — they allocate transport tasks, costs and risk only. (source: ICC Incoterms 2020 publication, via https://iccwbo.org/business-solutions/incoterms-rules/)
- **Four rules are maritime-only** (sea and inland waterway): **FAS** (Free Alongside Ship), **FOB** (Free On Board), **CFR** (Cost and Freight), **CIF** (Cost, Insurance and Freight). (source: ICC, https://iccwbo.org/business-solutions/incoterms-rules/incoterms-2020/)
- **Seven rules are any-mode** (road, rail, air, sea, multimodal): **EXW** (Ex Works), **FCA** (Free Carrier), **CPT** (Carriage Paid To), **CIP** (Carriage and Insurance Paid To), **DAP** (Delivered At Place), **DPU** (Delivered at Place Unloaded), **DDP** (Delivered Duty Paid). (source: ICC, https://iccwbo.org/business-solutions/incoterms-rules/incoterms-2020/)
- Standard allocation across the 11 rules (per ICC Incoterms 2020; table form is standard ICC/industry practice — see ICC quick charts, e.g. https://info.expeditors.com/hubfs/Incoterms%202020%20Chart%20with%20table.pdf):
  - **Export clearance** (export licence, customs formalities, export duties in the country of shipment): always the **seller** under all rules **except EXW**, where it is the buyer's obligation. This makes plain EXW impractical for international sales.
  - **Import clearance** (import formalities, import duties/taxes in the country of destination): always the **buyer** under all rules **except DDP**, where it is the seller's.
  - **Main carriage**: buyer arranges/pays under E and F rules (EXW, FCA, FAS, FOB); seller under C and D rules (CFR, CIF, CPT, CIP, DAP, DPU, DDP).
  - **Insurance**: a *duty to insure* exists only under **CIF and CIP**. Under CIF the seller insures at minimum cover **Institute Cargo Clauses (C)**; under CIP at the higher **Institute Cargo Clauses (A)** level. Under all other rules there is no obligation, though a party bears the risk and may insure in its own interest.
  - **Risk transfer (seller to buyer)**: EXW — goods placed at seller's disposal at its premises; FCA — goods handed to the buyer's carrier at the named place; FAS — goods placed alongside the ship at the port of shipment; FOB and CFR/CIF — **when goods are on board the vessel** at the port of shipment; CPT/CIP — **on handover to the first carrier**; DAP — goods placed at buyer's disposal, ready for unloading, at the named destination; DPU — same, but **after unloading**; DDP — same as DAP, plus import clearance done by seller.
- Under all four **"C" rules (CFR, CIF, CPT, CIP)**, the seller pays for carriage to destination **but risk passes much earlier** (on board at shipment / first carrier) — "paying for carriage does not mean bearing the risk to destination". (source: ICC Academy/ICC Incoterms 2020, via https://iccwbo.org/business-solutions/incoterms-rules/incoterms-2020/)
- **The 2020 changes vs Incoterms 2010**: (1) **DAT renamed DPU** (Delivered at Place Unloaded) to stress the destination can be any place, not just a terminal; DPU is the only rule where the seller unloads. (2) **FCA** gained an option (Articles A6/B6) for the buyer to instruct its carrier to issue an **on-board bill of lading to the seller**, who tenders it to the buyer — solving the container/FCA-vs-LOUC problem under letters of credit. (3) **CIP now requires ICC(A)-level cover** while **CIF keeps ICC(C)** as the default minimum, with parties free to agree more. (4) Costs consolidated into articles A9/B9; own-transport recognised (not always a third-party carrier); security-related requirements expanded in A4/A7. (source: ICC, https://iccwbo.org/business-solutions/incoterms-rules/incoterms-2020/)
- Common misuse warnings: FOB is often wrongly used for containerised cargo handed over at a terminal (FCA is the correct rule); EXW wrongly used for exports (seller can't clear export); DDP rarely workable for sellers without import registration/tax presence in the buyer's country; naming "Incoterms" without the year ("Incoterms 2020" + named place) is incomplete. (source: ICC guidance and trade.gov overview, https://www.trade.gov/know-your-incoterms — confidence high for the ICC points)

### SAFE PHRASINGS (Incoterms)
- "Incoterms 2020 is the ICC's set of 11 three-letter rules that say who does what, who pays, and where risk passes in an international sale. They do not decide who owns the goods or how payment is made."
- "Four rules — FAS, FOB, CFR and CIF — may only be used for sea transport. The other seven (EXW, FCA, CPT, CIP, DAP, DPU, DDP) can be used for any mode."
- "Only CIF and CIP contain an obligation to insure: CIF at minimum ICC(C) cover, CIP at the higher ICC(A) cover."
- "Under CFR, CIF, CPT and CIP the seller pays transport to the destination, but the risk passes to the buyer already at shipment — earlier than the destination."

## 2. Payment methods and risk allocation

- The main international payment methods, in increasing order of security for the **exporter**: advance payment (cash in advance / open-account terms like 30–60 days after invoice), documentary collection, and documentary credit (letter of credit). Advance payment is safest for the exporter and riskiest for the importer; open account is the reverse. (source: standard trade-finance structure; see ICC trade-finance materials and WTO/ICC reports)
- **Documentary collections** are governed by **ICC Uniform Rules for Collections (URC 522**, 1995 revision). Banks handle shipping documents against the buyer's payment or acceptance but give **no undertaking to pay** — payment depends entirely on the buyer. (source: ICC URC 522; consistent with ICC Banking Commission materials)
  - **D/P — documents against payment**: the buyer must pay before the bank releases the documents (and thus before it can collect the goods). Safer for the exporter.
  - **D/A — documents against acceptance**: the buyer receives the documents upon **accepting a time draft**, paying at maturity. The buyer can obtain the goods before paying, so D/A is riskier for the exporter than D/P.
- **Letters of credit (documentary credits)** are governed by **ICC UCP 600** (ICC Publication No. 600), effective **1 July 2007**, in **39 articles**. (source: ICC UCP 600, via ICC Banking Commission — confidence high; text paywalled)
  - A credit is an **irrevocable** undertaking by the issuing bank to pay against a **complying presentation** of documents (UCP 600 Articles 2, 3, 7).
  - The credit is **independent of the sales contract**: banks deal in documents, not goods (Articles 4 and 5). Discrepancies are the classic risk; banks examine documents on their face within a maximum of **five banking days** (Article 14) and must give a single notice of refusal listing all discrepancies (Article 16).
  - **Sight credit**: payment when complying documents are presented. **Usance/term/deferred-payment/acceptance credit**: payment at a later maturity (e.g. 90 days after B/L date).
  - **Confirmed credit**: a second bank (usually in the exporter's country) adds its own undertaking — used when the buyer's country/bank risk is high. **Unconfirmed** relies only on the issuing bank.
  - **Revolving credit**: reinstates itself up to a set amount/period instead of being re-issued per shipment.
  - **Standby letter of credit**: a guarantee-like instrument paid on default/non-performance against a stated document — governed by UCP 600 by choice or more commonly ISP98 — not a payment mechanism for the goods themselves.
- Risk spectrum (beginner formulation): advance payment = exporter's goods are safe but buyer pays before seeing goods; open account = buyer's goods arrive before payment; D/P sits between; D/A extends credit with less control; a confirmed, sight letter of credit is the closest to eliminating non-payment risk for the exporter — at the cost of bank fees and strict documentary compliance.

### SAFE PHRASINGS (payment)
- "Under a documentary collection the banks are a postal service for documents — they never promise to pay. Under a letter of credit the issuing bank does promise, if the documents comply exactly."
- "A letter of credit protects the seller only if the documents are perfect: the bank pays against documents, not against the actual goods."
- "D/P means pay to get the documents; D/A means sign a promise to pay later to get the documents."

## 3. WCO Harmonized System (HS)

- The Harmonized System is "a multipurpose international product nomenclature developed by the World Customs Organization (WCO)", described by the HS Convention; used by **more than 200 countries and economies** as the basis for customs tariffs and trade statistics, covering **more than 98% of world merchandise trade**. (source: WCO, https://www.wcoomd.org/en/topics/nomenclature/overview/what-is-the-harmonized-system.aspx)
- **Structure**: 21 sections → 96 chapters → **2-digit = chapter, 4-digit = heading, 6-digit = subheading**; more than 1,200 headings and **more than 5,000 commodity groups, each identified by a six-digit code**. (sources: WCO above; trade.gov, https://www.trade.gov/feature-article/overview-harmonized-system-codes)
- **Update cycle**: the WCO HS Committee amends the HS **every 5–6 years**. Current edition: **HS 2022, in force 1 January 2022** (seventh edition; 351 sets of amendments covering e-commerce, electronics/drones, security/dual-use goods and environmental provisions). **Next edition: HS 2028, in force 1 January 2028** (eighth edition, from the 7th review cycle). (sources: WCO, https://www.wcoomd.org/en/topics/nomenclature/instrument-and-tools/hs-nomenclature-2022-edition.aspx and https://www.wcoomd.org/en/topics/nomenclature/instrument-and-tools/hs-nomenclature-2028-edition.aspx)
- Exact subheading counts per edition vary between secondary sources (figures such as 5,765 for HS 2022 and 1,229 headings / 5,852 subheadings for HS 2028 circulate). **Teach "more than 5,000 six-digit subheadings"; do not assert a precise count.** (confidence: medium on precise counts)
- **Countries extend the six-digit code nationally** — typically 8 to 10 digits — for tariff rates, statistics and controls; national extensions are not standardised internationally. (source: trade.gov HS overview above)
- **Ghana applies the ECOWAS Common External Tariff (CET)**, based on the HS with ECOWAS/national 8–10-digit lines. The ECOWAS CET has **five tariff bands: 0%, 5%, 10%, 20% and 35%** (Ghana implemented the CET from 2016 under the Customs Act 2015 (Act 891); the 35% band was introduced by the 2019 CET revision). (confidence: high on the five bands; medium on the exact adoption dates of the 35% band)

### SAFE PHRASINGS (HS)
- "The HS is the WCO's universal product dictionary: every traded good gets a six-digit code that means the same thing in every country, and countries then add their own extra digits for duty rates."
- "Two digits pick the chapter, four the heading, six the subheading — and the same six digits must be used worldwide for statistics to match."

## 4. Transport documents

### Ocean bill of lading (B/L)
- The classic **three functions** of an ocean B/L: (1) **receipt for the goods** (prima facie/conclusive evidence of receipt); (2) **evidence of the contract of carriage**; (3) **document of title** — possession of a duly endorsed B/L controls the right to delivery, which is what makes it valuable for letters of credit and selling goods in transit. (source: Wikipedia "Bill of lading" summarising maritime law, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_of_lading; consistent with Hague-Visby framework — confidence high)
- **Negotiable (order) B/L** vs **straight B/L**: an order bill is consigned "to order" and transfers control by endorsement; a straight bill names one consignee and is not negotiable in the shipping sense. (source: as above)
- **Clean vs claused ("dirty") B/L**: clean = no remarks on the goods' apparent condition; claused = carrier notes defects (damaged packaging, leaks). Banks under a letter of credit require a clean B/L. (source: as above)
- **Master B/L vs House B/L**: the master B/L is issued by the ocean carrier (to the freight forwarder/NVOCC as shipper); the house B/L is issued by the forwarder to the actual shipper. (industry standard — confidence high)
- **Sea waybill**: the non-negotiable alternative — receipt and evidence of contract but **not a document of title**; cargo is released to the named consignee without presenting any paper, which suits trusted, open-account trade. (source: as above)
- **Telex release (express release)**: the shipper surrenders the full set of originals at origin and the carrier authorises electronic release at destination — waybill-like speed with B/L-based control up to surrender. (source: Wikipedia B/L article, above)

### Air waybill (AWB)
- The AWB is the **IATA-standard** air transport document. It is **always non-negotiable**: receipt, evidence of contract, freight bill and customs document — but **not a document of title**. (source: IATA-aligned industry references — confidence high)
- **MAWB** (master, issued by the airline) vs **HAWB** (house, issued by the freight forwarder). **AWB number = 11 digits**: 3-digit IATA airline prefix + 7-digit serial + 1 check digit (modulo 7 of the first eight). (industry standard — confidence high)
- **e-AWB**: under **IATA Resolution 672** (Multilateral e-AWB Agreement) the electronic AWB is the **default contract of carriage since 1 January 2019** on enabled trade lanes; paper became the exception. (source: IATA, https://www.iata.org/en/programs/cargo/e/efreight)

### Road and rail
- **CMR consignment note**: the CMR Convention (Geneva, 19 May 1956; in force 1961) standardises international road carriage and its consignment note. The note **accompanies the goods**, is **non-negotiable** (a receipt and evidence of contract, not a document of title), and an electronic **eCMR** is permitted under the 2008 Additional Protocol. (source: Wikipedia CMR Convention, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CMR_Convention — confidence high)
- **Ghana is not a CMR contracting party**; the convention's ~58 parties (as of January 2022) are overwhelmingly European plus a few states (e.g. Morocco, Tunisia, Iran, Kazakhstan). **West African road movements therefore use a generic (national/ECOWAS) consignment note**, not a CMR note. (source: party list on the Wikipedia CMR article — confidence high on non-membership; medium on the exact count over time)
- **Rail**: **CIM** consignment note under COTIF (administered by **OTIF**, Bern) in most of Europe/neighbouring regions, and the **SMGS** consignment note under the OSJD agreement in much of Asia/Eastern Europe; a **joint CIM/SMGS note** covers through journeys between the two systems. Both are non-negotiable carriage documents. (confidence: medium — rulebooks paywalled; structure uncontroversial)

### Multimodal
- **FIATA FBL — FIATA Multimodal Transport Bill of Lading**: a document issued by freight forwarders acting as multimodal transport operators, covering door-to-door movements across modes; the **negotiable FBL** is recognised worldwide as a document of title acceptable under letters of credit; a digital **eFBL** exists, mapped to UN/CEFACT standards. (sources: FIATA, https://fiata.org/resources; UNECE press on the eFBL data standard, https://unece.org/media/trade/press/365715)

### SAFE PHRASINGS (documents)
- "Only an ocean bill of lading (and documents like the FIATA FBL) is a document of title. Air waybills, road consignment notes and sea waybills are receipts and evidence of the contract — never title documents."
- "A clean bill says the goods were shipped in apparent good order; a claused bill shows defects, and banks will usually reject it."
- "Telex release: hand the originals back at origin and the line releases the cargo at destination electronically."

## 5. Trade certificates

- **Certificate of origin (C/O)** certifies that goods are wholly obtained, produced, manufactured or processed in a particular country. **Non-preferential** C/Os (traditionally certified by chambers of commerce under ICC World Chambers Federation guidelines) support MFN treatment, anti-dumping measures, sanctions checks and marking rules. **Preferential** C/Os (or origin declarations/self-certification) evidence qualification under a specific trade agreement and must be presented to claim reduced duties. (source: Wikipedia "Certificate of origin", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Certificate_of_origin; consistent with WCO Origin standards)
- **Ghana-relevant preferential certificates**: the **ECOWAS Certificate of Origin under the ETLS** (ECOWAS Trade Liberalisation Scheme) allows duty-free movement of goods approved as originating in ECOWAS member states; ECOWAS launched an **electronic ETLS certificate of origin (October 2024)**, which Ghana has adopted. The **AfCFTA Certificate of Origin** evidences preference under African Continental Free Trade Area rules of origin. (sources: ECOWAS ETLS portal, https://etls.ecowas.int/faqs/how-to-get-a-certificate-of-origin-for-approved-industrial-goods; GIZ Ghana announcement of the Ghana e-cert launch; confidence high on existence/purpose, medium on rollout detail)
- **Phytosanitary certificate**: issued by the plant-protection authority of the exporting country in IPPC model format (**Article V of the International Plant Protection Convention**) for plants and plant products; it certifies phytosanitary status only — pest-risk matters. (sources: IPPC, https://www.ippc.int; WTO SPS distinction)
- **Health/sanitary (veterinary) certificate**: the animal-and-human-health equivalent, required for products of animal origin (meat, dairy, live animals, hides) — under the WTO SPS Agreement "sanitary" protects human/animal health, "phytosanitary" protects plant health. (source: WTO SPS Agreement framework)
- **Quality/inspection certificates**: third-party (or buyer-mandated) inspection reports/certificates attesting quality, quantity or condition at loading — common in commodity and bulk trades; contents are contractual, not standardised globally. (confidence: high as a concept)
- **Dangerous goods declaration**: the shipper's modal declaration (e.g. Shipper's Declaration for Dangerous Goods under IATA DGR; Dangerous Goods Declaration under IMDG) stating UN number, proper shipping name, class, packing group, quantities and packing/labeling compliance. (sources: IATA DGR / IMDG Code structure — see Section 9)

### SAFE PHRASINGS (certificates)
- "A certificate of origin says where the goods come from. The non-preferential kind is routine paperwork; the preferential kind is money — it is what unlocks duty-free or reduced-duty entry under a trade agreement such as ETLS or AfCFTA."
- "A phytosanitary certificate is for plants and plant products; a sanitary or health certificate is for animal products. Each is issued by the exporting country's competent authority, not by the trader."

## 6. Containers (ISO 668 / ISO 1496)

- Freight containers are standardised under **ISO 668** (classification, dimensions, ratings) and **ISO 1496** (specification and testing). ISO 668 gives **minimum internal dimensions**; carriers' data sheets give typical figures, which is what textbooks quote. (source: Wikipedia ISO 668, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_668)
- **20ft dry ("standard")**: internal approx. **5.898 × 2.352 × 2.393 m** (≈33 m³); **max gross mass 30,480 kg** (67,200 lb); tare approx. **2,230 kg**; payload approx. **28,250 kg**. (source: Wikipedia "Intermodal container", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermodal_container — confidence high for the typical figures)
- **40ft dry**: internal approx. **12.032 × 2.352 × 2.393 m** (≈67.5 m³); max gross commonly rated 30,480 kg (some 32,500 kg); tare approx. 3,750–3,800 kg; payload approx. 26,700 kg. (source: as above)
- **40ft high cube**: external height **9 ft 6 in (2.896 m)**, internal height approx. **2.698 m**, volume ≈75–76 m³; footprint identical to standard 40ft. (source: as above)
- ISO 668's 2016 amendment raised the permissible maximum gross to **36,000 kg** for plating-tested boxes; in practice **road weight limits**, not the container rating, usually cap payload. Teach 30,480 kg as the traditional design max gross and check the CSC/equipment plate per box. (confidence: high that both figures exist; medium on how commonly 36 t-rated boxes are used)
- **Special types**: **reefer** (refrigerated, insulated, for perishables — about 6% of the fleet); **open top** (removable tarpaulin roof, crane-loaded over-height/long cargo); **flat rack** (no sides or roof, out-of-gauge machinery); **ISO tank** (tank in a 20ft frame for liquids/gases/powders, often dangerous goods — under 1% of the fleet). (source: Wikipedia Intermodal container, above)
- **TEU / FEU**: twenty-foot / forty-foot equivalent units — capacity counted in standard 20ft or 40ft boxes; **1 FEU = 2 TEU**; a 40ft high cube still counts as 1 FEU. (source: as above)
- **Terminology**: **tare** = empty container weight; **payload** = max permissible cargo weight (max gross minus tare); **max gross** = tare + cargo (safety-rated total). VGM (Section 7) is the verified *actual* gross of the packed box, not the rating. (sources: ISO 668 terminology + SOLAS VGM framework)

### SAFE PHRASINGS (containers)
- "A standard 20-foot box takes about 28 tonnes of cargo and roughly 33 cubic metres; a 40-foot box doubles the volume but not the permitted weight — heavy cargo goes in 20-footers, light bulky cargo in 40-foot or high-cube boxes."
- "Max gross is the legal maximum for box + cargo; tare is the empty box; payload is what's left for cargo."

## 7. Ocean freight operations

- **Liner vs tramp**: liner services run published routes and schedules at published tariffs (container shipping); tramp vessels (bulk, tanker chartering) go where cargo demands under charter parties. (industry standard — confidence high)
- **Principal vessel types**: container ships; **dry bulk carriers** (grain, ores, clinker); **tankers** (crude/products/chemicals/LNG); **RoRo** (roll-on/roll-off for vehicles); **general/breakbulk** (unitised or project cargo on conventional ships). (industry standard — confidence high)
- Booking flow: cargo is **booked** with the line (or via a forwarder); the line confirms with a **booking confirmation**; historically a **shipping order** instructed the ship to receive cargo (today mostly electronic workflows). (confidence: medium on the shipping-order term's current usage)
- **Cut-offs** before each sailing: **cargo/VGM cut-off** — when the packed, VGM-declared container must be at the terminal (CY) or CFS; **documentation (SI) cut-off** — deadline for the shipping instruction/B/L details. Missing a cut-off usually means rollover to the next vessel. (sources: carrier explainer practice — e.g. Maersk/Hapag-Lloyd education pages — confidence medium-high)
- **VGM (Verified Gross Mass)**: SOLAS Regulation **VI/2**, mandatory since **1 July 2016** — the **shipper** must obtain and communicate the verified gross mass of every packed container, by **Method 1** (weighing the packed container) or **Method 2** (tare + contents, using calibrated equipment, per IMO Guidelines MSC.1/Circ.1475); **no container without VGM may be loaded**. (source: IMO, https://www.imo.org/en/OurWork/Safety/Pages/Verification-of-the-gross-mass.aspx)
- **B/L issuance and telex release**: see Section 4. Cargo release without paper: sea waybill or telex/express release.
- **Typical freight-rate components** (definitions, no amounts): **ocean freight** (base line-haul rate, per container or per tonne/revision); **BAF / bunker adjustment factor** (fuel surcharge; now often expressed via attached index clauses) and **CAF / currency adjustment factor** (exchange-rate surcharge); **THC — terminal handling charge**, at **origin (OTHC)** and **destination (DTHC)**, covering container handling at terminals; **CFS charges** for container freight station handling of LCL cargo; **documentation fee** (B/L issuance); **seal charge**; **ISPS charge** (security cost recovery under the SOLAS/ISPS Code). (sources: carrier/industry glossaries — Maersk Logistics Explained, iContainers — confidence medium-high; names are universal, application varies by line and trade)
- **GRI — general rate increase**: a liner-pricing term for an **across-the-board upward adjustment of base freight rates**, typically announced for contract seasons or capacity management on a trade lane; distinct from ad-hoc surcharges. (industry practice — confidence medium-high)

### SAFE PHRASINGS (ocean)
- "Before a container ship sails, two clocks matter: the cargo cut-off (box must be at the terminal, VGM declared) and the documentation cut-off (shipping instructions finalised). Miss either and your box waits a week for the next vessel."
- "The freight quote is rarely one number: base ocean freight plus terminal handling at both ends, documentation, seal, security (ISPS), and fuel-related surcharges such as BAF."

## 8. Air cargo

- **Chargeable weight = the higher of gross weight and volumetric weight**. The IATA volumetric formula: **volumetric weight (kg) = L × W × H in centimetres ÷ 6000** (6,000 cm³ = 1 kg; ≈166.67 kg per m³). Some carriers/contracts use 5,000 or 7,000 as contractual divisors; **6,000 is the IATA standard**. (sources: Maersk "Air cargo chargeable weight", https://www.maersk.com/logistics-explained/transportation-and-freight/2025/03/10/air-cargo-chargeable-weight; IATA-aligned industry references)
- **ULD (unit load device)**: aircraft containers and pallets that consolidate cargo for loading; certified to IATA ULD Regulations (ULDR) and treated as aircraft equipment. Common examples: **AKE** (LD3-type lower-deck container) and **PMC** (96 × 125 inch main/lower-deck pallet). Keep treatment light — the two-letter/one-digit type codes identify deck, footprint and restraint. (confidence: medium-high)
- **e-AWB**: see Section 4 — default since 1 January 2019 on enabled lanes under IATA Resolution 672. Historical adoption figures (≈56% in Sept 2018; ≈64–66% in Oct 2019) date quickly; do not quote current percentages without a live source. (source: IATA, https://www.iata.org/en/programs/cargo/e/efreight)
- **Cargo acceptance basics**: confirmed booking/AWB, security screening/status under national regulated-agent regimes, acceptance checks (marking, packaging, ULD build-up), and **DGR acceptance** — dangerous goods accepted only by trained, authorised staff against the IATA DGR checklists. (industry standard — confidence medium-high)

### SAFE PHRASINGS (air)
- "Air cargo pays by volume as well as weight: divide L × W × H in centimetres by 6,000, and the airline charges whichever is heavier — the real weight or the volumetric weight."
- "An air waybill is never negotiable — the cargo goes to the named consignee, full stop."

## 9. Dangerous goods

- The **UN Model Regulations** divide dangerous goods into **nine classes**: **1 Explosives** (divisions 1.1–1.6); **2 Gases** (2.1 flammable, 2.2 non-flammable non-toxic, 2.3 toxic); **3 Flammable liquids**; **4 Flammable solids** (4.1 flammable solids/self-reactive, 4.2 liable to spontaneous combustion, 4.3 emit flammable gas with water); **5 Oxidising substances and organic peroxides** (5.1 oxidisers, 5.2 organic peroxides); **6 Toxic and infectious substances** (6.1 toxic, 6.2 infectious); **7 Radioactive material**; **8 Corrosive substances**; **9 Miscellaneous dangerous substances and articles** (includes lithium batteries, dry ice, environmentally hazardous substances). (source: UN Model Regulations structure, reflected in IMO/IATA/UNECE materials — confidence high)
- **UN numbers** identify each regulated substance/article (four digits, "UN" prefix, e.g. UN 1950 aerosols; UN 3480 lithium-ion batteries) together with its **proper shipping name**. (confidence high; do not quote the current upper bound of the UN-number range)
- **Packing groups** grade danger for packing performance: **PG I great danger, PG II medium danger, PG III minor danger** — apply to most classes **except 1, 2 and 7**. (confidence high)
- **Modal rulebooks**: **IMDG Code** (International Maritime Dangerous Goods Code — sea, mandatory under SOLAS Chapter VII, updated on a two-year amendment cycle); **IATA DGR** (air, based on the ICAO Technical Instructions, updated annually); **ADR** (road — a **European** UNECE agreement; note for Ghana/West Africa teaching that ADR does not apply there, though its classification and UN-number system are global). (sources: IMO/IATA/UNECE — confidence high)
- **SDS/MSDS (safety data sheet)**: the 16-section GHS document from the manufacturer classifying a product's hazards; it is the starting point for deciding **if** goods are regulated for transport and under what UN entry — but the SDS itself is not the transport declaration.
- **DG declaration, packing and labeling**: the shipper declares DG on the modal declaration, uses UN-performance packaging, marks/packages with UN numbers and proper shipping names, and applies hazard **labels** on packages and **placards** on units/vehicles as the mode requires. (confidence high)

### SAFE PHRASINGS (DG)
- "Nine classes cover everything from explosives (Class 1) to the miscellaneous Class 9 where lithium batteries live. Classes 1, 2 and 7 have no packing group."
- "Same chemical, same UN number, but three rulebooks: IMDG for sea, IATA DGR for air, ADR for European roads. The UN classification travels; the road rulebook does not leave Europe."

## 10. Cargo insurance

- **Institute Cargo Clauses (ICC)** — the standard market clauses (current version 2009, published with the Institute of London Underwriters/Lloyd's market association wording) provide a three-level ladder: **(A)** covers **all risks of loss or damage**, subject to exclusions (wilful misconduct, ordinary wear/leakage, inherent vice, delay, war and strikes unless separately bought); **(B)** and **(C)** are **named-perils** covers listing the insured events. **(B) adds** risks such as washing overboard, water entry, and loss/damage in loading/discharge relative to **(C)**, which covers **major casualties only** (fire, explosion, vessel stranding/grounding/sinking/capsizing, collision/derailment/overturning of conveyance, discharge at a port of distress, general average sacrifice). Premium rises with the ladder: C cheapest, A dearest. (sources: clause-comparison references, e.g. https://jameshallam.co.uk/institute-cargo-clauses-a-b-c and broker/underwriter comparison charts — confidence high on the ladder; the clause texts themselves are paywalled)
- **General average**: the maritime-law principle (codified in the **York-Antwerp Rules**, first 1890, updated 1994/2004/2016) under which a **voluntary sacrifice** (e.g. jettison) or extraordinary expense made to preserve the **common adventure** from a common peril is shared proportionally among ship, cargo and freight interests; modern declarations include the *Ever Given* (2021) and *Maersk Honam* (2018) cases. Cargo owners post security (typically via insurers) before delivery. (source: Wikipedia "General average", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_average — confidence high)
- **Insurable interest**: the insured must stand to gain from the cargo's safe arrival or suffer from its loss — the principle that ties insurance cover to a genuine stake in the goods (foundation of marine insurance law; taught as a general principle). (confidence: high as a principle)
- **Certificate of insurance**: the short document evidencing that a shipment is covered under an open/marine policy — commonly required under CIF/CIP so the buyer receives assignable evidence of cover.
- **Incoterms tie-in**: only **CIF and CIP** oblige the seller to insure for the buyer's benefit — CIF to **ICC(C) minimum**, CIP to **ICC(A)** — covering, at minimum, the shipment-leg risk from the port/place of shipment to the named destination, for **110% of the contract value** (the customary sum insured under the clauses; the 110% figure is standard market practice reflected in the clauses and ICC guidance — confidence medium-high). Sellers of goods under CIF/CIP should ensure the insurance period covers the full transit risk passed to the buyer. (ICC source for cover levels: https://iccwbo.org/business-solutions/incoterms-rules/incoterms-2020/)

### SAFE PHRASINGS (insurance)
- "ICC(C) covers the ship sinking and burning; ICC(B) adds water and handling damage; ICC(A) covers everything unless it is specifically excluded. The alphabet is a coverage ladder."
- "General average is the oldest sharing rule in shipping: if cargo is deliberately sacrificed to save the voyage, everyone with goods on board shares the loss — even those whose cargo was untouched."

## 11. Container charges: demurrage, detention, storage

- **Free time**: the allowance (commonly 2–7 days for imports; varies by carrier, terminal and trade) before time-based charges begin. **Last free day (LFD)**: the final day without charges. (source: iContainers, https://www.icontainers.com/help/demurrage-detention-per-diem-shipping-delay-charges; Terminal49 glossary)
- **Demurrage**: charged by the **ocean carrier** when an import container stays **inside the terminal** beyond free time before pickup. (source: as above; consistent with Maersk explainer, https://www.maersk.com/logistics-explained/transportation-and-freight/2023/08/28/what-is-demurrage-detention-in-shipping-for-buyers)
- **Detention**: charged by the **ocean carrier** when the container is **outside the terminal** (with the consignee, in transit) but is **not returned empty to the carrier's depot** by the due date. (sources: as above)
- **Storage**: the **terminal's own** space charge for cargo/containers occupying terminal land — separate from, and additional to, carrier demurrage. (source: as above)
- **Variants**: charges differ for **import vs export** legs and by equipment type (reefer free time is usually shorter); lines publish **combined / "through" detention & demurrage** tariffs where one clock runs from discharge to empty return (common in US practice the clocks differ from Europe/Africa usage — teach the inside/outside distinction, not a universal day count). (confidence: medium on regional practice details)
- Rates and free days are **purely contractual tariffs** of each line/terminal — no standard amounts exist internationally. (confidence high)

### SAFE PHRASINGS (charges)
- "Demurrage is paying to keep a full box sitting in the port; detention is paying to keep the line's box out of the port too long; storage is what the terminal charges for the space regardless of who owns the box."

## 12. Customs concepts

- **Single Window**: "a facility that allows parties involved in trade and transport to lodge standardized information and documents with a single entry point to fulfil all import, export, and transit-related regulatory requirements" — the definition originates in **UNECE Recommendation No. 33** (updated 2020) and is endorsed and used by the **WCO**; Ghana's trade single window operates through **ICUMS** (Integrated Customs Management System). (source: UNECE Rec. 33, https://unece.org/DAM/cefact/recommendations/rec33/rec33_trd352e.pdf and 2020 edition; confidence high)
- **Pre-arrival declaration**: customs lodging of cargo manifest/declaration data **before arrival**, enabling advance risk analysis and faster release (basis of e-manifest/ACI/ISF-style regimes; consistent with WCO Revised Kyoto Convention Standard 3.21 and WCO data model practice). (confidence: high as a concept)
- **Risk management / selectivity**: customs target intervention using criteria-based systems; declarations are channelled to **green** (release without inspection), **yellow** (document check) or **red** (physical inspection) — a channel model used in many administrations, including Ghana's ICUMS. (industry/agency practice — confidence high as a concept)
- **Authorised Economic Operator (AEO)**: created under the **WCO SAFE Framework of Standards** (adopted June 2005; SAFE 2025 edition current). Pillar I = customs-to-customs cooperation; Pillar II = **customs-to-business partnership**, under which an operator with a sound security/compliance record is certified by customs and receives facilitation benefits; **mutual recognition arrangements (MRAs)** extend benefits across borders. (source: WCO SAFE Framework, https://www.wcoomd.org/-/media/wco/public/global/pdf/topics/facilitation/instruments-and-tools/tools/safe-package/safe-framework-2025_en.pdf — confidence high)
- **WTO Valuation Agreement** (Agreement on Implementation of Article VII of GATT 1994): six methods applied **sequentially**: (1) **transaction value** — the price actually paid or payable for the goods when sold for export, adjusted per Article 8 additions; (2) transaction value of **identical goods**; (3) transaction value of **similar goods**; (4) **deductive value** (resale price minus margins/costs); (5) **computed value** (cost of production + profit); (6) **fall-back** — any reasonable means consistent with the Agreement. **Methods 4 and 5 may be reversed at the importer's request**; arbitrary/fictitious or importing-country selling prices are prohibited as bases. (source: WCO FAQ on Customs Valuation, https://www.wcoomd.org/en/topics/valuation/overview/faq-valuation.aspx — confidence high)
- **Rules of origin**: **non-preferential** origin (for MFN duty, anti-dumping, marking, statistics) vs **preferential** origin (qualifying goods under a trade agreement get reduced duties). Core tests include **wholly obtained** and **substantial transformation** (change of tariff heading, ad valorem percentage, or processing criteria). Preference claims need a valid certificate/declaration of origin (see Section 5). (sources: WCO origin framework + Wikipedia C/O article — confidence high)
- **Transit regimes**: **TIR Convention** (Geneva, 1975; administered by UNECE with the IRU-managed guarantee): goods move in **sealed** vehicles/containers under a **TIR carnet** with duties suspended across multiple borders, backed by an international guarantee chain; ~78–79 contracting parties (mostly Europe/Central Asia/Middle East) — **Ghana is not a party**. (source: Wikipedia TIR Convention, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TIR_Convention — confidence high)
- **ECOWAS ISRT — Inter-State Road Transit Convention** (adopted 29 May 1982, Convention A/P4/5/82): the West African customs-transit scheme allowing goods to move by road between customs offices of ECOWAS member states **under duty suspension**, with customs **seals**, a **single transit document** (the "triptyque") and a guarantee system — the regional analogue of TIR; being digitised (SIGMAT electronic transit system). Ghana applies ISRT for transit cargo to landlocked neighbours. (sources: Convention text via AfricanLII, https://africanlii.org/akn/aa-ecowas/act/convention/1982/5-p4/eng@1982-05-29/publication; ECOWAS updates on SIGMAT — confidence high on existence/purpose; medium on operational details)

### SAFE PHRASINGS (customs)
- "A single window means the trader submits everything once, to one place, and government agencies share the data — instead of chasing the same form around five agencies."
- "Valuation starts with what you actually paid (transaction value). Only if customs cannot accept it do the five fallback methods come in, in strict order — and the importer can ask for methods 4 and 5 to swap places."
- "TIR is the global transit passport; the ISRT 'triptyque' is West Africa's own version of the same idea — seals and a guarantee so duties are paid at the final destination, not at every border."

## DO NOT CLAIM (commonly misstated or unverifiable)

1. **No precise HS subheading count.** WCO says "more than 5,000 commodity groups" with a six-digit code; secondary sources conflict on exact per-edition counts (e.g. 5,765 for HS 2022; 1,229 headings/5,852 subheadings for HS 2028). Teach "more than 5,000"; never cite a specific number without a dated WCO source.
2. **No exact Incoterms risk phrasing beyond the ICC text** — and never say Incoterms govern ownership, payment, or that CIF/CIP cover "the whole journey's risk" as an Incoterms duty beyond the contract value basis; also never call DPU "delivered at place unloaded" anything other than the renamed DAT.
3. **Never use FOB for container handovers at a terminal**, and never say the seller must clear export under EXW in practice without warning it is the buyer's Incoterms obligation.
4. **CMR in West Africa**: do not present the CMR note as the West African road document. Ghana is not a CMR state; regional road moves use generic/ECOWAS consignment notes.
5. **Current freight indices, carrier rates, demurrage/detention amounts, and e-AWB adoption percentages**: none may be quoted. They change continuously (Drewry/SCFI indices, carrier tariffs, e-AWB ~56%/66% figures date from 2018–2019).
6. **ISO container weights**: do not mix 30,480 kg (traditional max gross), 32,500 kg (some 40ft ratings) and 36,000 kg (ISO 668:2020 amendment) without explaining which applies; payload must be derived from tare per the equipment plate, and road limits usually cap real loads.
7. **Packing groups do not apply to Classes 1, 2 and 7** — never attach PG I/II/III to explosives, gases or radioactive material.
8. **ADR is a European agreement** — do not teach it as the road rulebook applying in Ghana/West Africa (classification is global; the agreement is not).
9. **UCP 600 does not "guarantee payment"** — it provides a bank undertaking only against a complying presentation; never say banks check goods.
10. **110% insurance value under CIF/CIP** is market practice reflected in the clauses; the *Incoterms* text sets minimum cover and price basis — do not invent other percentages.
11. **VGM is not optional and is not the carrier's job**: since 1 July 2016 the shipper provides it (SOLAS VI/2); no VGM, no loading.
12. **Exact ISRT/TIR operational details (document forms, guarantee amounts, current SIGMAT rollout status)**: describe the concept; specifics vary by administration — direct learners to the national customs authority.
13. **Anything marked (medium confidence) above** — exact HS 2028 subheading counts, CIM/SMGS procedural details, ECOWAS CET 35%-band adoption dates, current free-time norms, shipping-order usage — must be re-verified or taught as general concepts, not figures.
