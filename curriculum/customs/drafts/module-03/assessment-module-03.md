=== ASSESSMENT Q1 | tests lesson 3.1 ===
QUESTION:
A container has been released by customs but has not left the terminal for six days, and storage charges are accruing. The importer demands that the Customs Division "order the terminal to release it free of storage". Which option best evaluates the demand?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The demand is justified: customs released the goods, so customs controls everything that happens to them afterwards, including the terminal's charges.
2. The demand is justified only if the goods were examined before release.
3. The demand confuses two threads: release is the customs decision under section 6(1), and it has been made; the delivery sequence and the accruing storage are the terminal's operational services, charged on the port's published tariff — the remedy runs through the terminal's procedures, not through a customs order.
4. The demand confuses two threads, and the storage charges are unlawful once the goods are released.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. The lesson's core distinction is regulatory decision versus operational service. Release under section 6(1) is the customs decision — the conditions were met, and the goods left customs control. What runs after release is the terminal's operational layer: the delivery sequence, the gate pass, and the storage charges on the port's published tariff. The two threads have different owners and different remedies — a release delay is answered by the clearance provisions; a delivery delay is answered by the terminal's procedures and the commercial terms.

Answer 1 makes customs the manager of the yard because it controls the goods in the yard — the control ended at release, and it never included the terminal's tariff. Answer 4 asserts an unlawfulness that does not exist: post-release storage on a published rate is the commercial layer working normally.

The takeaway: diagnose by actor and capacity — customs answers for the release, the terminal answers for the delivery, and the frustrated importer is talking to the wrong counterparty.

=== ASSESSMENT Q2 | tests lesson 3.1 ===
QUESTION:
A private company wants to run a yard for handling unreleased import containers near the harbour. Which option best states what makes its yard part of the customs control system?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Nothing is required — any yard near a port is automatically a customs-controlled area.
2. The company applies to the Commissioner-General for a licence under section 46(1) to operate the area as a customs-controlled area — the second kind of area the section describes being precisely for the temporary storage of unreleased imported goods, including undeclared and seized goods.
3. The Minister designates private yards by Gazette notice, as with ports.
4. The yard becomes a customs-controlled area when the first container arrives.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 46(1) of Act 891 allows a person in charge of an area for the temporary storage of goods imported at a port of entry and not yet released — including goods not yet declared and goods seized as liable to forfeiture — to apply to the Commissioner-General for a licence to operate the area as a customs-controlled area. The licence brings the private yard inside the legal perimeter: the Authority's control over goods, persons and conveyances extends into it, and section 5(1)'s permitted activities apply there.

Answer 1 dissolves the perimeter that designation and licensing create. Answer 3 misassigns the power: the Minister's Gazette notices designate ports and approved places, not private yards; the Commissioner-General's licence is the section 46 route. Answer 4 confuses physical use with legal status — the perimeter is created by designation or licence, never by cargo.

The takeaway: a private yard enters the customs system by the licence route, and until it does, its goods' lawful handling runs on different provisions entirely.

=== ASSESSMENT Q3 | tests lesson 3.1 ===
QUESTION:
In a client briefing, an agent states: "Terminal operator XYZ handles 70 percent of Tema's containers, according to my experience." Which option best assesses the statement against the programme's rules?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The statement is acceptable, because the agent works at Tema daily.
2. The statement is acceptable because terminal market shares are published publicly.
3. The statement breaches the anchoring discipline: the programme's sources name the port authority and the Ports of Tema and Takoradi, not private operators or market shares — and institutional facts come only from an anchor, a fact sheet, or a verified authoritative source (spec C4.5). The memo should describe the operator generically or verify and cite the claim.
4. The statement is acceptable because operators' names are not factual claims.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. The writing spec's rule C4.5 governs institutional facts — who operates or regulates what — and requires an anchor, a fact sheet, or a verified authoritative source, with the citation. The programme's sources (the port operators deck) name the Ghana Ports and Harbours Authority and the Ports of Tema and Takoradi; they do not name private terminal operators or their market shares. A percentage attributed to "my experience" is precisely the unverifiable claim the rule removes from professional writing.

Answer 1 substitutes presence for sourcing — daily presence is how unverified claims feel true. Answer 2 asserts a public record without citing it; if the share is published, the citation is easy and the claim should carry it. Answer 4 is wrong on the nature of the claim: an operator's name and share are exactly the institutional facts the rule governs.

The takeaway: generic until sourced — describe the actor, or verify and cite the name and the number. Both are professional; the unsourced specific is neither.

=== ASSESSMENT Q4 | tests lesson 3.2 ===
QUESTION:
A shipment's paperwork names four possible actors who could have lodged its declaration: the goods' owner, the importer, the exporter, and a licensed customs house agent acting under written authority. Which option best states the Act's position on who may lodge?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Only the owner may lodge, because the owner bears the duty.
2. Any four are lawful declarant identities: section 151 defines the declarant as the person who makes a declaration for customs purposes — the owner of the goods, the importer, the exporter, or the agent — and the agent's lodgement rests on the licence (s.43) and the written authority (s.44).
3. Only the agent may lodge, because declarations are technical documents.
4. Any employee of any of the four may lodge on the firm's behalf.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 151 of Act 891 defines the declarant as the person who makes a declaration for customs purposes, and it names the lawful identities: the owner of the goods, the importer, the exporter, or the agent. The agent route carries its own machinery — the licence to carry out customs business (section 43, and the section 151 definition of customs house agent) and the written authority to produce on demand (section 44).

Answer 1 narrows the four identities to one; the duty's incidence and the lodgement's identity are separate questions. Answer 3 is the opposite narrowing — declarations are lodged by importers and exporters themselves every day. Answer 4 invents a fifth door: an employee who is none of the four identities, lodging for another without the licence, is outside the Act's scheme.

The takeaway: four lawful identities, exhaustive — own name (owner, importer, exporter) or licensed agent with written authority. Every declaration names which identity lodged.

=== ASSESSMENT Q5 | tests lesson 3.2 ===
QUESTION:
An officer asks an agent for the written authority behind a declaration. The agent produces the firm's customs house agent licence and declines to show anything else. Which option best evaluates the response?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The response is correct: the licence is the authority section 44 contemplates.
2. The response is incorrect: section 43's licence authorises the firm to carry out customs business as a profession, while section 44 requires production of the authority for the specific act — the principal's written instruction — and the officer's demand is answered with that paper.
3. The response is incorrect because agents may never reveal client papers to customs.
4. The response is correct if the declaration contained no errors.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. The lesson separates the two papers: the licence under section 43 — the grant to carry out customs business, defined in section 151 as an activity that has to do with the importation or exportation of goods — authorises the profession. The authority under section 44 authorises the engagement: the principal's written instruction for the specific act, which the agent must produce on demand. The officer's question is not "may you act as an agent?" but "did this principal instruct this lodgement?" — and the licence cannot answer that.

Answer 1 merges the papers, which is the confusion section 44 exists to prevent: a licence tells the officer nothing about this client's instruction. Answer 3 invents a confidentiality shield over the very document the Act makes producible. Answer 4 makes the authority's necessity depend on the declaration's quality — an error-free declaration from an unauthorised agent is still lodged without the authority the Act requires.

The takeaway: licence for the profession, authority for the engagement — the officer's demand under section 44 is answered by the engagement paper, every time.

=== ASSESSMENT Q6 | tests lesson 3.2 ===
QUESTION:
A bonded carrier licensed under section 45 is offered a contract to move goods that are NOT under any customs procedure — ordinary cleared goods from a warehouse to a shop. Which option best states the position?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The carrier may not move them, because its licence covers only bonded goods and ordinary goods require a different carrier licence.
2. The carrier may move them as an ordinary carrier would — the licence's special force is that it authorises the transport of bonded goods within the country, which is what provisions like section 95(1)(a)'s transit movement require; ordinary cleared goods move under ordinary carriage.
3. The carrier must move them duty-free, because its licence exempts everything it carries.
4. The goods become bonded goods automatically once the bonded carrier carries them.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 151 of Act 891 defines a bonded carrier as a carrier licensed in accordance with the Act to transport bonded goods within the country — the licence's special force is the authority over goods under customs procedure, which is what section 95(1)(a)'s transit movement invokes when it permits movement "by a bonded carrier". Cleared goods in free circulation move under ordinary carriage law; the carrier holding a bonded licence does not lose the ability to carry them, and carrying them does not change their legal state.

Answer 1 invents an exclusivity the licence does not carry — the licence adds an authority; it does not remove the ordinary one. Answer 3 confuses the carrier's licence with a duty exemption for the cargo; the goods' duty position follows their procedure, not their truck. Answer 4 reverses the logic — the goods' state determines whether the bonded-carrier licence is needed, never the reverse.

The takeaway: the bonded carrier's licence is an authority over goods under procedure — transit and warehousing need it; free-circulation goods do not, and carrying them changes nothing.

=== ASSESSMENT Q7 | tests lesson 3.3 ===
QUESTION:
A vessel bound for Tema diverts to anchor overnight at a fishing town so the master can visit a relative ashore, then proceeds to the port the next morning. No cargo is moved during the stop. Which option best states the position under section 16(1)?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The diversion is lawful because no cargo was touched.
2. The diversion is lawful because the master's command includes routing.
3. The diversion breaches section 16(1): the conveyance must come to a port, or a place allowed by the Commissioner-General in special circumstances, without touching at any other place in the country — the obligation governs the touching, not the cargo's movement.
4. The diversion is lawful if the vessel reports the stop by radio.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 16(1) of Act 891 provides that a conveyance arriving in the country shall come to a port, or to any other place allowed by the Commissioner-General in special circumstances, without touching at any other place in the country, and shall proceed quickly to the proper place of mooring or unloading. The obligation is on the touching — the unapproved stop is a breach of the routing rule whether or not any cargo moved, because an uncontrolled touching is an uncontrolled stop inside the country's waters.

Answer 1 invents a cargo-only scope; the provision governs the conveyance's movement, not merely its cargo's. Answer 2 substitutes command discretion for the statutory routing — the master's operational authority runs inside the law's obligations, not over them. Answer 4 invents a reporting cure for a routing breach; the section's remedy structure does not include a radio-notice exception.

The takeaway: the arrival's routing is prescribed before any cargo question arises — port or allowed place, direct, prompt — and the touching itself is what the provision governs.

=== ASSESSMENT Q8 | tests lesson 3.3 ===
QUESTION:
The master's report on arrival lists 320 packages; the discharge list counts 320; the bills of lading total 318. Which option best describes the discrepancy and the professional response?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The two extra packages are a rounding difference; customs reconciles annually.
2. Two packages are on the report and discharge list with no bill of lading behind them — a discrepancy to resolve by establishing what they are and bringing them onto lawful paper (a declaration, an amendment, a stores entry), because the report, the bills and the discharge list are three views of the same goods that must agree.
3. The bills of lading should be amended to add two packages, without checking what the packages are.
4. The report is the only document customs reads; the bills are commercial papers customs ignores.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. The lesson's reconciliation discipline runs on the three views: the master's report under section 17 is the craft's statement of what arrived; the bills of lading evidence the commercial consignments; the discharge list counts what came ashore. Two packages the report and discharge account for but no bill covers are packages whose commercial custody the papers do not explain — the discrepancy is found by the papers, and the response is the lesson 2.3 discipline: establish the truth from the source documents, then correct the document that errs and bring the goods onto lawful paper.

Answer 1 normalises the gap; the border's reconciliation has no rounding tolerance for packages whose story is missing. Answer 3 amends documents before establishing the facts — the direction that converts a discrepancy into a false statement. Answer 4 discards the commercial papers the reconciliation exists to check.

The takeaway: a report-bill gap is unexplained goods in the perimeter — each answer must end with the goods on some lawful paper.

=== ASSESSMENT Q9 | tests lesson 3.3 ===
QUESTION:
At night, without a report and outside the supervised discharge, a vessel's crew unloads crates to a small boat that then heads up the coast. Which option best states the position under Act 891?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. No consequence follows, because the vessel's overall report was already made for the voyage.
2. No consequence follows for the vessel; only the boat's crew can be liable.
3. The crates are goods unlawfully unloaded and are liable to forfeiture under section 41 — the lawful sequence (arrival report, supervised discharge inside the controlled area under section 5(1)(a)) was bypassed — and the seizure and disposal machinery follows its own provisions.
4. The consequence is only a warning to the master, because the quantity is unknown.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 41 of Act 891 provides for the forfeiture of goods unlawfully loaded or unloaded — and the scenario is its paradigm: goods worked outside the arrival sequence's lawful path, outside section 21's supervised unloading, outside the controlled area's permitted activities, to a craft and destination no document accounts for. The liability attaches to the goods themselves; the seizure, claims and disposal machinery of Module 10 follows on its own provisions.

Answer 1 mistakes the report for a licence to unload outside the sequence — the report brings reported cargo into the lawful path; it does not authorise unreported work outside it. Answer 2 understates the exposure by pointing at the boat's crew; the goods' forfeiture and the vessel-side responsibilities run regardless. Answer 4 invents a de minimis warning — the unlawfulness is in the act, and the quantity is unknown precisely because the act was unlawful.

The takeaway: the supervised sequence is the goods' legal shield; cargo worked off the sequence is cargo in forfeiture's reach, and the papers will show the gap.

=== ASSESSMENT Q10 | tests lesson 3.4 ===
QUESTION:
A consignment has been examined with no offence found, and the required documents are acquired — but the goods' procedure requires an agency permit that has not yet been issued. The agent asks for release "on the strength of the first two conditions". Which option best states the position under section 6(1)?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Release may be granted: the first two conditions are the substantive ones.
2. Release may be granted if the agent promises the permit within a week.
3. Release may not be granted yet: section 6(1)'s conditions are conjunctive — examined or exempted, no offence found, required documents acquired, and the stated conditions met — and the outstanding permit is an unmet stated condition.
4. Release may not be granted because the goods must always be physically examined.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 6(1) of Act 891 states release's conditions conjunctively: the Commissioner-General may release declared goods as soon as they have been examined or exempted from examination, where an offence has not been found to have been committed, the required documents have been acquired, and the stated conditions are met. The scenario satisfies the first three — but the outstanding permit is a stated condition for the goods' procedure, and a conjunctive list releases on all its conditions, not on most of them.

Answer 1 drops the fourth condition — the provision's structure does not rank its conditions into substantive and formal. Answer 2 invents an undertaking mechanism the section does not provide; a condition is satisfied when it is satisfied. Answer 4 misstates the first condition, which expressly contemplates exemption from examination.

The takeaway: release is the whole checklist read together — the agent's skill is knowing which condition is unmet, and the client's expectation is set accordingly.

=== ASSESSMENT Q11 | tests lesson 3.4 ===
QUESTION:
An importer's goods were released two days ago but remain in the terminal yard while the delivery sequence runs, and storage charges accrue daily. The importer asks his agent to "get customs to stop the charges". Which option best advises the importer?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The agent should ask customs to waive the charges, since customs performed the release.
2. The agent should explain the two clocks: release ended the customs control, so the accruing storage is the terminal's commercial charge on its published tariff — the delivery sequence and the charges run on the terminal's procedures, and the client's remedy is to take delivery, not to seek a customs waiver.
3. The agent should refuse to pay the charges; post-release storage is unlawful.
4. The agent should re-lodge the declaration so the goods re-enter customs control and the charges pause.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. The lesson's release-versus-delivery distinction answers the scenario: release under section 6(1) is the customs decision ending legal control; the delivery sequence and the storage are the terminal's operational layer, charged on the port's published tariff. Two clocks run in a clearance — the statutory clock before release (the conditions, the fourteen-day rule, the uncleared-goods machinery) and the commercial clock after it (the tariff's free days and rates) — and this client is on the second clock, whose counterparty is the terminal.

Answer 1 directs the waiver request at an authority that neither sets the tariff nor owes the waiver. Answer 3 asserts an unlawfulness with no basis — post-release storage on a published rate is the commercial layer working normally. Answer 4 invents a re-lodgement mechanism that would achieve nothing except a second clearance.

The takeaway: name the clock before advising — before release the provisions answer; after release the tariff answers, and the fastest remedy is taking delivery.

=== ASSESSMENT Q12 | tests lesson 3.4 ===
QUESTION:
An agent tells a client whose goods were deposited in the State warehouse: "Deposit means the State has taken your goods permanently — there is nothing left to do." Which option best evaluates the advice?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The advice is correct: deposit under section 53 is the final disposal of the goods.
2. The advice is correct, because abandoned goods have no legal protection.
3. The advice is wrong on the law: section 53's deposit secures the goods and the duties that may be due — it is a stage in a procedure, with the auctioned-goods machinery (s.93) and the seizure, claims and disposal provisions (ss.124-126) following on their own terms; the client's position is to be advised on that machinery, not abandoned with the goods.
4. The advice is wrong because deposit applies only to seized goods, never to abandoned ones.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 53 of Act 891 provides the deposit of goods in the State warehouse, and section 151 defines the place as for the deposit of uncustomed, un-entered, un-examined, abandoned, detained or seized goods, for the security of the goods or the payment of duties that may be due. The definition's purpose clause — security — and the machinery that follows (section 93's auctioned goods; sections 124 to 126's seizure, claims and disposal) show the deposit is custody under a provision, not a conclusion about the goods' fate.

Answer 1 is the conflation the lesson warns against — deposit is a stage, and the machinery after it has its own procedure. Answer 2 is false twice: abandoned goods are expressly a deposit ground, which is precisely why their position is defined rather than lost. Answer 4 misreads the definition's list, which names abandonment as one of the six deposit grounds.

The takeaway: the failure path — uncleared, uncustomed, deposited — is a path of provisions, and correct advice names the stage and the machinery that follows it, because the client acts at the stage they are in.
