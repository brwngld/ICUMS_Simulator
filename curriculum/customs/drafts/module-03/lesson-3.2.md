=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Who may act in a customs transaction
BODY:
Every declaration comes from someone with a lawful identity, and section 151 fixes the four: the declarant is the person who makes a declaration for customs purposes — the owner of the goods, the importer, the exporter, or the agent. Those four are the lawful hands a declaration can come from, and each carries its own responsibility for the particulars stated.

The owner and the importer or exporter act for themselves. The agent acts for another — and "agent" is itself a defined term: a person performing customs business on behalf of another person, where "customs business" means an activity that has to do with the importation or exportation of goods. The definitions interlock: the agent's work is customs business, performed on behalf of a principal, and the licence that authorises it is the customs house agent's licence.

The practical reading: when a clearance is lodged, the file should show which of the four identities is acting — because the answer determines whose responsibility the particulars are, whose licence covers the work, and whose written authority must exist. A declaration from none of the four identities is a declaration from nobody the Act recognises.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The customs house agent's licence
BODY:
"Customs house agent" is defined in section 151: a person who is granted a licence to carry out customs business. Section 43 is the licensing provision — the Act's gate through which the profession enters — and it frames the licence as the authority to perform customs business for others.

The licence is what separates the profession from the ad hoc helper. An unlicensed person may be an employee of an importer; that person cannot carry out customs business on behalf of others as of right. The licence converts the work into a regulated profession: the licensed agent stands between the client and the border, lodges declarations in the client's name, and answers for the particulars to the same provisions that answer for the importer's own statements.

The GRA's training deck supplies the practice frame: the Authority, as required by law, trains and certifies persons to perform agency duties for and on behalf of persons who do business with the Customs Division. The licence is the end of that route — training, certification, and the grant of the licence to carry out customs business.

What the licence does not do is manufacture competence by itself: the agent's duties run from the Act's provisions, and the next block's authority requirement is the first of them.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Written authority: section 44's requirement
BODY:
Section 44 provides the agent's first duty: the agent must produce authority. The provision's principle is simple and absolute — a person performing customs business on behalf of another must be able to show, on demand, that the principal authorised the act.

The requirement is the profession's integrity instrument, and it works in three directions. For the principal: the written authority defines what the agent may do, so the agent's acts are the principal's instructions made visible. For customs: the authority document is what the officer checks to confirm the declaration's lodger is acting for a real principal — the officer's entitlement to demand it is the section's own words. For the agent: the authority is protection — an agent acting within written authority performs the principal's instructions, and an agent acting outside them acts at their own risk.

The professional discipline that follows: every client engagement starts with the authority document; every declaration lodged traces to it; and the authority is kept with the file, because the officer's demand can come at any point in the clearance. An agent who cannot produce authority has a problem that no competence can cure — the Act requires the paper, and the paper must exist.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The agent and the freight forwarder
BODY:
The trade uses two professions that overlap without being identical, and the learner must hold the line between them.

The customs house agent is the Act's creature: licensed to carry out customs business — activities that have to do with the importation or exportation of goods — and performing it on behalf of principals under section 44's written authority. The agent's work is at the border: declarations, examinations, releases, the clearance's legal acts.

The freight forwarder is the logistics profession's organiser: arranging the carriage, consolidating consignments, booking the vessels, coordinating the door-to-door movement. Forwarding is a real and substantial profession — the Logistics programme teaches it — but Act 891 does not define it, and the forwarding work of arranging carriage is not, by itself, customs business.

The overlap is real: many firms hold both capacities, and a forwarder's staff may include licensed agents. But the capacities stay distinct: arranging the shipment is forwarding; lodging the declaration is customs business, and it requires the licence and the written authority. A client needs to know which capacity a firm is acting in — because the duties, the liabilities and the licence requirements differ between them.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The bonded carrier: goods moving under control
BODY:
One more defined actor completes the border's professional cast. A "bonded carrier" is defined in section 151 as a carrier licensed in accordance with the Act to transport bonded goods within the country — and section 45 is its licensing provision.

The bonded carrier exists because of a control problem the transit and warehousing regimes create: goods under a duty suspension — transit movements, warehoused goods, goods moving between controlled places — must travel without leaving customs control, and the carrier that moves them must be lawfully able to do so. The licence is that ability. A bonded carrier's movement keeps the goods inside the procedure; an unlicensed carrier's movement of bonded goods is the procedure breaking.

The learner's reading: when a provision permits movement "by a bonded carrier" — as section 95(1)(a) does for transit — the defined term is doing real work. The movement's lawfulness depends on the carrier's licence, which is why the licence exists and why the provision names it.

The professional cast is now complete: declarants act, agents represent them under licence and authority, and bonded carriers move the goods that stay under control.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Certification: the route into the profession
BODY:
The GRA's training deck describes the route into the profession in one sentence: the Authority, as required by law, has set out to train and certify persons to perform agency duties for and on behalf of persons who do business with the Customs Division. The deck's purpose slides add what the trained agent is for: the laws give the framework to classify and value goods and impose duties, and the agent's work operates that framework for clients.

The sentence carries three professional facts. The route runs through training — the profession's competence is taught, not assumed. It runs through certification — the Authority certifies the persons it trains, which is how the trade's quality is policed. And it runs to agency duties performed for others — the licence's work is representational, on behalf of principals who do business with the Customs Division.

This programme is, in effect, that training's written spine: the mandate (Module 1), the documents (this module), the procedures (Module 4), the technical rules (Modules 7 to 9), and the professional practice this module closes with. The learner reading these lessons is walking the deck's route in text.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A CHA onboards a new importer
BODY:
Step 1. The engagement. A trader importing tyres approaches a licensed customs house agent. The agent's first professional act is not the declaration — it is the engagement paper: written authority under section 44, defining what the agent may do (clear this importer's consignments), for whom (the trader), within what scope (the tyres trade, at the stated ports).

Step 2. The licence check. The trader's officer asks whether the firm may act. The agent produces the licence: the grant to carry out customs business — the defined term from section 151 — held by the firm's licensed agents. The authority document and the licence are two different papers: the licence authorises the firm to do the work; the authority authorises this work for this client.

Step 3. The declaration. A tyre consignment arrives. The agent lodges the declaration as the declarant — the fourth lawful identity — acting under the written authority. The particulars are the agent's own statements now, checked against the invoice and packing list before lodgement.

Step 4. The demand. The proper officer asks to see the authority. The agent produces it from the file. The clearance proceeds — and had the paper been missing, the clearance would have stopped there, whatever the agent's competence.

What does this example demonstrate? The licence, the authority and the declarant identity are three separate papers with three separate roles — and the clearance needs all three in order.

Key lesson: the agent's first work is paper, not presence — licence, authority, then declaration.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A forwarder and an agent at a foreign gateway
BODY:
Step 1. The shipment. A Ghanaian exporter ships machinery to a buyer in Europe, and engages two firms: a freight forwarder to arrange the carriage from the factory gate to the buyer's warehouse, and a licensed agent to lodge the export declaration at Tema.

Step 2. The split. The forwarder books the vessel, consolidates the cargo, and coordinates the delivery to the port — the logistics of the movement. The agent performs the customs business: the export declaration, the supporting documents, the clearance acts. Section 151's definition of customs business marks the line — the agent's work is the activity that has to do with importation or exportation, as the Act frames it.

Step 3. The overlap in practice. At the destination, the buyer's clearing agent performs the import clearance, and the forwarder arranges the inland delivery from the port to the warehouse. The two capacities alternate along the chain — forwarder, agent, forwarder, agent — each doing the work its licence and role cover.

Step 4. The client's discipline. The exporter's file records which firm acted in which capacity, because the liability for a misdeclaration sits with the declarant — the agent's capacity — and the liability for a missed shipment sits with the carriage contract — the forwarder's capacity.

What does this example demonstrate? Forwarding and customs business alternate along an international chain, and the file that names the capacity of each act is the file that assigns liability correctly.

Key lesson: arrange the movement with a forwarder; declare the movement with a licensed agent — and keep the two roles labelled in the file.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: The agent's signature is a statement
BODY:
When a licensed agent lodges a declaration, the particulars are the agent's own statements — section 151's false-statement definition reaches statements made with respect to goods subject to customs control, whoever made them, and the agent who signs the declaration has made statements about the goods. The practical consequence is the profession's discipline: the agent verifies the particulars against the supporting file before lodgement, because the agent's defence is not "the client told me" but "the file supported it". The written authority under section 44 authorises the agent to act; it does not transfer the responsibility for the statements away from the agent. An agent who lodges unverified particulars holds the risk of the false-statement provisions personally — which is why the verification habit of lesson 2.2 is the profession's habit too.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Forwarders are real, and undefined
BODY:
The freight-forwarding profession is real, substantial, and — deliberately in this lesson — described generically. Act 891 does not define "freight forwarder"; the definition the Act gives is for the agent performing customs business, and the forwarder's arranging work is not, by itself, customs business. The generic description is not a slight: it is the accuracy the sources support. When the institutions fact sheet is written, the forwarders' regulatory detail — associations, licensing if any, the relationship to the CHA licence — will come from it with citations. Until then, the learner's discipline holds: the licensed agent is the Act's defined profession; the forwarder is the trade's organising profession; the capacities overlap in firms but stay distinct in law, and the file labels which capacity acted.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
An unlicensed office assistant in an importer's firm lodges a declaration on the firm's behalf, without any agent's involvement. Which option best states the position under Act 891?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The lodgement is valid, because the firm is the importer and an employee may act for it.
2. The lodgement is valid only if the assistant holds a driving licence.
3. The lodgement is outside the lawful identities: the declarant must be the owner, the importer, the exporter, or the agent — and "agent" means a person performing customs business on behalf of another, licensed as a customs house agent; the assistant holds neither identity the Act recognises for lodging on behalf of another.
4. The lodgement is valid if the assistant later obtains a licence.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 151 of Act 891 defines the declarant as the person who makes a declaration for customs purposes — the owner of the goods, the importer, the exporter, or the agent — and defines an agent as a person performing customs business on behalf of another person, with the customs house agent as the person licensed to carry out that customs business. The firm's assistant is none of the four: the assistant is not the owner, importer or exporter in their own name, and lodging for another as an agent is customs business requiring the licence.

Answer 1 treats employment as authority to perform customs business — the licence exists precisely because employment is not that. Answer 2 is absurd on its face. Answer 4 confuses a future licence with a present act; the lodgement's legality is judged when it is made.

The takeaway: the declarant's four identities are exhaustive — act in your own name as owner, importer or exporter, or act for another under the licence and the written authority. There is no fifth door.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
A proper officer asks a licensed agent to produce the written authority for a declaration the agent lodged for a client. The agent refuses, saying the licence itself is proof enough. Which option best evaluates the refusal?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The refusal is correct: the licence is the authority, and producing it satisfies section 44.
2. The refusal fails: the licence authorises the agent to carry out customs business generally, while section 44 requires the agent to produce the authority for the specific act — the principal's written instruction for this clearance — and the officer's demand is exactly what the section provides for.
3. The refusal fails only if the client complains in writing first.
4. The refusal is correct, because agent-client papers are confidential and cannot be shown to customs.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 44 of Act 891 provides that the agent produces authority — the principal's authorisation for the act performed on the principal's behalf. The licence and the authority are two different papers with two different jobs, as the lesson separates them: the licence (section 43; the section 151 definition of customs house agent) authorises the profession; the authority authorises the particular engagement. An officer's demand under section 44 is answered with the engagement paper, not with the licence.

Answer 1 merges the two papers into one — the exact confusion the section is written to prevent, because a licence says nothing about whether this client instructed this act. Answer 3 invents a complaint precondition; the officer's demand is the trigger. Answer 4 misstates confidentiality: the authority document is precisely the paper the Act makes producible.

The takeaway: licence for the profession, authority for the engagement — the agent carries both, and section 44's demand is answered with the second.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A firm describes itself as "handling everything for your import — shipping, warehousing, delivery, and the customs declaration". Which option best states how the Act's categories apply to the firm's activities?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. All the activities are customs business, because they all relate to the import.
2. None of the activities is customs business, because the firm is a private company.
3. The movement and logistics work is forwarding — arranging carriage and delivery, which Act 891 does not define — while the customs declaration is customs business under section 151's definition, and lodging it for others requires the customs house agent's licence and the section 44 written authority; the capacities overlap in firms but stay distinct in law.
4. The declaration is customs business only if the firm also owns the goods.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 151 of Act 891 defines customs business as an activity that has to do with the importation or exportation of goods, and the customs house agent as the person licensed to carry out that business. The firm's shipping, warehousing and delivery work is the forwarding profession's organising work — real and substantial, but not defined by the Act and not, by itself, customs business. The declaration, lodged for others, is customs business in the Act's sense: it requires the licence and, under section 44, the principal's written authority.

Answer 1 swallows the forwarding work into customs business because of proximity to the import — the definitions distinguish the acts, not the project. Answer 2 makes the opposite error, declaring the declaration outside the definition by pointing at the firm's corporate form. Answer 4 invents an ownership test that appears nowhere.

The takeaway: label the capacity for each act — forwarding arranges the movement; customs business declares it — and the licence and authority follow the act that needs them.
