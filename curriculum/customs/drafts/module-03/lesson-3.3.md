=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Arrival: the obligations of the conveyance
BODY:
The cargo story at a border begins with the craft's own legal obligations, and section 16(1) states them. A conveyance arriving in the country shall come to a port, or to any other place allowed by the Commissioner-General in special circumstances, without touching at any other place in the country; on arriving at that port or place, it shall proceed quickly to the proper place of mooring or unloading as the nature of the port or place will admit, again without touching at any other place.

Three obligations, each with a point. The destination is prescribed: a port, or a Commissioner-General-allowed place — the craft does not choose its entry point by convenience. The routing is direct: no touching at intermediate places, because every touch is an uncontrolled stop where goods can leave the craft unseen. The movement inward is prompt: the craft proceeds quickly to the mooring or unloading place, because a vessel loitering in the roads is a vessel whose cargo is outside the perimeter's full control.

Section 151's definition of arrival — the point in time when a conveyance crosses the borders into the country — started the clock; section 16's obligations are the clock's first rules. Everything the cargo does next happens inside them.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The report on arrival
BODY:
The craft does not just arrive — it declares what it has brought. Section 17 provides the report to be made on arrival of the conveyance: the master's statement of the craft and its cargo, the border's first written account of what has entered. Section 18 supplies the consequence side — the failure to make the report, or to account for packages reported, is itself dealt with by the Act.

The report is the manifest chain's legal starting point. Everything downstream reconciles to it: the discharge list counts what was unloaded against what was reported; the declarations claim goods that the report brought in; the transport documents' counts must match the report's counts. The report is also the first document the officer reads when a question arises, because it is the craft's own statement — made by the person the Act holds responsible — of what the border is about to receive.

Lesson 2.3 introduced the report as the master's paper; this lesson places it in the arrival sequence. The learner's discipline is unchanged: the report, the bills of lading and the packing lists are three views of the same goods, and they must agree.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Breaking bulk and unloading, under supervision
BODY:
The physical act of taking cargo off the craft is where the border first touches the goods, and the Act surrounds it with provisions. Section 20 governs the breaking of bulk — the opening of a craft's holds and the commencement of discharge. Section 21 provides for mooring and unloading: where the vessel ties up, and how the goods come ashore, under the supervision the provisions establish.

Why the supervision matters: unloading is the moment the goods pass from the craft's custody into the port's custody — inside the customs-controlled area, where section 5(1)(a)'s permitted loading and unloading happens. A discharge that happens outside the supervision is not a procedural footnote; it is goods leaving one custody without entering the other, which is why section 41's forfeiture provision waits for goods unlawfully loaded or unloaded.

The learner's operational picture: the quay is a controlled interface. The bulk is broken lawfully, the cargo comes ashore lawfully, the counts reconcile, and the goods enter the temporary storage of section 5(1)(b) — undeclared goods included — until their declarations claim them.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The departure-side papers
BODY:
The cargo's paper duties do not end at the import side; the departing craft carries its own set, and the provisions mirror the arrival's.

Section 39 requires the master to deliver an account of cargo before departure: the outbound craft states what it is carrying out of the country, so the border can reconcile the goods leaving against the goods that were entered for export or in transit. Section 40 provides the transire — the document the master delivers on arrival for coastwise movements, the coastal trade's own paper.

The reconciliation discipline is the same as the arrival's, run in reverse: the account of cargo must match the export declarations and the transit documents for everything leaving on the craft. A container on the account with no export declaration behind it is an unexplained outbound movement; an export declaration for goods that never boarded is an entry with no movement. Both are discrepancies the papers themselves reveal — which is why the account exists.

The learner's habit holds in both directions: report, bills, account — three views of the same goods, and they must agree.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Unlawful loading and unloading: the forfeiture provision
BODY:
The arrival sequence's provisions are not advisory, and section 41 is where their teeth are: goods unlawfully loaded or unloaded are liable to forfeiture. The provision's reach covers cargo worked outside the lawful sequence — unloaded before the report, loaded without the account, moved at a place that is not a port or an allowed place, discharged outside the supervision the Act establishes.

The word to read carefully is liable: as with the fourteen-day rule, the provision creates exposure to forfeiture, and the machinery that follows — seizure, claims, disposal — runs on its own provisions, which Module 10 teaches. What section 41 adds to this lesson is the rule of the sequence: the arrival obligations, the report, the supervised discharge and the departure account are the lawful path, and cargo worked off the path is cargo in jeopardy.

The learner's operational takeaway is simple: the lawful sequence is also the safe sequence. A discharge performed inside the provisions produces reconciling papers; a discharge performed outside them produces goods the Act can take.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Terminal handling: the operational layer
BODY:
Once the cargo is lawfully ashore, the terminal's operational layer takes over: the container is received from the quay, stacked in the yard's blocks, tracked through the terminal's system, and delivered against the release. The port operators deck describes this layer — the seaport as the link where the cargo changes custody from the vessel to the shore operation — and the learner should hold it as operations, not law.

The distinction matters because the terminal's handling is governed by the port's own terms: stacking plans, dwell-time rules, delivery sequences. The customs layer governs the legal state of the goods — declared or undeclared, released or held — while the terminal layer governs their physical position. The two layers meet at the handover: the delivery order against the cleared declaration is the moment the operational layer hands the goods back to the trade.

The learner's diagnostic from lesson 3.1 applies here: a stuck container's cause is either a legal thread (undeclared, held, not released — customs) or an operational one (yard position, gate pass, tariff unpaid — terminal). The provisions of this lesson govern the first; the deck's operations govern the second; the professional names which one before proposing a remedy.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: An arrival day at Tema, step by step
BODY:
Step 1. The approach. A container vessel crosses into Ghanaian waters bound for the Port of Tema — arrival under section 151's definition — and proceeds directly to the port: section 16(1)'s obligation, no touching at any other place.

Step 2. The report. The master delivers the report under section 17: the craft, the voyage, and the cargo — 900 containers, each with its bill of lading. The report becomes the manifest chain's starting count.

Step 3. The discharge. The vessel moors at the terminal — section 21's mooring and unloading — the bulk is broken under section 20's provisions, and the quay cranes lift the containers into the customs-controlled area. Section 5(1)(a) permits the unloading; section 5(1)(b) receives the containers into temporary storage, declared and undeclared alike.

Step 4. The reconciliation. The terminal's discharge list is read against the master's report and the bills of lading: 900 discharged, 900 reported, 900 on the bills. The counts agree — the craft's paper trail reconciles, and the goods sit lawfully in the yard awaiting their declarations.

Step 5. The contrast. Had a container been discharged at a beach outside the port, outside the report and the supervision, section 41's forfeiture provision would name it: goods unlawfully unloaded, in jeopardy from the moment they touched the sand.

What does this example demonstrate? The arrival sequence is a chain of lawful acts — arrive, report, discharge, store — and each act produces the paper the next act reconciles against.

Key lesson: the lawful path and the reconciling path are the same path; walk one and you have the other.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: An unexplained container found at a foreign port
BODY:
Step 1. The discovery. At a European port, a routine reconciliation of a departing vessel's account of cargo against its loaded containers finds one container on the account that no export declaration covers — and one export declaration whose container is not on board.

Step 2. The read. The officers treat the pair as one question: what happened to the container that was declared for export but never loaded — and what is the container that was loaded with no declaration? The account and the declarations disagree, and the discrepancy is found by the papers, not by a search.

Step 3. The resolution. The carrier's records show the declared container was rolled to a later sailing; the loaded container belongs to a consignment whose declaration was lodged at the previous port. The papers are corrected, the movements are documented, and the departure proceeds with a reconciled account.

Step 4. The transfer. The scenario is not Ghanaian, and it teaches by parallel: section 39's account of cargo and the export declarations must reconcile in Ghana the same way. The papers that disagree are the papers that need correcting before the craft sails.

What does this example demonstrate? The departure-side reconciliation works internationally on the same logic: the account, the declarations and the physical load must tell one story.

Key lesson: a container without a declaration, or a declaration without a container, is a question the border will ask — answer it with the papers before the craft sails.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: The three views must tell one story
BODY:
The master's report on arrival, the bills of lading, and the account of cargo before departure are three views of the same goods at three moments — and the system's reliability is that they tell one story. The report is the craft's account of what came in; the bills evidence the commercial consignments; the departure account states what leaves. Where the three agree, the cargo's custody chain is proven by paper. Where they disagree, the disagreement is a finding — an unexplained container, an undeclared movement, goods that left without papers — and the correction must establish the facts before any document is touched. The discipline from lesson 2.3 applies at the border's edge: find the truth, correct the paper, never align the paper to convenience.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Coastwise trade is its own regime
BODY:
Sections 30 to 36 of Act 891 govern coastwise aircraft and ships — vessels moving goods between places within the country, the coastal trade. This lesson's arrival sequence is the international movement's; the coastwise regime has its own entry outward and clearance (s.30), permission requirements (s.32), name-display rules (s.33) and deviation rules (s.34). The learner's boundary discipline: do not blend the two. A vessel in the coastwise trade is not an arriving international conveyance, and its papers are the coastwise papers. When a client's movement is domestic-by-sea, the provisions that answer are ss.30–36; when it crosses the border, this lesson's provisions answer. Name the movement's kind first — international arrival, export, transit, or coastwise — and the right set of provisions follows.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A vessel's captain, running behind schedule, stops overnight at an unapproved beach town to take on provisions before proceeding to Tema, where the cargo is to be discharged. Which option best states the position under section 16(1)?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The stop is lawful, because provisions are not cargo and the vessel may stop anywhere for supplies.
2. The stop breaches section 16(1): the conveyance must come to a port, or a place allowed by the Commissioner-General in special circumstances, without touching at any other place in the country.
3. The stop is lawful provided the vessel did not discharge any cargo at the beach.
4. The stop is lawful because the master's discretion covers routing decisions entirely.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 16(1) of Act 891 provides that a conveyance arriving in the country shall come to a port, or to any other place allowed by the Commissioner-General in special circumstances, without touching at any other place in the country, and shall proceed quickly to the proper place of mooring or unloading. The provision governs the craft's routing from the border crossing onward — and an unapproved overnight touch is a touching at another place.

Answer 1 invents a supplies exception the section does not carry; the craft's own needs are what the stores regime and the proper officer's permissions handle, not an unapproved routing. Answer 3 narrows the breach to discharge — the provision's wording covers the touching itself, before any question of cargo moves. Answer 4 mistakes commercial discretion for the statutory routing the border requires; the direct-arrival rule is what makes the report and the discharge reconcilable.

The takeaway: the arrival's routing is prescribed — port or allowed place, direct, prompt — and every deviation is a provision engaged, whatever the vessel's motive.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
The master's report on arrival states 640 packages discharged, while the bills of lading for the same vessel total 632. Which option best describes the professional response?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The difference is a clerical rounding and can stand.
2. The extra 8 packages reported but unaccounted by any bill are a discrepancy to resolve: establish what the 8 are from the craft's papers and the terminal's discharge list, correct the document that is wrong, and account for the goods — because the report, the bills and the discharge list are three views of the same goods that must agree.
3. The bills of lading should be amended to add 8 packages immediately, without checking.
4. The report controls and the bills are irrelevant to customs.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. The lesson's reconciliation discipline runs on the three views: the master's report under section 17 is the craft's statement of what arrived; the bills of lading evidence the consignments; the discharge list counts what came ashore. Eight packages on the report with no bill behind them are packages whose custody the papers do not account for — goods the border received without a commercial story. The response is the lesson 2.3 discipline applied at the quay: find the truth (what are the eight, whose are they, is the report or a bill wrong), correct the document that errs, and bring the goods onto some lawful paper — a declaration, an amendment, a stores entry — so the views tell one story.

Answer 1 normalises an unexplained gap between the craft's own statement and the commercial papers. Answer 3 amends documents before establishing the facts — the false-statement direction. Answer 4 discards the commercial papers the reconciliation needs.

The takeaway: a report-bill gap is eight questions, not a rounding — and each answer must end on some lawful paper.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A vessel's crew, without the proper officer's supervision or any report, unloads twelve cartons at night onto a private boat moored alongside. Which option best states the consequence under Act 891?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. None, because the cartons were small and the quantity trivial.
2. None, because the vessel had already made its arrival report for the rest of the cargo.
3. The cartons are goods unlawfully unloaded and are liable to forfeiture under section 41 — the lawful sequence (report, supervised discharge inside the controlled area) was bypassed, and the forfeiture machinery of seizure, claims and disposal follows its own provisions.
4. Only the master is liable personally, and the goods themselves are unaffected.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 41 of Act 891 provides for the forfeiture of goods unlawfully loaded or unloaded — and the scenario is the provision's paradigm: cargo worked outside the lawful sequence, outside the supervised unloading of section 21, outside the customs-controlled area's permitted activities of section 5(1)(a), at a place and in a manner the arrival provisions do not authorise. The liability follows the goods.

Answer 1 invents a de minimis escape the provision does not contain; the unlawfulness is in the act, not the quantity. Answer 2 mistakes the report for a licence — the report brings the reported cargo into the lawful sequence; it does not authorise unreported unloading outside it. Answer 3 names the correct provision and its consequence. Answer 4 understates the exposure: forfeiture acts on the goods, and the persons responsible face the offence machinery separately.

The takeaway: the lawful sequence is the goods' protection — cargo worked off the sequence is cargo in forfeiture's reach, whatever the quantity.
