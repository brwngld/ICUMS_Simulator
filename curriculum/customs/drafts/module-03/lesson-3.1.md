=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The four actors at a seaport clearance
BODY:
A seaport clearance involves four kinds of actor, each holding a different legal position, and the clearance stalls the moment a learner cannot tell them apart.

Customs — the Customs Division of the Ghana Revenue Authority — is the regulator: it controls the goods under Act 891, decides examination and release, and its decisions are statutory acts.

The port authority owns and manages the harbour infrastructure: the berths, the quays, the common facilities. In Ghana the training deck names the Ghana Ports and Harbours Authority, with the Port of Tema and the Port of Takoradi as the country's gateways.

Terminal operators run the working yards inside the port: they receive containers from the vessels, stack them, and hand them over against cleared declarations — an operational service performed inside the customs-controlled perimeter.

Shipping lines carry the goods: their vessels bring the cargo in, their masters report it, and their equipment (the containers) often stays in the trade for years.

One clearance, four capacities — regulator, landlord, handler, carrier — and every document the learner reads names one of them acting in one of those capacities.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The place definitions tell the actors apart
BODY:
Lesson 1.3 taught the place definitions; this lesson uses them to sort the actors' powers, because at a working port the definitions are the map of who may do what.

The port is designated by the Minister, by notice in the Gazette and two national newspapers — the political act that brings a harbour into the customs system. Within it, the approved places of loading and unloading are the quays and jetties where cargo may lawfully be worked. The customs-controlled areas inside the port — designated by the Commissioner-General or licensed under section 46 — are the perimeter where the Authority has control over goods, persons and conveyances, and where section 5(1)'s permitted activities happen: loading, unloading, inspection, assessment, clearing; temporary storage of unreleased goods; embarkation and disembarkation; storage and processing under bond.

Read against the actors: the port authority's infrastructure hosts the designated places; the terminal operator works inside the customs-controlled area; customs controls what happens there; the shipping line's vessel is the conveyance that arrives. The definitions assign the legal positions — the actors simply occupy them.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Ghana's seaports, as the deck presents them
BODY:
The port operators deck gives the national picture in three verified strokes, and the learner should carry them with their source.

Seaports are a vital link in the global supply chain, handling up to 90 percent of world freight. Ghana's seaports handle over 80 percent of the country's international trade, serving as economic gateways for domestic commerce and for landlocked West African neighbours whose cargo transits through Ghana. The gateways the deck names are the Port of Tema and the Port of Takoradi.

The shares matter to the learner's sense of scale: the border this programme teaches is, for most goods, a quay. When a later module discusses transit corridors, the landlocked-neighbour point explains why — much of what clears Tema is not staying in Ghana, and the transit procedures of Module 11 exist for it.

What the deck's picture does not do is name private terminal operators or attribute operational claims to them — and this lesson follows that boundary deliberately, as the caution below records. The institutions fact sheet, when written, will carry the named-actor detail the decks do not provide.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Regulatory decisions versus operational services
BODY:
The most useful distinction at a working port is between a regulatory decision and an operational service — and the learner must never conflate them.

A regulatory decision is a statutory act by customs: to examine or exempt from examination, to release under section 6(1), to seize, to demand documents. It is governed by Act 891's provisions, it is made by the officer whose competence it is, and it can be objected to through the dispute machinery.

An operational service is a commercial act by the port or terminal: receiving a container from the vessel, stacking it, issuing the gate pass, handing it over. These are governed by the port's own terms, tariffs and procedures — commercial law, not Act 891.

The distinction resolves the common confusions. A delayed release is customs: its remedy is the clearance process, not the terminal. A delayed gate pass is the terminal: its remedy is the port's procedures. A container can be released by customs and still sit in the yard — released is a legal state, delivered is an operational one — and lesson 3.4 builds on exactly that gap.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: How the actors interlock in one clearance
BODY:
One container's clearance passes through all four actors in a fixed interlock, and each handover is a document moment.

The shipping line's vessel arrives and the master reports (section 17); the line's manifest names the container. The terminal operator lifts it from the vessel into the yard — inside the customs-controlled area, under section 5(1)'s permitted storage. The importer's agent lodges the declaration with the supporting file; customs examines or exempts from examination, and releases under section 6(1). The terminal issues the delivery order against the cleared declaration; the port authority's infrastructure gates the container out.

Four actors, four capacities, one chain: the line carried, the port hosted, the terminal handled, customs controlled. The chain's handovers are where hold-ups live — and the learner who can name which actor owns each handover can diagnose any stalled clearance: a documentation problem is customs's thread, a yard problem is the terminal's, a vessel problem is the line's.

That diagnostic habit — name the actor, name the capacity, then name the provision or the tariff — is the professional skill this lesson exists to build.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Naming actors: the discipline of the sources
BODY:
This lesson names two institutions on the deck's authority — the Ghana Ports and Harbours Authority as the port authority, and the Port of Tema and the Port of Takoradi as the gateways — and it names nothing else, for a reason the programme makes explicit.

Institutional facts are anchor facts: who operates or regulates what comes only from an anchor, a fact sheet, or a verified authoritative source (spec C4.5). The institutions fact sheet that would carry the named detail — which terminal operators run which yards, which body licenses the agents, which agency controls which goods — is not yet written, so this lesson keeps every private actor generic: "the terminal operator", "the shipping line", "the carrier".

The discipline protects the learner twice. It prevents false claims — no operational statement is attached to a named private company that the sources do not support. And it teaches the verification habit: when the learner needs a named actor's role in practice, the answer comes from the fact sheet or an authoritative source, cited — never from memory or from the yard's rumour. Generic until sourced is the professional standard.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: One container's journey through Tema's four actors
BODY:
Step 1. The line. A shipping line's vessel berths at Tema carrying a container of automotive spare parts. The master reports the arrival under section 17; the line's manifest lists the container among the cargo.

Step 2. The port and the terminal. The Ghana Ports and Harbours Authority's harbour hosts the berth; the terminal operator lifts the container into the customs-controlled area and stacks it. Under section 5(1)(b), the temporary storage of goods not yet released — including undeclared goods — is a permitted activity, so the container's wait in the yard is lawful storage inside the perimeter.

Step 3. The regulator. The importer's agent lodges the declaration with the supporting file. The proper officer reads the file, and the risk system selects the container for a document check only. No offence is found; the required documents are acquired; the Commissioner-General releases the goods under section 6(1).

Step 4. The handover. The terminal issues the delivery order against the cleared declaration; the port's gate passes the container out. The operator's tariff charges the storage days; customs charges nothing for the release itself.

Step 5. The diagnostic. A week later the agent's colleague asks why a different container is stuck. The answer names the actor: its declaration failed the document check — a customs thread, not a yard thread.

What does this example demonstrate? The four actors' capacities are distinguishable at every handover, and each handover has its own paper.

Key lesson: name the actor and the capacity first; the remedy follows the capacity, not the frustration.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: The same chain at a foreign gateway
BODY:
Step 1. The scene. At a major European gateway — the port complex that serves a continent's hinterland — the same four capacities exist: the state customs administration regulates; the port authority manages the harbours; private terminal operators run the yards under concession; the shipping lines call with their vessels.

Step 2. The interlock. A container is discharged, stacked in the terminal's yard, declared electronically by the importer's representative, selected for scanning by the customs administration, released, and gated out. Every handover matches Tema's chain: the master's report, the yard's storage inside the controlled perimeter, the declaration's particulars, the release decision, the delivery order. The actors' names change; the handover sequence does not.

Step 3. What differs. The names differ, the tariffs differ, and the electronic systems differ. The legal architecture — a designated port, a controlled perimeter, a release decision, an operational handover — does not.

Step 4. The lesson transferred. A learner who can diagnose a stuck container at Tema by actor and capacity can diagnose one at any gateway, because the capacities are universal even where the institutions are local.

What does this example demonstrate? The four-actor interlock is the international shape of a seaport clearance; only the institutions and tariffs are local.

Key lesson: learn the capacities, and every port's clearance reads in your own language.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Name only what the sources name
BODY:
The discipline this lesson practised is worth stating as a rule: name only what the sources name. The deck named the port authority and the two ports, so this lesson named them; the deck did not name private terminal operators or shipping lines serving the trade, so this lesson described them generically — "the terminal operator", "the carrier". The rule exists because institutional errors are the errors that travel: a wrong operator name repeated in a client memo is a claim the professional must retract later, and a market-share figure quoted from memory is a figure no tariff page can verify. When the institutions fact sheet is written and verified, the named detail will come from it, with citations; until then, generic descriptions are not vagueness — they are accuracy at exactly the level the sources support, which is the only level a professional writes at.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Port charges and customs charges are different ledgers
BODY:
A container's stay at the port generates two kinds of charge, and the learner must keep the ledgers separate. The customs charges — duty, import VAT, levies — are statutory, imposed by Act 891 and the tariff, collected by the Authority, and each has its legal base. The port charges — storage, handling, garden dues — are commercial, set by the port authority's and terminal's published tariffs, and governed by contract and commercial law, not by the customs statute. Confusing the ledgers produces nonsense advice: customs does not set the storage rate, and the terminal does not owe the import duty. The only overlap is procedural — the cleared declaration is what lets the container out, and the terminal's release of the goods follows it. Two ledgers, two authorities, one gate.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A container has been sitting in the terminal yard for nine days. The importer's agent wants it moved for examination, the terminal says the yard's stacking plan controls movement timing, and the importer demands "customs order it out now". Which option best analyses the position?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The importer is right: customs controls the goods, so customs controls the yard's stacking plan.
2. The terminal is right about everything, because the goods are on the terminal's land.
3. Both threads are real and different: the terminal's stacking and movement procedures are its operational responsibility, while any examination decision is customs's regulatory one — the importer's demand must follow the thread that owns the delay.
4. The importer has no remedy at all until the goods are released.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. The lesson's core distinction is regulatory decision versus operational service. Customs's control of the goods under Act 891 makes examination, release and related decisions statutory acts; the terminal's stacking plan, yard procedures and movement timing are operational services governed by the port's own terms. A container inside a customs-controlled area is subject to both — the Authority's control over the goods, and the operator's management of its yard — and the two threads answer different questions.

Answer 1 collapses the threads: customs's control of the goods does not make customs the manager of the yard's physical operations. Answer 2 makes the opposite collapse — the land does not exempt the goods from the Authority's control under section 4 and section 5(1)(b)'s permitted storage. Answer 4 is false: the importer's remedies run through both threads, from the clearance process to the port's procedures.

The takeaway: diagnose the delay by naming the thread — the regulatory delay is answered by the provisions, the operational delay by the port's procedures, and neither answers for the other.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
Which of the following is a regulatory decision rather than an operational service?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The terminal issuing a delivery order against a cleared declaration.
2. The port authority publishing its storage tariff for the year.
3. The proper officer exempting a consignment from examination and the Commissioner-General releasing it under section 6(1).
4. The shipping line nominating the vessel for the next voyage.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. The lesson's distinction: a regulatory decision is a statutory act by customs, governed by Act 891's provisions and performed by the officer whose competence it is. Release under section 6(1) — the Commissioner-General may release declared goods as soon as they have been examined or exempted from examination, where no offence is found and the required documents are acquired — is the paradigm: a statutory act, with conditions, performed by a named authority.

Answer 1 is an operational handover — the terminal's service against the cleared declaration. Answer 2 is commercial: the port authority's tariff is its own published terms, not a customs provision. Answer 4 is a shipping-line commercial decision. All three are real acts by real actors — but they are not statutory customs decisions, and the learner must be able to say so.

The takeaway: if the act's authority comes from a provision of Act 891 and is performed by the officer it names, it is regulatory; if it comes from a tariff or a contract, it is operational — and the remedies differ accordingly.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A learner states in a client memo: "The terminal operator at Tema is XYZ Company, and it processes 60 percent of the country's containers." No source in the programme's materials supports the claim. Which option best evaluates the statement?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The statement is acceptable professional shorthand, because everyone in the trade knows the operator.
2. The statement is acceptable if the learner believes it to be true.
3. The statement violates the anchoring discipline: institutional facts come only from an anchor, a fact sheet, or a verified authoritative source (spec C4.5); the sources here name the port authority and the two ports, not a private operator or a market share — so the memo should describe the actor generically or verify and cite the claim.
4. The statement is acceptable because market shares are public knowledge that needs no citation.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. The writing spec's rule C4.5 governs institutional facts — who operates or regulates what — and requires them to come from an anchor, a fact sheet, or a verified authoritative source, with the same rule as the Logistics spec's institutional clause. The programme's sources name the Ghana Ports and Harbours Authority and the Ports of Tema and Takoradi; they do not name private terminal operators or market shares, so the memo asserts what nothing in the sources supports.

Answer 1 substitutes trade familiarity for sourcing — the exact habit the rule exists to break, because familiarity is where unverified claims originate. Answer 2 makes belief the standard, which is the opposite of verification. Answer 4 asserts that public knowledge needs no citation, which is false in professional writing: the more "obvious" a claim, the less anyone checks it.

The takeaway: generic until sourced — describe the actor without the name, or verify the name and cite it. Both are professional; neither substitutes for the other.
