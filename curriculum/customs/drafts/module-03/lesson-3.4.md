=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Release: the four conditions
BODY:
Release is the clearance goal, and section 6(1) states its conditions precisely: the Commissioner-General may release declared goods as soon as the goods have been examined or exempted from examination, where an offence has not been found to have been committed under the Act, the required documents have been acquired, and the stated conditions are met.

Four conditions, each doing work. Examined or exempted: the control question is answered — the goods were looked at, or the risk system lawfully said they need not be. No offence found: the control did not surface a violation. Required documents acquired: the supporting file of section 14 is complete — the permits, certificates and papers the goods demand. Stated conditions met: whatever further conditions attach to the goods' procedure are satisfied.

Release is therefore a legal conclusion built from the whole clearance: the risk decision, the control result, the file's completeness, the procedure's conditions. The learner reads section 6(1) as a checklist that every clearance must pass before the goods leave — and the checklist's order matters, because release comes after examination, not before it.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Release is a decision; delivery is a handover
BODY:
The most practically useful distinction in this lesson is between release and delivery, because they are different events owned by different actors.

Release is the customs decision: the Commissioner-General's act under section 6(1) that ends the goods' detention and permits them to leave customs control. It is made when the four conditions are met.

Delivery is the physical handover: the terminal's release of the container against the cleared declaration, the gate pass, the truck's exit from the yard. It is an operational act, governed by the port's procedures and tariffs.

The gap between them is where clients get confused: goods can be released and still sit in the yard for days — storage accruing, delivery sequence pending — because release ended the legal control without moving the box. The agent's advice names which event the client is waiting for: a release delay is a customs thread (the conditions of section 6(1)); a delivery delay is an operational thread (the terminal's procedures). Same container, two different questions, two different remedies.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Uncleared goods: when the lifecycle stalls
BODY:
Not every consignment gets declared and released. Goods that sit without clearance pass into a defined legal state, and section 52 provides for them: the uncleared goods provision — the Commissioner-General's machinery for goods nobody has cleared within the lawful time.

The state the goods enter is named in section 151: "uncustomed goods" means goods liable to duty on which the full duties due have not been paid, and goods, whether liable to duty or not, that are imported or exported or in any way dealt with contrary to the provisions of the Act in relation to customs. The definition has two limbs — the unpaid-duty limb and the non-compliance limb — and both matter: goods that were never declared can be uncustomed even if no duty was ever assessed on them, because the dealing with them was contrary to the customs provisions.

Why the definition matters at the port: the uncustomed state is the gateway state for the Act's storage machinery — the State warehouse provision of section 53 takes uncustomed goods in, along with the un-entered, un-examined, abandoned, detained and seized. The learner reads section 52 as the stall, and the State warehouse as where the stall lands.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The State warehouse: where stalled goods go
BODY:
Section 53 provides the deposit of goods in the State warehouse, and section 151 defines the place: a place approved by the Commissioner-General for the deposit of uncustomed, un-entered, un-examined, abandoned, detained or seized goods, for the security of the goods or the payment of duties that may be due.

The definition's list is the lesson's map — six ways goods come into the State's keeping: uncustomed (duty unpaid or dealing non-compliant), un-entered (never declared), un-examined, abandoned (the owner walked away), detained (held under a power), and seized (taken under the enforcement machinery). For each, the purpose clause is the same: the security of the goods, or the payment of the duties that may be due.

What the deposit is, and is not: it is the State taking custody of goods whose clearance failed, under a defined provision; it is not the end of the goods' story. What follows — the auction, the claims process, the disposal rules — is its own machinery with its own protections, and Module 10 teaches it. This lesson's boundary is the deposit: the stall has a destination, and the destination is defined.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Storage, risk and expense: the waiting goods
BODY:
Goods waiting in a customs-controlled area are waiting under rules, and the rules say whose risk the wait is. Section 5(2) provides that goods stored in a customs-controlled area with the permission of the proper officer, prior to shipment, are stored at the risk and expense of the exporter, and are subject to rent and other charges as may be prescribed. The same risk-and-expense principle runs through the storage of imported goods awaiting clearance: the goods remain the trader's goods, and the waiting is the trader's cost.

The storage costs are commercial — the terminal's or warehouse's rent and charges, prescribed or tariffed — and they are distinct from the customs charges: the goods can be released by customs and still accrue storage, because release ends the legal control without ending the physical wait. The professional advice covers both: the legal path to release (section 6(1)'s conditions) and the commercial path to delivery (the terminal's procedures and the clock of storage charges).

The discipline this teaches: answer the stalled clearance by naming the clock — the statutory clock (the fourteen-day rule of section 4(3), the lawful periods of section 52) and the commercial clock (the storage tariff) — because both run, and neither waits for the other.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Demurrage and detention: concepts, not figures
BODY:
The trade's vocabulary for waiting costs — demurrage (the charge for a container staying with the shipping line beyond its free days), detention (the charge for the container staying with the trader beyond its return date), and the port's own storage rent — is commercial vocabulary, and this lesson teaches the concepts without the figures.

The reason for the restraint is the anchoring discipline: the charges are set by the shipping lines' tariffs and the port's published rates, not by Act 891 — so the lesson states the concept (the waiting goods generate commercial charges, on clocks the carriers and the port set) and refuses the numbers, which belong to the tariffs. What the Act does supply is the legal frame around them: the goods under customs control while they wait (section 5(1)(b)'s storage), the risk-and-expense rule for stored goods, and the statutory clocks — the fourteen-day rule and the uncleared-goods machinery — that can overtake the commercial clocks if the clearance stalls long enough.

The learner's advice formula: the commercial clocks are in the tariffs; the statutory clock is in the Act; both are running — clear the goods before the statutory one overtakes the commercial one.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Two containers, two stalls, two threads
BODY:
Step 1. The release stall. A container of spare parts is declared; the risk system selects it for examination. The examination finds nothing; the required documents are complete; the Commissioner-General releases the goods under section 6(1). The agent books the delivery — but the terminal's yard sequence puts the container's extraction three days out. The client calls, frustrated.

Step 2. The diagnosis. The agent names the thread: the goods are released — the customs conditions of section 6(1) are met — so the wait is the terminal's delivery sequence, an operational matter on the terminal's procedures and its storage tariff. The remedy is the port's thread, not a customs one.

Step 3. The second container. A different client's container sits undeclared for weeks. No declaration has been lodged; the goods are now uncustomed within the section 151 definition — liable to duty on which the full duties have not been paid. Section 52's machinery and the fourteen-day rule of section 4(3) — the goods were entered under another procedure — are both closing in.

Step 4. The rescue. The agent lodges the declaration, the goods are examined and released, and the container leaves the yard before the statutory clocks overtake it — but after the commercial storage clock has cost the client real money.

What does this example demonstrate? Two stalls, two threads, two clocks — the released-but-undelivered container waits on operations; the undeclared container races the statute.

Key lesson: name the thread and the clock before advising — the remedy is the thread's, and the cost is the clock's.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: The same wait at a foreign port, and the free-time clock
BODY:
Step 1. The scene. At a large North-European port, an importer's consignment clears customs quickly but the buyer's warehouse is full; the container stays in the terminal for eleven days after release. The terminal's tariff gives five free days; days six to eleven accrue storage charges per the published rate.

Step 2. The two clocks. The customs control ended at release — the goods left customs control with the conditions met. What runs now is the commercial clock: the terminal's free time and storage rate. No provision of the customs statute sets the charge; the tariff does.

Step 3. The statutory shadow. Had the goods not been released — had they stalled undeclared — the statutory clock would run instead: the uncleared-goods machinery and, where entered goods overstay, the fourteen-day rule. The commercial clock and the statutory clock run on different provisions, and the professional watches both.

Step 4. The transfer. The lesson is the same for Ghanaian practice: released goods wait on the commercial clock; unreleased goods race the statutory one; and the agent who names which clock is running names the client's real problem.

What does this example demonstrate? Release splits the waiting into two worlds — the commercial clock after release, the statutory clock before it — and advice names which world the client is in.

Key lesson: clear the goods before the statute's clock overtakes the tariff's; and never quote a charge the tariff does not publish.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Deposit is a stage, not an ending
BODY:
Section 53's State warehouse deposit is where stalled goods land, and the learner must not read it as the goods' end. The deposit secures the goods and the duties that may be due; what follows — the auctioned-goods procedure of section 93, the seizure and claims machinery of sections 124 to 126, the disposal rules — runs on provisions with their own protections, and Module 10 teaches them in full. The distinction matters in advice: a client whose goods were deposited still has a legal position to manage, and a client told the goods are "gone" will abandon a position that the machinery still allows. Deposit is the State taking custody under a defined provision; the story after the deposit belongs to Module 10.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Quote the concept, not the charge
BODY:
Demurrage, detention and storage are real costs with real rates — and none of those rates is in this programme's sources. The tariff and the carriers' schedules set them, and they change; a figure quoted today is a guess by next quarter. The anchoring rule (spec C4.5) and the money rule (spec C4.3) therefore both bite: this lesson states the concepts and the clocks, and refuses the figures. The professional habit transfers directly to practice: when a client asks what the waiting will cost, the answer names the clock (the terminal's tariff, the carrier's free days) and points to the published rate — because the professional who quotes a remembered demurrage figure has replaced a checkable fact with a guess that one tariff page can falsify. Concepts with sources; figures with citations; nothing in between.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A consignment has been examined, no offence was found, and the required documents are complete — but one condition specific to the goods' procedure (an agency permit) is still outstanding. The agent asks whether the goods can be released now. Which option best states the position under section 6(1)?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Yes — examination and documents are the only conditions that matter.
2. Yes, because the risk system selected the goods for a document check only.
3. No — section 6(1) requires all its conditions: examination or exemption from examination, no offence found, the required documents acquired, and the stated conditions met; an outstanding procedure-specific permit means the last condition is unmet, and release waits for it.
4. Yes, if the agent signs an undertaking to supply the permit after release.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 6(1) of Act 891 states release's conditions conjunctively: the goods have been examined or exempted from examination, an offence has not been found, the required documents have been acquired, and the stated conditions are met. Release is a legal conclusion built from the whole clearance, and every condition must be satisfied — the scenario's first two are, the third is, but the outstanding permit is the "stated conditions" failing.

Answer 1 drops the conjunctive structure — the provision names four conditions, not two. Answer 2 confuses the control decision (examination or exemption) with the release decision; selection for a document check answers the first condition only. Answer 4 invents an undertaking mechanism the section does not provide — a condition is met when it is met, and the permit is the condition.

The takeaway: release is a four-condition checklist read together — one outstanding condition is a waiting clearance, whatever the agent's optimism.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
An importer abandons a consignment at the port and stops all contact. Months pass with no declaration lodged and no duty paid. Which option best describes the goods' legal state and destination under Act 891?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The goods are simply the port's property now, and no customs provision applies.
2. The goods are uncustomed goods within section 151's definition — dealt with contrary to the customs provisions, with no duty paid — and section 52's machinery with the section 53 State warehouse deposit takes them in, the deposit securing the goods and the duties that may be due.
3. The goods become free samples that the terminal may distribute.
4. The goods are automatically exported back to the origin country at customs' expense.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 151 of Act 891 defines uncustomed goods as goods liable to duty on which the full duties due have not been paid, and goods, whether liable to duty or not, that are imported or exported or in any way dealt with contrary to the provisions of the Act in relation to customs — the abandoned consignment satisfies the definition's second limb (imported goods dealt with contrary to the customs provisions) with no declaration lodged and no duty paid. Section 52's machinery addresses the uncleared goods, and section 53 provides the deposit in the State warehouse — defined as the place approved by the Commissioner-General for the deposit of uncustomed, un-entered, un-examined, abandoned, detained or seized goods, for the security of the goods or the payment of duties that may be due. Abandoned goods are expressly in the list.

Answer 1 dissolves the customs perimeter at the moment it matters most. Answer 3 and Answer 4 invent outcomes with no provision behind them.

The takeaway: abandonment has a defined legal path — uncleared, uncustomed, deposited — and each stage names its provision; the advice that follows the path starts before it is walked.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A client's goods were released by customs five days ago but are still in the terminal yard, and storage charges are accruing. The client demands that customs "waive the storage". Which option best advises the client?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The client should demand the waiver from customs, because customs released the goods and is therefore responsible for the storage.
2. The client is right to refuse the charges; storage after release is unlawful.
3. The advice is to name the thread and the clock: release ended the customs control, so the accruing storage is the terminal's commercial charge on its published tariff — the remedy runs through the terminal's procedures and the client's own delivery arrangements, not through a customs waiver.
4. The charges can be waived by any officer of the Customs Division at the client's request.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. The lesson's distinction between release and delivery answers the scenario: release under section 6(1) is the customs decision that ends the legal control; the delivery sequence and the storage accruing in the yard are the terminal's operational layer, charged on the port's published tariff. The goods left customs control when release was granted; what runs now is the commercial clock, and its terms live in the tariff — not in any customs provision, and not in any officer's discretion to waive.

Answer 1 assigns the terminal's tariff to customs — the two ledgers the lesson separates. Answer 2 asserts an unlawfulness with no basis; post-release storage on the published tariff is exactly how the commercial layer works. Answer 4 invents a waiver power that the statute does not give to customs at all, let alone to any officer.

The takeaway: after release, the clock is commercial and the counterparty is the terminal — name the thread, check the tariff, and move the goods.
