=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The State warehouse: the deposit and its purpose
BODY:
Section 53 provides the deposit of goods in the State warehouse, and section 151 defines the place: a place approved by the Commissioner-General for the deposit of uncustomed, un-entered, un-examined, abandoned, detained or seized goods, for the security of the goods or the payment of duties that may be due. The definition's list is the deposit's map — six ways goods come into the State's keeping — and its purpose clause is the deposit's two reasons: the goods' security, or the duties that may be due.

Section 52 provides the uncleared-goods machinery that feeds it: the goods nobody clears within the lawful time. The deposit is the machinery's destination — the goods' move from the port's controlled areas into the State's defined custody.

The deposit is a stage, and this lesson works the machinery that follows it: the auctioned-goods procedure of section 93, the seizure-and-forfeiture machinery of sections 124 to 126, and the allocation document the learner will actually meet — the anonymised Form 80 specimen. The deposit's boundary from Module 3 stands: the State's custody is not the goods' end.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The auction: section 93's procedure
BODY:
Section 93 provides the auctioned-goods procedure, and its provisions are specific.

Subsection (1): goods may be auctioned under the direction of the Commissioner-General. Subsection (2): the price at which goods are sold at an auction sale includes the customs duty — the auction's price is duty-inclusive, which is why the buyer takes the goods without a further duty charge. Subsection (3): the value of duty for the goods auctioned is not less than the value estimated by the proper officer — the auction's floor is the officer's estimate. Subsection (4): the Auction Sales Act, 1989 (PNDCL 230) does not apply to sales under the Act where the sale is conducted by a proper officer — the customs auction runs on the customs Act's own machinery.

The learner's reading: the auction is the suspense's resolution by sale — the goods leave the State's custody through a sale whose price carries the duty, directed by the Commissioner-General, floored by the proper officer's estimate, under the customs Act's own rules rather than the general auction statute.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Seizure and the notice: section 124's machinery
BODY:
Section 124 provides the seizure-and-forfeiture machinery's opening. Subsection (1): a conveyance used in the importation, attempted importation, landing, removal, movement, exportation or attempted exportation of uncustomed, forfeited, prohibited or restricted goods is liable to forfeiture to the State — the conveyance itself, not only the goods.

Subsection (2) states the notice's forms: where goods liable to forfeiture are seized, the seizing officer gives written notice of seizure to the owner — by personal delivery; by registered post to the usual or last known address; in the case of a body corporate, at its registered or principal office; or, where the person has no address in the country or the address is unknown, by publication in the Gazette or a daily newspaper of national circulation.

The notice's forms are the owner's rights' start: the seizure that is not notified is a seizure the claims machinery cannot answer. The learner's reading: the machinery's discipline is documentary — seizure, notice, claim, disposal — and each stage's paper is the next stage's input.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Claims for seized goods: section 125's requirements
BODY:
Section 125 provides the claims machinery, and its requirements are documentary and precise.

Subsection (1): an owner or the authorised agent of an owner may claim the restoration of goods seized under the Act. Subsection (2): the claimant states the place of residence and occupation. Subsection (3): the claimant produces the original copy of the notice of seizure at the time of making the claim. Subsection (4): where the claimant cannot produce the original notice, the claimant makes a statutory declaration that the seized goods belonged to the claimant at the time of seizure. Subsection (5): where the claimant resides outside the country, the agent entering the claim states on oath that the agent has the claimant's full authority.

The requirements are the machinery's discipline: the claim is the owner's (or the authorised agent's), identified, evidenced by the notice or by the statutory declaration, and — for the abroad claimant — authorised on oath. The learner's reading: the machinery's protections are procedural, and the procedure is the claim's road.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Disposal: section 126's custody and sale machinery
BODY:
Section 126 provides the disposal machinery, and its stages are timed.

Subsection (1): goods suspected to be uncustomed, when seized, are delivered immediately into the Commissioner-General's custody, unless the Commissioner-General directs otherwise. Subsection (2): goods of a non-perishable nature are deposited in a State warehouse. Subsection (3): perishable goods may be sold immediately by public auction by the proper officer. Subsection (4): non-perishable goods may be sold by public auction after fourteen days' notice published in the Gazette or at least two daily newspapers of national circulation.

The machinery's reading: custody first, warehouse for the non-perishables, immediate auction for the perishables, and the fourteen-day Gazette notice before the non-perishables' sale — the notice being the same public-notice instrument the place definitions use (the port, the approved places). The disposal's stages are the owner's rights' clock: the notice's publication is the auction's gateway, and the claim's machinery runs alongside it.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A vehicle's path from seizure to allocation
BODY:
Step 1. The state. A vehicle imported without duty being settled becomes uncustomed goods — section 151's definition: goods liable to duty on which the full duties have not been paid. Its overstay in a customs-controlled area engages section 4(3)'s fourteen-day rule: liable to forfeiture.

Step 2. The seizure and the notice. The goods are seized; section 124(2)'s notice is delivered — personally, by registered post to the last known address, or by publication where the address is unknown. The notice starts the owner's claim rights under section 125.

Step 3. The custody and the deposit. The goods are delivered into the Commissioner-General's custody (section 126(1)) and deposited in the State warehouse (section 126(2)) — non-perishable, so no immediate sale.

Step 4. The auction. The goods are auctioned under section 93: under the Commissioner-General's direction, the price including the customs duty, floored by the proper officer's estimate. The fourteen-day Gazette notice of section 126(4) precedes the sale.

Step 5. The allocation. The auction's outcome produces the allocation letter — the Form 80 specimen's structure: the goods' particulars (type, chassis, age, capacity), the revenue liability, the offer price excluding handling charges. The allocated buyer pays and takes the vehicle; the account closes.

What does this example demonstrate? The path is a chain of provisions — the uncustomed state, the forfeiture exposure, the seizure and notice, the custody, the auction, the allocation — and each stage's paper is the next stage's input.

Key lesson: the machinery is documentary from seizure to allocation, and the specimen is the chain's last paper — read as training, not as a template for the parties.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A perishable consignment's fast path
BODY:
Step 1. The goods. A consignment of fresh produce is abandoned at the port — the owner gone, no declaration lodged. The goods are perishable: their value decays daily.

Step 2. The custody. The goods are uncustomed goods within section 151's definition — imported, dealt with contrary to the customs provisions — and section 126(1) delivers them into the Commissioner-General's custody.

Step 3. The perishable branch. Section 126(3) provides the perishable goods' immediate sale: the proper officer may sell by public auction without the fourteen-day wait — the provision's recognition that the goods' value cannot wait for the notice cycle.

Step 4. The non-perishable contrast. Had the goods been machinery, section 126(2)'s State-warehouse deposit and section 126(4)'s fourteen-day Gazette notice would govern — the same machinery, timed to the goods' nature. The notice's publication runs in the Gazette or at least two daily newspapers of national circulation — the same public-notice instrument the place definitions use — and the owner's claim rights under section 125 run alongside the notice's clock.

What does this example demonstrate? The disposal machinery times itself to the goods: perishables sell immediately, non-perishables wait for the notice — and both paths are the Act's own.

Key lesson: the goods' nature sets the clock — and the learner reads the machinery's timing from the goods' character.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
At an auction conducted by a proper officer under section 93, a buyer offers below the proper officer's estimated value. Which option best states the position?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The sale proceeds at the buyer's offer, because auctions follow the bids.
2. The sale proceeds at the buyer's offer, minus the customs duty the buyer then pays separately.
3. The subsection's floor applies: section 93(3) provides that the value of duty for the goods auctioned is not less than the value estimated by the proper officer, and section 93(2) provides that the auction price includes the customs duty — so the bid below the estimate cannot stand as the duty's value.
4. The auction is cancelled permanently because one bid was low.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 93 of Act 891 states the auction's machinery: subsection (1) — goods may be auctioned under the Commissioner-General's direction; subsection (2) — the price at which goods are sold at an auction sale includes the customs duty; subsection (3) — the value of duty for the goods auctioned is not less than the value estimated by the proper officer. A bid below the estimate cannot set the duty's value below the officer's estimate — the subsection's floor applies to the duty's computation.

Answer 1 reads the auction as free-market bidding; the provision's floor is the revenue's protection. Answer 2 splits the price and the duty — subsection (2) makes the auction price duty-inclusive. Answer 4 invents a permanent cancellation; the floor governs the duty's value, and the auction's process continues under its machinery.

The takeaway: the auction's price includes the duty, and the duty's value floors at the proper officer's estimate — the revenue's protection is written into the sale itself.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A perishable consignment and a machinery consignment are both seized and delivered into the Commissioner-General's custody on the same day. Which option best states how section 126 times their disposal?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Both are sold immediately, because seizure starts the auction.
2. Both wait the same fourteen days, because the machinery is uniform.
3. The perishable goods may be sold immediately by public auction by the proper officer (section 126(3)); the non-perishable machinery is deposited in a State warehouse (section 126(2)) and may be auctioned after fourteen days' notice published in the Gazette or at least two daily newspapers (section 126(4)) — the machinery's timing follows the goods' nature.
4. Both are destroyed, because seized goods have no value.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 126 of Act 891 provides the disposal machinery's timing: subsection (1) — seized goods delivered immediately into the Commissioner-General's custody; subsection (2) — non-perishable goods deposited in a State warehouse; subsection (3) — perishable goods immediately sold by public auction by the proper officer; subsection (4) — non-perishable goods sold by public auction after fourteen days' notice published in the Gazette or at least two daily newspapers of national circulation. The takeaway: the disposal's timing follows the goods' nature — the perishables' immediate auction, the non-perishables' fourteen-day notice — and each stage names its subsection.
