=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The warehousing regime: duty parked, control full
BODY:
The warehousing regime is the suspense machinery at its fullest: goods sit secured in a private bonded warehouse — the building or place appointed by the Commissioner-General and licensed to be a place where goods to be warehoused are secured — with the duty question parked until the goods' destiny resolves it. Section 79 provides the regime's entry: the private bonded warehouse; section 5(1)(d) provides its place in the customs-controlled area's permitted activities: the storage and processing of imported goods under bond.

The regime's two anchors were taught earlier and are now worked: the licence (section 47(1)'s three conditions — the due licence, the keeper's deposited bond, the prescribed fees) and the appointment (section 79's regime, where the warehoused goods are secured). The keeper — section 151's warehouse keeper, the person licensed to operate the private bonded warehouse — is the regime's accountable professional.

This lesson works the regime as the keeper runs it: the licence held, the bond deposited, the records kept, the movements choreographed, the failures named. The licence is in an approved form (section 47(2)) and revocable at any time (section 47(3)) — the regime's permission is alive, and the keeper's compliance is what keeps it.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The keeper's records: the regime's account of itself
BODY:
Section 80 requires records to be kept in the bonded warehouse, and the requirement is the regime's spine. The goods under suspense are the revenue's security: every entry into the warehouse, every removal, every delivery must be on the keeper's records, because the records are how customs knows what is under suspense at any moment — and how the bond's exposure is measured.

Section 151's record definition carries the keeper's records whole: information kept in the ordinary course of business pertaining to any customs activity — statements, declarations, documents, data, books, papers, correspondence, accounts, financial accounting data. The warehouse's records are that definition in operation: the goods' entry, their stowing and position, their removals and deliveries, each accounted.

The audit provisions connect: section 7's post-clearance audit and the verification powers read the keeper's records, because the records are the regime's account of itself. A keeper whose records are complete can answer any question about the warehouse — what is secured, for whom, since when, moved where. A keeper whose records are incomplete is running a security the revenue cannot verify — and section 80's duty is what makes that incompleteness a breach, not a backlog.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The movement choreography: removals, deliveries, stowings, entries
BODY:
Goods under suspense still move, and the Act choreographs each movement.

Section 85 governs the removal — from a transit shed or from one warehouse to another: goods changing controlled places under control, the suspense travelling with them. Section 86 provides delivery in special circumstances — the Act's valve for cases the ordinary rules do not fit, under the conditions it states. Section 87 governs the stowing of goods — the physical arrangement inside the warehouse, under the regime's rules. Section 88 provides the entry of warehoused goods — the suspense's resolution: the goods enter home use, the duty is paid, and the regime's account closes for them.

The bonded carrier's role runs through the removals: section 151 defines it as the carrier licensed to transport bonded goods within the country, and section 45 is its licence — the movements between controlled places are its lawful work.

The choreography's principle: every movement happens under a provision, is recorded under section 80, and carries the suspense with the goods. Goods moved lawfully carry their suspense; goods moved outside the machinery meet the failure provisions.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The failure provisions: the regime's named risks
BODY:
Sections 81 to 84 name the regime's failure modes, and their sequence reads like the security manual the suspense needs.

Section 81: goods not duly removed — goods that should have left the regime and did not, with the Act's response. Section 82: unlawful access — persons in the warehouse who should not be there, next to the secured revenue. Section 83: unauthorised removal of goods — goods leaving the regime outside its machinery. Section 84: destruction and stealing of goods — the security's worst failures, with the Act's response.

Each provision exists because the suspense is only as strong as its controls: the bond secures the duty (section 47(1)(b)), the records account for the goods (section 80), and the failure provisions name what happens when the controls are breached. The learner reads them as the regime's risk register, written into the Act — the things that can go wrong with secured goods, and the provisions that answer.

The boundary holds: the penalties and offences these failures can carry are Module 14's. This lesson names the provisions and their subjects; the offence content waits.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The bonded carrier and the movement security
BODY:
The goods' movements between controlled places run on the carrier's licence, and the carrier's licence runs on the security machinery.

The bonded carrier — section 151's definition, the carrier licensed to transport bonded goods within the country — holds section 45's licence for the movements the regimes require: section 95(1)(a)'s transit movements "by a bonded carrier", the warehousing regime's removals between places. The carrier's licence is the movement's lawfulness: goods under suspense moved by an unlicensed carrier are goods leaving the regime's control.

The movement's security runs on the machinery of sections 108 to 111: the forms of security (section 108 — a cash deposit or its equivalent; a bond given by a reputable bank, financial institution or insurance guarantor; an undertaking by an authorised economic operator), the surety's joint and several liability (section 109), the release and cancellation of the guarantee (section 110), and the bond's validity (section 111 — taken on behalf of the Authority, valid even if given by a person under twenty-one, cancellable by the Commissioner-General's order).

The learner's chain: the goods' movement is lawful because the carrier is licensed, the movement is recorded, and the security stands behind the duty the movement defers.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The keeper's compliance is the licence's life
BODY:
The regime's permission is alive, and the keeper's compliance is what keeps it alive. Section 47(3) lets the Commissioner-General revoke the licence at any time, with the licence fee's refund constrained to cases the subsection considers unreasonable or hardship-causing to withhold. Section 109 lets the Commissioner-General require a new bond or security where the bonded person or a surety dies, becomes bankrupt, departs the country leaving insufficient property, or is otherwise unable — in the Commissioner-General's opinion — to satisfy the security.

The keeper's compliance is therefore the licence's continuing condition: the records kept (section 80), the goods secured, the movements lawful, the bond's surety solvent. The regime's permission is not a founding event — it is a standing state, and the provisions keep it under review.

The learner's advice to a warehouse operator: the licence is the business, and the compliance is the licence — the records, the bond, the movements' lawfulness. The provisions' review powers are the regime's quality control, and the operator's discipline is what keeps the review uneventful.

HEADING: Goods warehoused, removed, entered — the keeper's year
BODY:

The duty question is parked; the goods are secured.

Step 2. The wait and the stowing. The goods are stowed per section 87, their position recorded. The keeper's records show the goods' entry, position and status — the regime's account of the suspense.

Step 3. A partial removal. A buyer purchases half the goods for the Ghanaian market. The exit is section 88's entry of warehoused goods: the duty on the sold goods is paid, and the goods leave the regime. The keeper's records show the removal and the entry — the account reconciles: half the goods out with duty, half still secured.

Step 4. A removal to another warehouse. The remaining goods move to a warehouse nearer the new buyer: section 85's removal — from one warehouse to another, under control, the suspense travelling with the goods, the movement on the records at both ends.

Step 5. The year's account. The keeper's records reconcile the whole year: goods in, goods stowed, goods removed under section 88 with duty, goods moved under section 85 under control — every movement a provision, every provision on the records.

What does this example demonstrate? The regime runs as a choreography of recorded movements — each exit and transfer named by its provision, the records reconciling the whole.

Key lesson: the keeper's records are the regime's account — and the year that reconciles is the year the licence stays safe.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A warehouse keeper's stock verification finds goods recorded as present that are physically missing. Which combination of provisions does the regime's response run through?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Section 85's removal provisions — the goods are treated as lawfully moved to another warehouse.
2. Section 80's records (the goods were accounted as secured), section 83's unauthorised removal and section 84's destruction and stealing provisions (the failure provisions), and the security machinery of sections 108-111 (the bond answering the duty the missing goods put at risk).
3. Section 88's entry provisions — the goods are treated as entered into home use.
4. Section 52's uncleared-goods machinery — the goods are treated as never warehoused.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. The regime's failure machinery is the answer's chain: section 80 of Act 891 required the keeper's records to account for the goods as secured — the records show what the regime lost; section 83's unauthorised removal and section 84's destruction and stealing provisions name the failure's kinds; and the security behind the suspense — the bond deposited under section 47(1)(b), running through sections 108 to 111's machinery (the forms of security, the surety's joint and several liability, the release and cancellation rules, the bond's validity) — answers the duty the missing goods put at risk.

Answer 1 converts the failure into a lawful removal — the opposite of the records' account. Answer 3 converts the failure into a duty payment that was never made. Answer 4 dissolves the regime's records into a state the goods never held.

The takeaway: the failure provisions name the regime's risks, the records prove what was lost, and the bond answers the duty — the three provisions are the failure's answer, read together.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
The surety on a bonded warehouse's bond becomes bankrupt. Which option best states the Commissioner-General's power and the regime's response?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Nothing: the surety's status is the surety's private matter until a claim is made.
2. The Commissioner-General may require a new bond or other security: section 109(2) provides the power where the person under the bond or a surety dies, becomes bankrupt or enters into an arrangement with creditors, departs the country leaving insufficient property, or is otherwise unable — in the Commissioner-General's opinion — to satisfy the security; and section 111 provides that the bond is taken on behalf of the Authority and cancellable by the Commissioner-General's order.
3. The bond is void immediately, and the goods are released from the regime.
4. The surety's bankruptcy transfers the bond to the warehouse keeper personally, with no further formality.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 109(2) of Act 891 provides the new-security power: where the person under the bond or a surety dies, becomes bankrupt or enters into an arrangement or composition with or for the benefit of creditors, departs the country without leaving sufficient property, or is for any other reason — in the Commissioner-General's opinion — unable or likely to be unable to satisfy the bond or security, the Commissioner-General may require a new bond or other form of security. Section 111's validity provisions (the bond taken on behalf of the Authority; cancellable by the Commissioner-General's order) frame the regime's security as a standing, reviewable state.

Answer 1 waits for the claim — the provision's power is preventive, exercised on the insolvency itself. Answer 3 dissolves the regime on the surety's failure; the machinery's answer is a new security, not a release. Answer 4 invents an automatic transfer with no provision behind it.

The takeaway: the security is a standing state under review — the surety's insolvency engages the new-security power, and the regime's protection is the machinery, not the hope.
