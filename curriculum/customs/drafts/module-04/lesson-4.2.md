=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The procedure code as a regime selector
BODY:
A declaration does not just describe goods — it selects the legal regime they enter. The procedure code carried on the declaration tells customs which procedure the goods are being placed under: home use, temporary admission, warehousing, transit, transhipment, export — and each procedure carries its own conditions, documents, controls and duty treatment.

The code's force is legal, not clerical. Selecting a code is placing the goods under a set of provisions: the goods declared under a transit code enter the transit procedure's obligations (bond, movement, acquittal); the goods declared under a warehousing code enter the bonded regime (security, records, removals). The code is how the declarant's choice of regime is expressed in the system — which is why choosing it is a legal decision, not a form-filling one.

The learner's discipline: know the regime you need before you know the code you want. The Act's sections define the regimes; the code expresses the choice; and the wrong code is a wrong procedure — with the wrong obligations, the wrong security, and the wrong duty treatment attached.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The declaration types the regimes produce
BODY:
Each regime produces its own entry type, and the learner can read the types as the regime map in miniature.

The import entry places goods under home use (section 74) — the default clearance into free circulation. The export entry declares goods leaving under section 97 — passed for the purpose of the export, and for overland movements one of the two elements of the legal export. The transit entry places goods under section 95's movement procedure — bond or bonded carrier or convention — with the acquittal machinery waiting at the exit. The warehouse entry places goods into the bonded regime of sections 79–88 — secured, duty deferred. The transhipment declaration covers section 96's same-port transfer.

One statutory form — the declaration defined in section 151 — expresses all of them, because the form's particulars are the goods' legal statement wherever they are lodged. What changes with the type is the procedure the particulars serve: a transit declaration's route fields exist because transit's obligations are about movement; a warehouse entry's security references exist because the bonded regime's obligations are about security.

The learner's reading: the entry type is the regime's shadow. Read the type, and you know which provisions the goods are under.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Pre-entry and the electronic declaration
BODY:
Two definitions carry the entry's timing and medium.

The "pre-entry" — section 50 — is the entry of goods prior to their importation into Ghana. Its commercial value is speed: the declaration work completed before the vessel arrives, so the goods' release can follow quickly after examination or exemption. The pre-entry is a full entry made early, not a draft; its particulars take legal effect within the entry machinery.

The "electronic declaration" — section 151 — is a declaration made electronically to the Commissioner-General, whether personally or on behalf of any person as agent, of the particulars of a consignment of goods imported or exported, or any other declaration the Commissioner-General authorises in connection with import or export under the Act. The definition makes the medium lawful: the electronic form is the declaration, with the same statutory force as paper.

Together they describe how modern clearances actually run: declarations lodged electronically, often before the goods arrive. The learner's discipline carries over unchanged — the electronic declaration's particulars are statements, the pre-entry's particulars take effect, and both are proved by the supporting file of section 14.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Missing or incomplete documents: the entry's failure mode
BODY:
Section 51 provides the entry stage's failure mode: missing or incomplete documents. Its position in the Act — between the entry provisions (ss.48–50) and the uncleared-goods machinery (s.52) — is the lifecycle's warning light: the entry that arrives without its full supporting file does not simply proceed with gaps; it engages a provision built for exactly that state.

The file it protects is section 14's: the documents to be submitted with the entry — the invoice, the transport document, the packing list, the permits and certificates the goods' procedure demands. A missing permit for restricted goods, a missing transport document, an incomplete declaration: each is the provision's subject.

The lesson's discipline is the one Module 2 built: assemble the file before lodging, because the provision's existence means an incomplete entry is a defined legal state with consequences — not a working draft that customs will quietly tolerate. The declaration's particulars are statements; the file is their proof; and section 51 is what happens when the proof is missing.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The regime map, from the Act's own sections
BODY:
The procedure codes select regimes, and the regimes are defined by the Act's own sections — which makes the Act itself the map of what each code selects.

Home use — section 74: the default, duty paid, free circulation. Temporary admission — section 75: goods in, duty suspended, goods out. Inward processing — section 76: goods in for processing and re-export. Re-importation — sections 77 and 78: Ghanaian goods returning, in the same state or after processing abroad. Warehousing — sections 79 to 88: goods secured under bond, duty deferred. Transit — section 95: goods crossing the country, duty secured. Transhipment — section 96: goods changing vessels in one port, duty unpaid. Exports — section 97: goods leaving, by vessel or overland.

The learner's map-reading skill: for any consignment, walk the sections and ask which one describes what the trader actually intends — then the code expresses that choice. The sections are the regimes' definitions; the codes are their addresses in the system; and a code chosen without reading its section is a procedure chosen blind.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Three consignments, three procedures
BODY:
Step 1. The cement. A trader imports cement for sale in Ghana. The goods will enter the market and stay: the regime is home use (section 74), the entry is the import declaration, the duty is paid, the goods enter free circulation. No special machinery applies.

Step 2. The machinery. A construction company imports earth-moving equipment for a two-year road project, after which the equipment will leave. The regime is temporary admission (section 75): the goods enter, the duty is suspended because the goods will not stay, and the procedure's conditions govern the stay. The code selects that regime; the declaration carries it.

Step 3. The corridor. A forwarder moves a consignment from Tema through Ghana to a landlocked neighbour. The goods never enter home use: the regime is transit (section 95) — duty secured by bond or bonded carrier, the movement under control, the acquittal waiting at the exit. The transit entry expresses it.

Step 4. The reading. Three consignments, three intentions — stay, visit, cross — and three procedures. The intentions were the trader's; the regimes are the Act's; the codes carry the choice into the system.

What does this example demonstrate? The procedure is chosen by what the trader intends the goods to do, and the Act's regime map is where the intention finds its legal home.

Key lesson: intent first, regime second, code third — never the reverse order.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: The same goods, different codes, at a foreign border
BODY:
Step 1. The pair. At a European border post, two identical machine parts arrive the same week. One is declared for free circulation; the other is declared under the border's temporary-admission procedure because it is exhibition equipment that will leave after the show.

Step 2. The consequences. The free-circulation goods pay their duty and enter the market. The exhibition goods enter under their procedure's security and conditions — duty deferred, departure required. Same goods, same origin, same tariff heading — different procedures, different duty outcomes, both lawful.

Step 3. The discipline the difference teaches. The trader's intention — sell here, or exhibit and leave — is what the procedure expresses, and the documentation must prove the intention's conditions: the temporary admission's departure obligation, the free circulation's duty payment. The officer reads the code, then tests the goods against the regime's conditions.

Step 4. The transfer. Ghana's regime map works the same way: the sections define the procedures, the codes select them, and the intention behind the movement is what the declaration legally expresses.

What does this example demonstrate? Identical goods can lawfully take different procedures — the procedure is chosen by the movement's intent, and the duty outcome follows the procedure, not the goods.

Key lesson: goods do not choose their procedure; the trader's intention does, and the declaration expresses it.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: The code names the regime, not the rate
BODY:
A procedure code tells customs which legal regime the goods enter — and the learner must not read it as a rate label. The duty treatment follows the regime (home use pays; temporary admission defers; transit secures), but the rate itself comes from the tariff row the classification selects, and the base comes from the valuation rules. A consignment's duty is the product of regime, classification and valuation together — three separate questions, answered from three separate sources: the procedure's section, the tariff row, and the valuation provisions. The learner who conflates them will quote a code where a rate is wanted, or a rate where a regime is wanted. The discipline: code answers "which procedure", classification answers "which heading", valuation answers "what base" — and the duty is where the three meet.

HEADING: Never quote a code from memory
BODY:

The learner who knows the regime map (home use, temporary admission, warehousing, transit, transhipment, export) can find any code on a current list in seconds; the learner who memorised digits owns their staleness.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A trader intends to bring exhibition equipment into Ghana for a two-week trade show, after which the equipment will leave. Which option best states the correct approach to the procedure?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Declare the goods for home use, because they will physically be in Ghana for two weeks.
2. Select the procedure the Act provides for goods that enter and leave — temporary admission under section 75 — because the trader's intention is a temporary stay, and the duty outcome follows the procedure.
3. Declare the goods for transit, because they will leave the country.
4. Bring the goods in without a declaration, because exhibition equipment is not traded.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. The lesson's method is intent first, regime second, code third. The trader's intention is a temporary stay with departure — which is the regime section 75 of Act 891 provides: temporary admission, where goods enter with the duty suspended because they will leave. The procedure's conditions govern the stay, and the code expresses the choice in the declaration.

Answer 1 confuses physical presence with legal intention: home use puts the goods into free circulation with duty paid — the wrong outcome for goods that will leave. Answer 3 selects transit, whose movement is between ports or through the country under bond — not a stay for exhibition. Answer 4 abandons the declaration system entirely; goods subject to customs control enter under procedures, whatever their trade-show purpose.

The takeaway: the declaration expresses the trader's intention through the regime the Act provides — and the intention (stay, visit, cross) is what picks the procedure.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
Where does the Act place the pre-entry in the entry machinery, and what does it achieve?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Section 50 provides the pre-entry — the entry of goods prior to their importation into Ghana — so the declaration work is completed before arrival and release can follow quickly after examination or exemption.
2. Section 50 provides a lighter declaration for small consignments.
3. Section 50 allows goods to be entered after release, to correct the particulars.
4. The pre-entry is the same thing as the electronic declaration; both terms describe the medium.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1. Section 151 of Act 891 defines the pre-entry as the entry of goods prior to their importation into Ghana, and section 50 provides the machinery. The pre-entry is a full entry made early: its particulars take legal effect within the entry machinery, and its commercial value is the speed it buys — the declaration work is done before the vessel arrives, so release can follow quickly once the goods are examined or exempted under section 6(1).

Answer 2 invents a small-consignment variant; the pre-entry is about timing, not size. Answer 3 confuses the pre-entry with correction machinery — it precedes importation, it does not follow release. Answer 4 merges two different definitions: the electronic declaration (section 151) is about the medium — made electronically to the Commissioner-General — while the pre-entry is about the timing. A pre-entry may be electronic, but the terms answer different questions.

The takeaway: the pre-entry moves the declaration work before the goods; the electronic declaration moves it onto the lawful medium — different definitions, different work.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A consignment arrives without the transport document required for its entry. Which option best states the position, and the discipline it teaches?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The entry proceeds and the document can follow at the trader's leisure, because documents are administrative.
2. The goods are destroyed, because missing documents forfeit them immediately.
3. Section 51 provides for missing or incomplete documents — the entry's defined failure mode — and the discipline is the one Module 2 built: assemble section 14's supporting file before lodging, because an incomplete entry is a defined legal state with consequences, not a tolerated draft.
4. The transport document is optional for goods arriving by sea.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 51 of Act 891 provides for missing or incomplete documents, and its position in the Act — after the entry provisions (ss.48–50), before the uncleared-goods machinery (s.52) — marks it as the entry's defined failure mode. Section 14 requires the documents to be submitted with the entry, and the transport document is among the core file: the movement facts it carries are part of the entry's proof.

Answer 1 treats the file as administrative decoration; the declaration's particulars are statements proved by the file, and section 51 exists because missing proof is a legal state. Answer 2 leaps to forfeiture — the defined consequence for missing documents is not instant destruction; the machinery runs on its own provisions. Answer 4 invents a mode-based exemption with no basis in section 14.

The takeaway: the file is the entry's proof, section 51 is its failure mode — assemble before lodging, because an incomplete entry is a defined state, not a draft.
