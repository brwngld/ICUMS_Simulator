=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Home use: the default procedure
BODY:
Section 74 of Act 891 provides the procedure most goods follow: home use — importation into free circulation, where the goods clear for consumption in Ghana with the duty paid and the requirements satisfied. Everything the earlier modules taught as "the clearance" — arrival, declaration, examination or exemption, release under section 6(1) — is the home-use procedure's spine, because home use is what a declaration selects when no special regime applies.

What home use achieves, legally: the goods leave customs control and enter the country's economic circulation. They may be sold, used, transformed, or moved domestically without further customs formality — because the border's claims on them (the duties of section 1(1), the restrictions of the control provisions, the agencies' requirements) have been settled at clearance.

What home use costs: the duty and charges at the rate the Harmonised System specifies (section 1(1)), calculated on the customs value, plus the compliance with every condition the goods' procedure carries. The learner should read home use as the baseline: every other customs procedure in the Act — temporary admission, warehousing, transit — is defined by how it differs from this default, usually by deferring or securing the duty home use would pay.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The export trigger: when goods legally leave
BODY:
Section 97 states when an export legally occurs, and it has two branches.

The vessel branch: subsection (1) provides that exports occur where goods are placed on board an aircraft or ship for consignment to a place outside the country. The trigger is the loading for a foreign consignment — the goods' placement on board, bound out.

The overland branch: subsection (2) provides that overland exportation occurs where the declaration is passed for that purpose and the goods cross the boundaries of the country. Both elements must be present — the declaration passed, and the physical crossing — because an overland export completes only when the papers and the movement meet at the boundary.

Subsection (3) adds the supervision frame: goods to be exported are subject to customs supervision and may be subject to customs controls, as prescribed by Regulations — and the prohibitions and restrictions on export are as provided in the relevant Schedules.

Why the branches matter: the export's legal moment decides which obligations had attached by then, and the two branches time the same event differently for sea and land. The learner fixes the branch before fixing anything else about an export.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The legal moments: import, entry, export
BODY:
Three fixed moments carry the timing law, and liability hangs on each.

"Time of import" — section 151: the time of arrival of a conveyance within the limits of Ghana from outside the country, with the intent to unlade merchandise. The import's obligations date from the crossing with intent to unlade.

"Time of entry" — section 48: the moment the declaration takes legal effect for the goods. Between arrival and entry, the goods are in the controlled area under the storage provisions; at entry, the declaration's particulars become the goods' legal statement.

"Time of export" — section 151: when goods within Ghana are placed on board a conveyance for consignment to a place outside Ghana and the international voyage commences — with section 97(2)'s overland variant adding the boundary crossing to the passed declaration.

Why the precision matters: section 1(1) imposes the duties and taxes on goods imported or exported — so the moment that qualifies the movement as an import or export is the moment the obligations crystallise. The rate in force, the party liable, the records due: all hang on which moment controls. A professional who cannot date-stamp the moment cannot say which law applied.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The conditions home use carries
BODY:
Home use is the default, not a free pass: the goods clear into circulation only when the clearance's full weight has been satisfied.

The duty and taxes: section 1(1) imposes them at the Harmonised System rate; sections 71 and 72 fix liability and calculation; section 73 provides payment. The structure is Module 8's subject — here, the point is that home use's duty is paid, not deferred or secured.

The documents: section 14's supporting file travels with the declaration, and section 51's missing-or-incomplete-documents provision stops the entry where the file fails.

The controls: the goods are examined or exempted from examination under the risk-based control of section 3; the agencies' permits for restricted goods are evidenced; and release follows under section 6(1) — no offence found, documents acquired, stated conditions met.

The learner's picture: home use is the whole clearance, applied. When a later module's procedure defers a piece of it — the duty, the processing, the movement — the deferral is measured against this baseline. That is why the module teaches home use first: it is the procedure every other procedure is defined against.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Why home use is the baseline
BODY:
Every special procedure in Act 891 is easiest to understand as home use with something changed, and the Act's own structure supports the reading.

Home use (section 74): goods enter free circulation — duty paid, controls satisfied. Temporary admission (section 75): the goods enter but the duty is suspended, because they will leave. Inward processing (section 76): the goods enter to be processed and re-exported. Warehousing (sections 79–88): the goods sit secured under bond, duty deferred until entry or re-export. Transit (section 95): the goods cross the country without entering it, duty secured by bond or convention. Transhipment (section 96): the goods change vessels in one port, duty unpaid.

Read that way, the procedures form a family tree with home use at the root: each special procedure is the default with one element altered — the duty deferred, the stay temporary, the movement controlled. The learner who holds home use firmly can learn any special procedure by asking the two questions: what is deferred here, and what secures it.

The rest of this module walks the family: the code that selects the procedure, the temporary-importation branch, and the suspense machinery that warehousing lives in.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The declaration in both procedures
BODY:
Import and export share one instrument: the declaration, defined in section 151 as a statutory form filed or to be filed with respect to imported or exported goods at the time of entry or export. The same statutory form, filed at different ends of the border, serves both procedures — and the declarant behind it is one of the four identities the Act fixes: the owner of the goods, the importer, the exporter, or the agent.

On the import side, the declaration takes effect at the time of entry (section 48) and carries the particulars the home-use clearance verifies. On the export side, the declaration is passed for the purpose of the export — and for overland movements, section 97(2) makes the passed declaration one of the two elements of the legal export, the boundary crossing being the other.

The pre-entry — section 50's entry of goods prior to their importation — serves the same instrument on the import side: the work done before arrival so that release can follow quickly after it.

One form, two directions, four lawful hands. The learner's file discipline from Module 2 applies at both ends of the border, because the declaration's particulars are statements wherever they are lodged.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Tiles for home use, end to end
BODY:
Step 1. The procedure. A Kumasi trader imports ceramic tiles for sale — no special regime applies, so the declaration selects home use under section 74: the goods will enter free circulation with duty paid.

Step 2. The entry. The pre-entry was lodged at sea under section 50; at the time of entry under section 48, the declaration takes effect. The supporting file under section 14 travels with it: the commercial invoice (FOB, per lesson 2.4), the bill of lading, the packing list.

Step 3. The value and the duty. The customs value is built from the invoice with the valuation rules' additions; the tariff row fixes the rate; sections 71 and 72 fix the liability and the calculation. The duty, import VAT and charges are assessed — paid, not deferred, because home use pays.

Step 4. The control. The risk system under section 3 selects a document check. The goods are exempted from physical examination; no offence is found; the required documents are acquired.

Step 5. The release and circulation. Section 6(1) is satisfied, and the Commissioner-General releases the goods. The tiles leave customs control into free circulation: sold, delivered, used — no further customs formality, because home use is complete.

What does this example demonstrate? Home use is the whole clearance applied: entry, value, duty, control, release — and the goods' freedom afterwards is the procedure's definition.

Key lesson: the default procedure is the whole curriculum's spine in miniature; learn it here and every special procedure is a variation.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: An export leg: cocoa crosses the boundary by road
BODY:
Step 1. The goods and the route. A Ghanaian processor ships 20 tonnes of processed cocoa to a buyer in a neighbouring country by road — an overland export, so section 97(2)'s branch applies.

Step 2. The declaration. The exporter's agent lodges the export declaration — the same statutory form, filed at the export end — and it is passed for the purpose of the export. Section 39's account of cargo rides with the departing conveyance, so the border can reconcile what leaves.

Step 3. The legal moment. Under section 97(2), the export occurs when the declaration is passed AND the goods cross the boundaries of the country. The agent date-stamps both: the passed declaration, the physical crossing. Either without the other is not the legal export.

Step 4. The supervision. The goods travel under customs supervision (section 97(3)), and the Schedules' export prohibitions and restrictions have been checked — cocoa is a controlled export commodity, and the permits the Schedules require are in the file.

Step 5. The mirror. At the destination border, the buyer's agent lodges the import declaration: the home-use procedure of the importing country. One movement, two procedures, joined at the boundary.

What does this example demonstrate? The overland export is two elements — declaration passed, boundary crossed — and the export declaration is the same instrument the import side uses, pointed the other way.

Key lesson: fix the branch (vessel or overland), fix the moment, and the export's obligations date from it.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Home consumption is free circulation, not freedom from obligations
BODY:
"Home use" releases the goods into free circulation — but free circulation describes the customs position, not an absence of law. The goods that leave a home-use clearance remain subject to every non-customs obligation that follows them: the agencies' product rules at the market, the standards their sale must meet, the records their trader keeps under section 9. What home use ends is the customs control — the detention, the procedure, the duty question — because the border's claims were settled at clearance. The learner's precision matters here: a client told the goods are "free now" should hear what that means — free of customs control, into the market — not that the goods have exited the legal system entirely. Section 7's post-clearance audit reads the file for years afterwards.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
An importer's goods arrive at Tema on Monday. The declaration takes effect on Wednesday, when the entry is passed. On Friday the goods are released into free circulation. Which option best states when time of import occurred under Act 891?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Wednesday, because the declaration taking effect is the import.
2. Friday, because importation is complete on release into free circulation.
3. Monday — time of import is defined in section 151 as the time of arrival of the conveyance within the limits of Ghana from outside the country with the intent to unlade merchandise; the entry and release are later stages, not the import moment.
4. Sunday, because the vessel crossed into Ghanaian waters the day before berthing.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 151 of Act 891 defines time of import as the time of arrival of a conveyance within the limits of Ghana from outside the country with the intent to unlade merchandise. The definition fixes the moment at the conveyance's crossing with intent to unlade — the arrival — and the later stages have their own names: the time of entry when the declaration takes effect (section 48), and the release when the conditions of section 6(1) are met. The three moments are distinct because they do distinct legal work.

Answer 1 confuses the entry with the import; the pre-entry machinery of section 50 exists precisely because the declaration can precede the goods. Answer 2 confuses the end of customs control with the start of importation — release is the exit from control, not the definition of entry into it. Answer 4 reaches before the intent to unlade can be fixed to a defined moment; the crossing with that intent is the definition's own line.

The takeaway: three moments, three names, three legal works — date-stamp the arrival as the import, and the rest of the timeline falls into place.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
A trader trucks goods to the Aflao boundary for export. The export declaration is passed on Tuesday. The goods physically cross the boundary on Thursday. On which day did the export legally occur, and why?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Tuesday — a passed declaration completes an export on its own.
2. Thursday — section 97(2) provides that overland exportation occurs where the declaration is passed for that purpose AND the goods cross the boundaries of the country; both elements met on Thursday.
3. Tuesday, because declarations always precede movements in customs law.
4. Neither day: overland exports require the goods to be placed on board a vessel.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 97(2) of Act 891 provides that in the case of overland exportation, exports occur where the declaration is passed for that purpose and the goods cross the boundaries of the country. The branch has two elements — the passed declaration and the physical crossing — and the export occurs when both exist, which the scenario's Thursday supplies.

Answer 1 drops the second element: a passed declaration for goods that have not crossed is a prepared export, not a legal one. Answer 3 asserts a declaration-first rule that contradicts the provision's conjunctive wording. Answer 4 confuses the branches: placement on board a vessel or aircraft is subsection (1)'s vessel-branch trigger; overland movements follow subsection (2)'s own rule.

The takeaway: the export's legal moment follows its branch — on board for the sea and air, declaration plus boundary for the land — and the branch is fixed before the obligations are dated.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
Which option best states what the home-use procedure achieves for imported goods, as section 74 provides it?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The goods enter free circulation — duty paid and requirements satisfied — and may be sold, used or moved domestically without further customs formality.
2. The goods enter the country duty-free, because home use is an exemption procedure.
3. The goods remain under customs control until they are re-exported.
4. The goods enter free circulation only after a period in a bonded warehouse.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1. Section 74 of Act 891 provides the home-use procedure: goods imported for home consumption enter free circulation when the clearance is complete — the duties and taxes of section 1(1) assessed and paid, the controls satisfied under section 3, the documents acquired, and release granted under section 6(1)'s conditions. Free circulation is the procedure's definition: the goods leave customs control into the market.

Answer 2 inverts the procedure's nature — home use pays; the duty-deferral procedures (temporary admission, warehousing) are the ones that suspend, and they are defined against this baseline. Answer 3 describes a suspense or transit state, which is precisely what home use is not. Answer 4 invents a warehouse requirement the Act does not carry — warehousing is a chosen alternative procedure, not a stage every import passes through.

The takeaway: home use is the default procedure the Act's other procedures are defined against — free circulation, duty paid, controls satisfied — and the learner measures every special procedure by what it changes from this baseline.
