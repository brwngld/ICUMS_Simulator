=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The suspense idea: duty deferred, control retained
BODY:
The deferral family's procedures postpone the duty question for goods that will leave. The suspense machinery postpones it for goods that stay — secured in a controlled place until the duty or the export settles them. Section 5(1)(d) names the place where it happens: the storage and processing of imported goods under bond, inside a customs-controlled area.

"Suspense" is the idea the Act builds: the duty question hangs — suspended — while the goods sit secured, and it is resolved later by whatever the goods' destiny turns out to be. Entry into home use: duty paid. Re-exportation: duty never paid, because the goods left. The suspense is the wait between entry and resolution, and the machinery of sections 79 to 88 is how the wait is controlled.

The learner's picture: a warehouse as a waiting room with a lock. The goods are in Ghana but not in its commerce; the revenue is not collected but not forgotten — it is secured; and every movement of the goods while they wait is a provision's business.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The licence and the appointment
BODY:
Two provisions create the place. Section 47(1) — taught in lesson 1.3 as the licence contrast — provides the conditions: a building or place may be used as a private bonded warehouse only if it is duly licensed by the Commissioner-General, the keeper deposits the required bond, and the keeper pays the prescribed fees. Section 79 then provides the regime: the private bonded warehouse, defined in section 151 as a building or place appointed by the Commissioner-General and licensed to be a place where goods to be warehoused are secured.

The two words carry two acts: appointed (the Commissioner-General selects the place) and licensed (the keeper is authorised to operate it). The keeper is defined too: the person licensed to operate a private bonded warehouse — the accountable professional of the whole regime.

The licence is in an approved form (section 47(2)), and section 47(3) lets the Commissioner-General revoke it at any time — with the revocation's consequences for the goods following the section's own terms. The learner's reading: the bonded warehouse exists because two officials' acts — the appointment of the place and the licence of the keeper — brought it into the regime, and both acts can be revisited.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The keeper's records
BODY:
Section 80 requires records to be kept in the bonded warehouse, and the requirement is the regime's spine. The goods under suspense are the revenue's security: every entry into the warehouse, every removal, every delivery must be on the keeper's records, because the records are how customs knows what is under suspense at any moment.

The lesson's record discipline from Module 2 applies with full force: the keeper's records are records within section 151's definition — information kept in the ordinary course of business pertaining to customs activity — and they are the regime's proof. An entry of goods not on the records is goods the security does not cover; a removal not recorded is goods that left the suspense without its machinery.

The audit provisions connect here: section 7's post-clearance audit and the verification powers read the keeper's records, because the records are the regime's account of itself. A keeper whose records are complete can answer any question about the warehouse; a keeper whose records are incomplete is running a security the revenue cannot verify — which is why the record duty is not clerical but structural.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Movements: removals, entries, deliveries
BODY:
Goods under suspense still move, and the Act choreographs the movements.

Section 85 governs removal from a transit shed or from one warehouse to another — goods changing controlled places under control, so the suspense travels with the goods. Section 88 provides the entry of warehoused goods — the moment the suspense ends by the goods entering home use, with duty paid. Section 86 provides delivery in special circumstances — the Act's own valve for cases the ordinary rules do not fit. Section 87 governs the stowing of goods — the physical arrangement inside the warehouse.

The choreography's principle: every movement of goods under suspense happens under a provision, because the movement is the security in motion. Goods moved lawfully carry their suspense with them; goods moved unlawfully leave the regime — and the next block's failure provisions are what meet them.

The learner's operational picture: the warehouse's daily life is entries (s.79's secured goods arriving), removals (s.85's controlled transfers), stowings (s.87), entries into home use (s.88), and special deliveries (s.86) — each named, each recorded under section 80, each a provision's business.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The failure modes the Act names
BODY:
The suspense regime's vulnerabilities get their own provisions, and their sequence reads like a security manual.

Section 81: goods not duly removed — goods that should have left the regime and did not. Section 82: unlawful access — persons in the warehouse who should not be there, next to the secured revenue. Section 83: unauthorised removal of goods — goods leaving the regime outside its machinery. Section 84: destruction and stealing of goods — the security's worst failures, with the Act's response.

Each provision exists because the suspense is only as strong as its controls: the bond secures the duty (section 47(1)(b)), the records account for the goods (section 80), and the failure provisions name what happens when the controls are breached. The learner reads them as the regime's risk register, written into the Act: the things that can go wrong with secured goods, and the provisions that answer.

Module 10 develops the regime fully — the bonds, the acquittals, the reconciliation. What this lesson requires is the machinery tour: the sections exist, they name the failures, and the failures engage them.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The security machinery behind the suspense
BODY:
The bond that section 47(1)(b) requires is not an informal promise — it plugs into the Act's security machinery of sections 108 to 111: the forms of security (s.108), the surety (s.109), the release and cancellation of the guarantee (s.110), and the validity of the bond (s.111). Module 10 teaches the machinery in full; this lesson places it.

The bond's job in the suspense regime: the duty that is deferred while the goods sit secured is guaranteed by the security — so if the goods leave the regime without their duty being settled, the guarantee answers. That is what makes the suspense safe for the revenue: the deferred question is not merely postponed; it is collateralised.

The learner's reading of the chain: the licence (s.47), the bond (s.47(1)(b) via ss.108–111), the records (s.80), the movement provisions (ss.85–88), and the failure provisions (ss.81–84) — five pieces, one regime. The suspense is safe because each piece covers the next's gap, and Module 10 assembles them into the working whole.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Goods warehoused pending a buyer, then entered and removed
BODY:
Step 1. The suspense begins. An importer's goods arrive without a buyer lined up. Rather than pay home-use duty on goods that may sit for months, the importer places them in a private bonded warehouse: the place is appointed and licensed (section 79, section 47), the keeper's bond secures the deferred duty, and the goods enter the suspense — secured, records opened under section 80, duty question hanging.

Step 2. The wait. The goods sit in the warehouse for four months. The keeper's records account for them throughout: their entry, their stowing (section 87), their position in the secured place. Nothing is owed yet; everything is secured.

Step 3. The buyer arrives. A buyer purchases the goods for the Ghanaian market. The exit from suspense is the entry of warehoused goods under section 88 — the goods enter home use, the duty is paid, and the suspense resolves the way the importer's new intention requires.

Step 4. The removal alternative. Had the buyer been abroad, the exit would have been section 85's removal — the goods moving under control to the port for re-exportation — with the suspense travelling with them until the departure closed it.

Step 5. The keeper's closure. The keeper's records close the goods' entry: entered, duty paid, delivered. The warehouse's account reconciles, and the security's exposure for those goods ends.

What does this example demonstrate? The suspense regime is a duty-parking service with its own choreography — secure, record, wait, and exit by entry or removal.

Key lesson: the goods' destiny picks the exit — home use pays, re-exportation moves — and the keeper's records prove the whole story.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A bond's failure at a foreign warehouse
BODY:
Step 1. The scene. At a foreign port's bonded warehouse, goods under suspense disappear: the keeper's records show them present, the physical count finds them gone. The regime's worst failure — the section 84 analogue: destruction and stealing of goods.

Step 2. The security's answer. The warehouse's keeper had deposited the bond that the licensing required. Because the goods under suspense were collateralised, the revenue's claim runs to the security: the duty that the goods' disappearance puts at risk is answered by the bond, and the surety behind it (the machinery of sections 108 to 111 in Ghana's Act) is the claim's counterparty.

Step 3. The keeper's exposure. The keeper's records had shown the goods present — so the failure is the keeper's: the security regime exists because the keeper's accountability is the regime's own. The licence that allowed the keeper to operate is also the licence the authority can revisit.

Step 4. The transfer. Ghana's Act names the same failures — goods not duly removed (s.81), unlawful access (s.82), unauthorised removal (s.83), destruction and stealing (s.84) — and the same machinery answers: the bond, the surety, the guarantee's validity.

What does this example demonstrate? The suspense is safe because it is collateralised: the failure of the physical control is answered by the security, and the keeper's accountability is the regime's design.

Key lesson: the bond is not paperwork — it is the reason the revenue can afford to wait.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Suspense stays; transit moves
BODY:
The learner's easiest confusion is between the suspense regime and transit, because both defer duty and both hold goods under control. The discriminator is the movement: warehousing keeps goods in a secured place — the suspense sits still, and the goods' exit is an entry into home use under section 88 or a re-exportation under section 89. Transit moves goods between places — the suspense travels with them under section 95's bond or bonded carrier, and the procedure closes at the exit with acquittal. The provisions signal the difference themselves: the warehousing machinery (ss.79–88) is about records, stowing and entries inside a place, while the transit machinery (s.95) is about bonds, carriers and routes between places. The learner who asks "do the goods stay or move?" has asked the question that picks the regime — and the answer decides which provisions the goods live under.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A trader wants to store imported goods for several months without paying the home-use duty yet, and asks what kind of place makes that lawful. Which option best states the position under Act 891?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Any secure private store can hold the goods under suspense, provided the trader pays rent.
2. A private bonded warehouse — a building or place appointed by the Commissioner-General and licensed under section 47 and section 79, with the keeper's bond deposited — is the place the regime provides: goods to be warehoused are secured there, with the duty question suspended.
3. The goods can be stored in a customs-controlled area indefinitely without any provision, because storage is a permitted activity.
4. Suspense storage requires the goods to be re-exported within fourteen days.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 47(1) of Act 891 provides the licensing conditions — due licence by the Commissioner-General, the keeper's deposited bond, the prescribed fees — and section 79 provides the private bonded warehouse, which section 151 defines as a building or place appointed by the Commissioner-General and licensed to be a place where goods to be warehoused are secured. The keeper — the person licensed to operate it — is the regime's accountable professional, and the suspense lives there: goods secured, duty deferred.

Answer 1 dissolves the regime into ordinary storage; the whole machinery (records, movements, failures, security) exists because the suspense needs a controlled place. Answer 3 half-knows the truth: section 5(1)(b) permits temporary storage in a customs-controlled area, but the bonded suspense of ss.79–88 is its own licensed machinery, not an indefinite permission. Answer 4 confuses the suspense with the fourteen-day rule of section 4(3), which governs entered goods in a controlled area — a different clock for a different state.

The takeaway: the suspense lives in a defined place, created by defined acts — appointment, licence, bond — and the keeper's records make it verifiable.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
Goods under suspense in a bonded warehouse are to be moved to another bonded warehouse in a different town. Which option best states what the Act requires?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The goods can simply be trucked to the new warehouse; the suspense does not care about movement.
2. Section 85 governs the removal — goods moving from a transit shed or from one warehouse to another under control, so the suspense travels with the goods — and the keeper's records under section 80 must account for the movement at both ends.
3. The goods must first enter home use and pay the duty, then be re-declared into the new warehouse.
4. Section 85 forbids movement between warehouses entirely.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 85 of Act 891 governs the removal from a transit shed or from one warehouse to another — the provision that choreographs goods changing controlled places while staying under suspense. The movement is under control, so the duty question keeps hanging; the keeper's records under section 80 account for the goods at both ends, because the records are the regime's proof of what is secured where.

Answer 1 dissolves the control that the suspense depends on — goods under suspense moved outside the machinery are the failure provisions' subject (s.83's unauthorised removal). Answer 3 forces a duty payment the regime exists to defer: the movement between secured places is precisely the case the suspension handles. Answer 4 is the opposite of the provision's text, which provides for the removal.

The takeaway: the suspense travels with the goods when the movement travels with the provision — section 85 is the choreography, and the records are its proof.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
During a bonded warehouse's stock verification, goods recorded as present are found missing. Which option best states how the regime responds?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The records are amended to remove the goods, and the matter ends.
2. The regime's failure provisions engage: goods not duly removed (s.81) and the unauthorised removal and destruction/stealing provisions (ss.83-84) name the failures, and the security behind the suspense — the bond of s.47(1)(b), backed by ss.108-111 — answers the duty the missing goods put at risk.
3. The missing goods are written off as a normal commercial loss for the keeper to absorb without any statutory consequence.
4. The keeper's licence is automatically renewed to allow restocking.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. The lesson's failure provisions are the regime's answer to exactly this state: section 83's unauthorised removal and section 84's destruction and stealing of goods name the ways secured goods leave the regime unlawfully; section 81's goods not duly removed covers the goods that fail to leave when they should. The records under section 80 showed the goods present — so the disappearance is the regime's failure, and the security answers it: the bond deposited under section 47(1)(b) runs through the machinery of sections 108 to 111 (forms of security, surety, release and cancellation of the guarantee, validity of the bond), which is why the deferred duty is collateralised.

Answer 1 amends the account to hide the failure — the false-statement direction, and the opposite of the records' purpose. Answer 3 treats a security failure as a commercial loss; the regime exists precisely because the revenue's exposure is not left to the keeper's absorptive capacity. Answer 4 invents a restocking renewal that no provision carries.

The takeaway: the suspense is collateralised — the failure provisions name the breaches, and the bond answers the duty the breaches put at risk.
