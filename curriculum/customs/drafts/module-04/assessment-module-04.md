MODULE 4 ASSESSMENT — Customs Regimes and Core Procedures
12 questions, 1 point each (platform default pass mark 70%). Questions are arranged in lesson order: Q1-3 test lesson 4.1, Q4-6 lesson 4.2, Q7-9 lesson 4.3, Q10-12 lesson 4.4.

=== ASSESSMENT Q1 | tests lesson 4.1 ===
QUESTION:
A trader tells his agent: "My goods cleared last week, so the duty must have been waived — home use means the border let them in free." Which option best evaluates the statement?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The statement is correct: goods that reach home use owe nothing, because release ends the revenue's interest.
2. The statement is correct only for goods under temporary admission.
3. The statement is wrong: home use is the default procedure in which the goods enter free circulation with the duties and taxes of section 1(1) assessed and paid — release under section 6(1) follows the payment and the controls, and ends the customs control, not the duty's existence.
4. The statement is wrong because home use applies only to goods that will be re-exported.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 74 of Act 891 provides the home-use procedure: goods enter free circulation when the clearance is complete — the duties and taxes imposed by section 1(1) assessed on the customs value, the controls satisfied, the documents acquired, and release granted under section 6(1)'s conditions. Release ends the customs control; it does not erase the duty that was paid to obtain it, nor does it make the payment optional.

Answer 1 confuses the end of control with the absence of revenue — the duty was the condition of the release, not a casualty of it. Answer 2 points at the wrong procedure: temporary admission is the deferral family's member, where the duty is postponed because the goods will leave; home use is where the duty is paid. Answer 4 inverts the procedure's direction — re-exportation belongs to the deferral family's exits, not to home use.

The takeaway: home use is the default the Act's other procedures are defined against — free circulation, duty paid — and the learner measures every special procedure by what it changes from this baseline.

=== ASSESSMENT Q2 | tests lesson 4.1 ===
QUESTION:
An exporter's goods are trucked to the Aflao boundary. The export declaration is passed on Monday; a logistics delay means the goods physically cross the boundary on Wednesday. A rival exporter places identical goods on board a vessel for Europe on Tuesday. Which option best states when each export legally occurred?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Both exports occurred when their declarations were passed — Monday and Tuesday respectively.
2. The trucked goods exported on Wednesday (section 97(2): the declaration passed AND the boundary crossed); the shipped goods exported on Tuesday (section 97(1): goods placed on board a vessel for consignment to a place outside the country).
3. Both exports occurred on physical crossing, because every export needs a boundary.
4. The trucked goods exported on Monday; the shipped goods on the vessel's departure day, whenever that falls.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 97 of Act 891 fixes the export's moment by branch. Subsection (1): exports occur where goods are placed on board an aircraft or ship for consignment to a place outside the country — the loading for the foreign consignment is the trigger, so the vessel exporter's export occurred on Tuesday's placement on board. Subsection (2): overland exportation occurs where the declaration is passed for that purpose AND the goods cross the boundaries of the country — two elements, and the trucker's export completed on Wednesday's crossing.

Answer 1 collapses both branches into the declaration; for the overland movement the provision is expressly conjunctive. Answer 3 imposes the boundary element on the vessel branch, whose trigger is the placement on board. Answer 4 misassigns both — the overland export does not occur at the declaration alone, and the vessel export does not wait for departure day.

The takeaway: the export's moment follows its branch — on board for the vessel, declaration plus boundary for the land — and the branch is fixed before any obligation is dated.

=== ASSESSMENT Q3 | tests lesson 4.1 ===
QUESTION:
A clearance file records three dates: the vessel's arrival on the 3rd, the entry taking effect on the 5th, and the goods' release on the 7th. A dispute arises about which date governs the duty's obligations. Which option best resolves it?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The 7th, because release is when the goods leave customs control and everything follows it.
2. The 5th, because the declaration is the only document that matters.
3. Each date governs its own stage — time of import (the arrival, section 151's definition), time of entry (section 48), and release (section 6(1)) — and the obligation at issue determines which moment controls; the duty's imposition attaches to the goods as imported or exported under section 1(1), which is why the moments are fixed by definition rather than by preference.
4. All three dates are legally irrelevant; the only date is the invoice's.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. The lesson's legal moments each do distinct work: time of import is defined in section 151 as the arrival of the conveyance within the limits of Ghana with intent to unlade merchandise; the time of entry under section 48 is when the declaration takes legal effect; and release under section 6(1) ends the customs control. Section 1(1) imposes the duties and taxes on goods imported or exported — so the moment that qualifies the movement as an import or export is the moment the obligations crystallise, and the dispute's answer depends on which obligation is at issue.

Answer 1 makes release the master date — release ends the control, it does not define the import. Answer 2 makes the declaration the only date — the pre-entry machinery of section 50 exists precisely because declarations can precede the goods. Answer 4 discards the statutory moments for a commercial paper.

The takeaway: three moments, three legal works — the professional date-stamps all three and lets the obligation at issue pick the governing one.

=== ASSESSMENT Q4 | tests lesson 4.2 ===
QUESTION:
A trainee must place four consignments under procedures: cement for sale in Ghana; exhibition equipment that will leave after a show; machinery for a corridor movement to a landlocked neighbour;goods to be stored pending buyers. Which option best matches each to its regime under the Act's map?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. All four take home use under section 74, because all four are imports.
2. Cement — home use (s.74, duty paid, free circulation); exhibition equipment — temporary admission (s.75, duty deferred, goods leave); corridor machinery — transit (s.95, duty secured by bond or bonded carrier); storage-pending goods — warehousing (ss.79-88, duty deferred under security).
3. Cement — warehousing; exhibition equipment — home use; corridor machinery — transhipment; storage-pending goods — temporary admission.
4. The trainee should ask the shipping line which code to use, because the line controls the procedures.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. The lesson's regime map reads the Act's own sections: home use (s.74) for goods entering free circulation with duty paid; temporary admission (s.75) for goods entering with the duty deferred because they will leave; transit (s.95) for goods moving across the country under a bond or bonded carrier without duty payment; the warehousing machinery (ss.79-88) for goods secured under bond with the duty deferred until entry or re-export. The trader's intention — sell, visit, cross, store — picks the regime, and the code expresses the choice.

Answer 1 flattens the map into the default; the procedures exist because intentions differ. Answer 3 scrambles the matches — warehousing for goods being sold now, home use for goods leaving, transhipment (s.96's same-port transfer) for a corridor. Answer 4 hands the legal choice to a commercial party whose interest is carriage, not the goods' procedure.

The takeaway: intent first, regime second, code third — and the Act's sections are the map that turns intention into procedure.

=== ASSESSMENT Q5 | tests lesson 4.2 ===
QUESTION:
A colleague says: "The pre-entry and the electronic declaration are the same thing — both are declarations made on the computer." Which option best corrects the colleague?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The colleague is right: both terms describe the electronic medium.
2. The colleague has merged two different definitions: the pre-entry (s.50) is about timing — the entry of goods prior to their importation into Ghana; the electronic declaration (s.151) is about medium — a declaration made electronically to the Commissioner-General. A pre-entry may be electronic, but the terms answer different questions.
3. The colleague is right, because all declarations are electronic now.
4. The colleague is wrong because the pre-entry is paper-only.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 50 of Act 891 provides the pre-entry, and section 151 defines it as the entry of goods prior to their importation into Ghana — a timing definition: the declaration work completed before the goods arrive, so release can follow quickly after examination or exemption. Section 151 separately defines the electronic declaration as a declaration made electronically to the Commissioner-General, whether personally or on behalf of any person as agent — a medium definition: the electronic form is the lawful declaration, with the same statutory force. One term answers when, the other answers how.

Answer 1 accepts the merge; the definitions' own words separate them. Answer 3's point about current practice may be true and is irrelevant to what the terms mean. Answer 4 overcorrects into a paper-only claim the Act makes nowhere.

The takeaway: the pre-entry answers "when", the electronic declaration answers "how" — and a professional who keeps the definitions separate can describe any clearance's timing and medium precisely.

=== ASSESSMENT Q6 | tests lesson 4.2 ===
QUESTION:
An agent lodges an entry without the transport document, arguing that "the code on the declaration determines the duty, so documents are secondary". Which option best evaluates the argument?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The argument is correct: the procedure code is the entry's substance.
2. The argument is half-right: the code matters, but documents are needed only for restricted goods.
3. The argument fails twice: section 51 provides for missing or incomplete documents as the entry's defined failure mode, with section 14's file as the entry's proof — and the procedure code's meaning is the regime it selects, not the rate or a substitute for the file; the code and the documents answer different questions.
4. The argument fails because transport documents are never required by the Act.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 51 of Act 891 provides for missing or incomplete documents, positioned after the entry provisions (ss.48-50) as the entry's defined failure mode; section 14 requires the documents to be submitted with the entry, and the transport document carries the movement facts the file must prove. Separately, the lesson's Important Note governs the agent's code claim: the code names the regime the goods enter, while the rate comes from classification and valuation — three questions, three sources — and none of them is answered by omitting the file.

Answer 1 makes the code the entry's substance; the code selects the regime, and the regime's proof is the file. Answer 2 invents a restricted-goods-only document rule; section 14's file is general. Answer 4 is false on the face of the documentary system — the transport document is a core file element the officer checks against the arrival report.

The takeaway: the code selects the regime, the file proves the entry — and section 51 is what an incomplete proof engages.

=== ASSESSMENT Q7 | tests lesson 4.3 ===
QUESTION:
A client asks his agent whether goods admitted under a deferral procedure "avoid the duty entirely". Which option best states the accurate answer?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Yes — deferral procedures exempt the goods from duty permanently.
2. No — a deferral postpones the duty question on conditions; the duty is owed unless the conditions are performed (the goods leave, or the procedure's stated resolution happens), while a true exemption under section 2 removes the question entirely.
3. Yes, provided the goods are never sold.
4. No — deferral means the duty doubles when it eventually arises.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. The lesson's deferral-versus-exemption distinction is the module's most consequential discipline. An exemption — section 2's exempt goods specified in the Harmonised System, the scheduled persons and organisations, the removal article — removes the duty question: nothing arises. A deferral (temporary admission, warehousing, the processing regimes) postpones it: the duty would arise, but the procedure suspends the question because the goods' intended future makes collection premature — and the conditions are the promise that keeps it suspended.

Answer 1 commits the conflation the lesson dismantles: deferred is not forgiven, and the failed condition revives the question. Answer 3 adds a phantom condition — the sale is one way a deferral breaks, but the accurate answer is the structure: conditions performed, question stays postponed. Answer 4 invents a doubling that no provision carries; the revived question is the home-use duty, not a multiplied one.

The takeaway: the exemption removes the question; the deferral postpones it — and the learner who keeps them apart advises clients whose duty position is real.

=== ASSESSMENT Q8 | tests lesson 4.3 ===
QUESTION:
A company imports exhibition stands under temporary admission for a fortnight-long trade fair. After the fair, the company sells the stands in Ghana instead of re-exporting them. Which option best states the duty position of the sold stands?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The sale completes the admission procedure, and no duty follows.
2. The stands were exempt under section 2, so the sale is free of duty.
3. The admission's condition — the goods' departure — has failed for the sold stands: the deferred duty's question revives, and the stands' entry becomes home use with the duty paid, machine-for-machine as it were.
4. The duty position is unaffected, because the stands were never intended for the Ghanaian market.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 75 of Act 891 provides temporary admission: goods enter with the duty deferred because they will leave — the admission is temporary by definition, and the conditions exist to ensure the departure. Selling the stands into the Ghanaian market is the condition's failure: the goods have entered free circulation, which is the event the deferral postponed the duty against. The deferred question revives for the sold stands, and their position becomes the home-use entry with the duty paid.

Answer 1 mistakes the sale for the procedure's completion — the departure is the completion. Answer 2 claims a section 2 exemption that never existed for the stands; the admission was a postponement. Answer 4 argues from the original intention, but the deferral's condition tracks the goods' actual destiny — the intention did not keep the duty away; the departure would have.

The takeaway: the deferred duty is owed unless the deferral's condition is performed — machine by machine, stand by stand — and the breach is the entry into the market the admission was supposed to precede a departure from.

=== ASSESSMENT Q9 | tests lesson 4.3 ===
QUESTION:
A Ghanaian firm sends parts abroad for repair, and the repaired parts return. In a separate matter, the firm's exported goods are rejected by a foreign buyer and return untouched. Which option best states the returning provisions?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Both return under section 77, because the same firm owns both consignments.
2. Both return under section 78, because both goods left Ghana and came back.
3. The repaired parts return under section 78 — re-importation after outward processing, the goods having been transformed abroad (the Act's "manufacture": to make, produce, or cause to be made or produced, goods); the rejected goods return under section 77 — re-importation in the same state, having returned unchanged. The state at return picks the provision.
4. The repaired parts return under section 77 because repairs are not manufacture; the rejected goods need no provision at all.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. The lesson's discrimination test is the goods' state at return. The repaired parts were transformed abroad — the repair is the outward processing, and section 78 of Act 891 provides the returning regime for goods processed outside and returned; the section 151 definition of manufacture (to make, produce, or cause to be made or produced, goods) frames the transformation. The rejected goods returned unchanged — section 77's re-importation in the same state, the provision for goods that come back as they went.

Answer 1 collapses the distinction on ownership — the firm did not change; the goods' condition did. Answer 2 collapses it on the crossing — both crossed, but only one returned transformed. Answer 4's first half invents a repair carve-out the definitions do not carry, and its second half ignores that returning goods have provisions precisely because their duty question is real.

The takeaway: unchanged at return, section 77; transformed at return, section 78 — and the file proving the state is what makes either claim checkable.

=== ASSESSMENT Q10 | tests lesson 4.4 ===
QUESTION:
A trader asks where goods may lawfully be stored with the duty suspended for several months. Which option best states the place and the acts that create it?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Any rented store, provided the rent is paid.
2. A private bonded warehouse: a building or place appointed by the Commissioner-General and licensed under section 47's conditions (due licence, the keeper's deposited bond, prescribed fees) and section 79 — with the warehouse keeper defined in section 151 as the person licensed to operate it.
3. The importer's own premises, declared as such in the declaration.
4. A customs-controlled area, which is the same thing as a bonded warehouse.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 47(1) of Act 891 provides the licensing conditions — the building or place duly licensed by the Commissioner-General, the keeper's deposited bond, the prescribed fees — and section 79 provides the private bonded warehouse, which section 151 defines as a building or place appointed by the Commissioner-General and licensed to be a place where goods to be warehoused are secured, with the warehouse keeper as the person licensed to operate it. The two acts — the appointment of the place and the licence of the keeper — are what bring the suspense regime into existence for the goods.

Answer 1 dissolves the regime into ordinary storage; the machinery of ss.79-88 exists because the suspense needs a controlled, bonded, recorded place. Answer 3 invents a self-declaration route the Act does not carry. Answer 4 conflates the customs-controlled area (the perimeter of section 4 and section 5(1)) with the bonded regime — the area is where permitted activities happen; the warehouse is where goods are secured under suspense.

The takeaway: the suspense lives in a defined place created by defined acts — appointment, licence, bond — and the keeper's accountability is the regime's design.

=== ASSESSMENT Q11 | tests lesson 4.4 ===
QUESTION:
Goods under suspense in one bonded warehouse are sold to a Ghanaian buyer. Which option best states the exit from the suspense, and the records that account for it?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The goods are trucked to the buyer directly; the suspense transfers to the buyer's home.
2. The goods enter home use under section 88 — the entry of warehoused goods — with the duty paid; the keeper's records under section 80 account for the goods' entry, warehousing and exit, so the regime's account reconciles and the security's exposure for those goods ends.
3. The goods must be re-exported before they can be sold, because suspense goods can never enter home use.
4. The sale closes the suspense automatically, with no entry or duty.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 88 of Act 891 provides the entry of warehoused goods — the moment the suspense ends by the goods entering home use, with the duty the deferral deferred now paid. The keeper's records under section 80 are the regime's proof: the goods' entry into the warehouse, their stowing and position, and their exit are all accounted, so the security's exposure (the bond of section 47(1)(b) under the machinery of ss.108-111) ends for goods whose duty is settled.

Answer 1 moves goods under suspense outside the machinery — the unauthorised removal that section 83 names. Answer 3 reverses the regime's logic: the entry into home use is exactly how suspense goods can be sold — the exit exists for it. Answer 4 treats a private sale as the state's closure; the suspense ends through the provision, not through the invoice.

The takeaway: the goods' destiny picks the exit — home use pays (s.88), re-exportation moves (s.85 and s.89) — and the keeper's records prove which exit was taken.

=== ASSESSMENT Q12 | tests lesson 4.4 ===
QUESTION:
A trainee asks how to tell the suspense regime from transit when both defer duty. Which option best states the discriminator?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The regime is chosen by the goods' value: high-value goods transit, low-value goods are warehoused.
2. The regime is chosen by the goods' weight.
3. The discriminator is the movement: warehousing (ss.79-88) keeps goods secured in a place — the suspense sits still, and the exit is an entry into home use (s.88) or a re-exportation; transit (s.95) moves goods between places under a bond or bonded carrier — the suspense travels with them, and the procedure closes at the exit with acquittal. The provisions themselves signal it: records and stowings inside a place, versus bonds, carriers and routes between places.
4. There is no difference; the regimes are the same procedure under two names.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. The lesson's Important Note states the discriminator and its source in the provisions' own shape. The warehousing machinery of sections 79 to 88 is about a place: the keeper's records (s.80), the stowing (s.87), the entry of warehoused goods (s.88) — the suspense sits still, and the goods' exit is the entry into home use or the re-exportation. The transit machinery of section 95 is about movement between places: the bond or bonded carrier or both without duty payment, the TIR and ATA convention routes, the acquittal at the exit. Both defer duty; both hold goods under control; the movement is what picks the regime.

Answer 1 and Answer 2 invent value and weight tests that no provision carries — the regimes are chosen by the goods' destiny, not their metrics. Answer 4 dissolves a distinction the Act builds in separate sections with different machinery.

The takeaway: do the goods stay or move? — the question picks the regime, and the regime picks the provisions the goods live under.
