=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The two families of origin rules
BODY:
Section 70 of Act 891 opens the origin law with a distinction that organises the whole module. Subsection (1): preferential rules of origin are the rules used to determine the origin of goods for purposes of applying preferential tariff or preferential non-tariff measures. Subsection (2): non-preferential rules of origin are the rules used to determine the origin of goods for all other purposes, including the country-of-origin marking and for statistical purposes.

Two families, two purposes. The preferential family serves the trade agreements: a preference regime's rules decide which goods earn the agreement's tariff treatment. The non-preferential family serves everything else: the origin marking on the goods, the statistics the trade produces, and the measures that apply by origin without preference.

The learner's reading: the same goods can have two origins — the preferential origin a claim invokes, and the non-preferential origin the marking and the statistics state — and the two follow different rules. Which family applies is decided by the purpose the origin is asked for.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Wholly obtained: the first origin concept
BODY:
Section 70(3) provides the first origin concept: goods wholly obtained in a single country or territory, or wholly produced from domestic materials in the country or territory, are regarded as having their origin in that country or territory.

The Act's definitions carry the concept's words. "Produced" means grown, manufactured or mined — the three ways nature and labour yield wholly obtained goods. "Manufacture" means to make, produce, or cause to be made or produced, goods. "Raw material" means goods from which other goods are capable of being manufactured, and any residue from any process of manufacture.

Wholly obtained is the origin at its purest: the cocoa grown in Ghana, the shea nuts picked and processed in Ghana, the gold mined in Ghana — grown, manufactured or mined in one country, from that country's materials. The concept's simplicity is its test: any foreign input breaks the wholeness, and the goods move to the second concept — transformation.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Last substantial transformation: the second origin concept
BODY:
Section 70(4) provides the second concept: goods produced in more than one country or territory are deemed to originate in the country or territory where the goods underwent their last substantial transformation.

The concept answers the multi-country good: materials from several origins, made into one product — where is it from? The Act's answer is the last substantial transformation: the country where the goods last underwent the change that makes them what they are. The definition of "manufacture" — to make, produce, or cause to be made or produced — frames the transformation's nature; the "raw material" definition frames what went in.

What "substantial" means in detail — the change of classification, the value-add thresholds, the specific process rules — is the preference regimes' machinery, and this lesson states the concept without the thresholds: the thresholds belong to the instruments and their fact sheet. The learner's concept-level takeaway: multi-country goods originate where they last became substantially what they are.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Origin is the goods', not the trader's
BODY:
The origin concepts attach to the goods — grown, produced, transformed — and the learner must hold the attachment against a common confusion: the seller's or buyer's nationality.

A Ghanaian-owned company selling Chinese-made goods does not make the goods Ghanaian; a foreign-owned factory producing in Ghana produces Ghanaian-origin goods under the concepts. Section 70's provisions ask about the goods: wholly obtained where? Last substantially transformed where? The trader's nationality, the seller's address, the buyer's market — none of them is the origin's test.

The practical consequence is documentary: the origin claim's evidence is about the goods — their materials, their processing, their transformation — and the file proves the goods' story, not the trader's. The certificate of origin (section 70(6)'s provision) describes the goods and their origin criterion; it does not describe the trader's pedigree.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The proof power: origin is verified on request
BODY:
Section 70(5)-(6) provides the origin claim's verification machinery. Subsection (5): where a person indicates an origin of goods in a declaration, the Commissioner-General may require that person to prove the origin of the goods. Subsection (6): a person shall provide an invoice declaration of origin or, where applicable, a Certificate of Origin to the proper officer on request by the proper officer.

The two provisions are the claim's discipline: the origin indicated in the declaration is an assertion the declarant can be required to prove; and the proof's forms are named — the invoice declaration of origin, or the Certificate of Origin where the regime requires it. The proper officer's request is the machinery's trigger, and the declarant's obligation is to answer it.

The learner's practice: an origin claim is lodged with its evidence ready — the invoice declaration or the certificate — because the request can come, and the claim without its proof is an assertion the officer's request will find.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Raw materials and residues: the definitions' reach
BODY:
Section 151's "raw material" definition reaches further than its first clause: raw material means goods from which other goods are capable of being manufactured, and any residue from any process of manufacture. The residues are raw materials too — the offcuts, the by-products, the remains of the manufacturing process — capable of being further manufactured.

The definition's reach matters to origin's transformation concept: the process's residues carry their own origin question, and the definition brings them into the same analysis. A manufacturing process's waste that is itself manufactured into something else is raw material for that something else — and its origin story continues.

The learner's reading: the origin analysis follows the materials through their transformations, including the residues — and the definitions of "produced", "manufacture" and "raw material" are the vocabulary the whole analysis runs on. The preference regimes' rules use the same vocabulary with their own thresholds; the concepts here are the ground they build on.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Shea butter: wholly obtained, and a blend that is not
BODY:
Step 1. The pure case. A Ghanaian processor buys shea nuts picked in Ghana's northern regions, crushes and refines them into shea butter, and packs it for export. The nuts are grown in Ghana; the processing is in Ghana. Under section 70(3) — wholly obtained in a single country or wholly produced from domestic materials — the butter's origin is Ghana. The definitions say it: the nuts were produced (grown) in Ghana; the butter was manufactured (made) in Ghana.

Step 2. The blend. The processor's next product blends Ghanaian shea butter with imported coconut oil. The goods are produced in more than one country: section 70(4)'s concept applies — the origin is where the goods underwent their last substantial transformation.

Step 3. The question the blend raises. Did the blending in Ghana constitute the last substantial transformation? The concept says the analysis asks where the goods last became substantially what they are — and the answer's detail is the preference regime's rule, which this lesson states conceptually and the instruments carry in their thresholds.

Step 4. The two products' files. The wholly obtained butter's origin claim rests on its Ghanaian materials and Ghanaian manufacture; the blend's claim rests on its transformation analysis. The files differ because the concepts differ — and both files are ready for the proper officer's request under section 70(5)-(6).

What does this example demonstrate? Wholly obtained and last substantial transformation are two concepts for two goods — the pure and the blended — and the definitions of produced, manufacture and raw material are the vocabulary both run on.

Key lesson: the pure case is the simplest claim; the blend raises the transformation question — and both claims' files are prepared before the officer's request arrives.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A multinational product's transformation story
BODY:
Step 1. The goods. A European brand manufactures a smartwatch: the case is cast in one country, the display manufactured in another, the software developed in a third, and the final assembly and testing happen in a fourth. The watch is produced in more than one country.

Step 2. The concept applied. Section 70(4)'s rule: the watch originates where it underwent its last substantial transformation — the analysis asks where the goods last became substantially the smartwatch they are. The final assembly's country is the candidate; whether the assembly is "substantial" is the regime's rule's question.

Step 3. The marking and the preference distinguished. The watch's non-preferential origin — for its marking and the statistics — is stated under the non-preferential rules (section 70(2)); the watch's preferential origin — for any tariff preference a buyer's market grants — is determined under the preference regime's own rules (section 70(1)). The two answers can differ, and both are lawful.

Step 4. The learner's transfer. Ghana's import of such a watch reads the origin claim the same way: the declared origin is tested against the purpose the origin is asked for — and the proof machinery of section 70(5)-(6) is available to the officer either way.

What does this example demonstrate? The multi-country good's origin is its last substantial transformation — and the preferential and non-preferential families may state it differently, each for its own purpose.

Key lesson: the concepts travel; the thresholds are the regimes' — and the learner's analysis names which family's rules it is applying.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Origin is the goods' story, told by evidence
BODY:
The origin concepts attach to the goods: where they were grown, produced, mined, manufactured, transformed. The trader's nationality, the invoice's currency, the buyer's market — none is the origin's test, and a claim that rests on any of them is a claim about the wrong subject. The evidence follows: the goods' materials, their processing, their transformation — documented in the file the section 70(5)-(6) machinery can request. The learner's habit: an origin claim is stated about the goods, and its evidence is the goods' story — materials and transformations, each traceable to its document. The trader's story is commercial background; the goods' story is the origin — and the two stories live in different parts of the file, because the verification's questions are the goods' questions.

This lesson teaches the origin concepts — wholly obtained, last substantial transformation — and states the transformation's threshold question without answering it: what change counts as substantial, what percentage, what process. === MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A processor in Ghana imports coconut oil from abroad and blends it with locally grown shea butter to produce a body cream. Which option best states the origin concept that applies to the cream?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Section 70(3): the cream is wholly obtained in Ghana, because it was manufactured there.
2. Section 70(4): the cream was produced in more than one country, so it originates where it underwent its last substantial transformation — and the transformation's substance is the regime's rule's question.
3. The cream's origin is the coconut oil's origin, because the oil is the main ingredient.
4. The cream has no origin, because it is a mixture.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 70(4) of Act 891 provides that goods produced in more than one country or territory are deemed to originate in the country or territory where the goods underwent their last substantial transformation. The cream's inputs come from two origins — the imported coconut oil and the local shea butter — so the wholly-obtained concept of section 70(3) does not apply, and the transformation concept does. Whether Ghana's blending is the last substantial transformation is the regime's rule's question; the concept points the analysis there.

Answer 1 reads "manufactured in Ghana" as wholeness — but the imported coconut oil breaks section 70(3)'s wholeness, which is why the blend moved to the transformation concept. Answer 3 substitutes an ingredient-quantity intuition for the transformation's legal question. Answer 4 abandons the concepts: mixtures are exactly what section 70(4) provides for.

The takeaway: wholly obtained for the pure case; last substantial transformation for the multi-country case — and the blend's question is where the transformation happened, not what the main ingredient is.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
Which option best states the origin concepts' vocabulary as section 151 defines it?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Produced means imported; manufacture means packed; raw material means finished goods.
2. Produced means grown, manufactured or mined; manufacture means to make, produce, or cause to be made or produced, goods; raw material means goods from which other goods are capable of being manufactured and any residue from any process of manufacture.
3. Produced means assembled; manufacture means designed; raw material means packaging.
4. The terms are undefined in the Act and follow the preference regimes' own texts.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 151 of Act 891 defines the origin vocabulary in its own words: produced means grown, manufactured or mined — the three ways wholly obtained goods come to be; manufacture means to make, produce, or cause to be made or produced, goods — the transformation's act; raw material means goods from which other goods are capable of being manufactured, and any residue from any process of manufacture — the inputs, including the process's residues. The definitions are the vocabulary section 70's concepts run on: wholly obtained is produced or manufactured in one country; the transformation is the manufacture's change.

Answer 1 inverts every definition. Answer 3 substitutes trade-assembly meanings. Answer 4 abandons the definitions that section 70's concepts require — the Act defines the vocabulary, and the regimes build their thresholds on it.

The takeaway: grown, manufactured or mined; made or produced; inputs and residues — the definitions are the origin analysis's words, quoted and used exactly.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A foreign-owned factory in Ghana produces goods entirely from Ghanaian materials. A competing trader claims the goods "cannot be Ghanaian origin because the factory is foreign-owned". Which option best states the position under section 70's concepts?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The claim is correct: foreign ownership transfers the origin to the owner's country.
2. The claim is wrong: the origin concepts attach to the goods — section 70(3)'s wholly obtained and section 70(4)'s last substantial transformation ask where the goods were obtained, produced and transformed — and the trader's nationality is not the origin's test.
3. The claim is correct if the factory's manager is also foreign.
4. The claim is wrong because foreign-owned factories are exempt from origin rules.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 70's origin concepts attach to the goods: subsection (3) regards goods wholly obtained in a single country, or wholly produced from domestic materials, as originating there; subsection (4) deems multi-country goods to originate where they underwent their last substantial transformation. The questions are about the goods — their materials, their processing, their transformation — and the factory's ownership is not among the questions' elements. The foreign-owned factory producing from Ghanaian materials produces goods whose origin story is Ghanaian, under the concepts.

Answer 1 imports the owner's nationality as the origin — the confusion the lesson's block dismantles. Answer 3 extends the confusion to management. Answer 4 invents an exemption that no provision carries; the origin rules apply to the goods whoever owns the factory.

The takeaway: the origin is the goods' story — materials, processing, transformation — and the trader's pedigree is commercial background, not origin evidence.
