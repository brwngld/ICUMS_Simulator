=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The Ghana Standards Authority: mandate and history
BODY:
The standards deck tells the Authority's history in five dates, and the dates are the mandate's sources.

1967: established as the National Standards Board. 1973: reconstituted as the Ghana Standards Board by the Standards Decree, NRCD 173. 1975: made custodian of the Weights and Measures Decree, 1975 (NRCD 326) — legal metrology. 2011: renamed the Ghana Standards Authority. 2022: the Ghana Standards Authority Act, 2022 (Act 1078) consolidated NRCD 173 and NRCD 326 — re-establishing the Authority on a consolidated footing.

The deck describes the Authority as Ghana's national body for standards and a critical pillar of the national quality infrastructure, and its mandate at the ports and borders is the part of that work the customs learner meets: the standards requirements that imported goods must satisfy before they enter the market.

The subsidiary instruments the plan's legal sheet will characterise are visible in this history — NRCD 173 and NRCD 326 are the founding instruments the 2022 Act consolidated — and the labelling rule the next blocks teach is LI 1541:1992. The lesson names them as the decks name them; their content waits for the legal-anchors fact sheet.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The import requirements: conformity assessment and destination inspection
BODY:
The standards notes deck sets its teaching objectives in a list that is itself the requirements map: the essence of conformity assessment; destination inspection and import certification; the procedure for clearing imported goods with the GSA and its requirements; the labelling rule (LI 1541:1992); and prohibited items.

Conformity assessment is the requirement that the goods meet the standards their categories carry — evidenced before or at the border by certification. Destination inspection is the GSA's inspection of goods at Ghana's border — the assessment performed here, at the destination, rather than relying only on origin-side certificates. Import certification is the documentary layer: the certificates of conformity and analysis that evidence the goods' compliance.

The learner's reading: the requirements are about evidence. A standard is a claim about the goods; the conformity certificate, the analysis report and the inspection are the evidences that make the claim checkable at the border — the same evidentiary logic the whole documentary system runs on, applied to standards.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The certificate of conformity and the high-risk goods rule
BODY:
The notes deck's clearing procedure carries the documentary rule the border applies: imported goods on the red channel shall be accompanied by a certificate of conformity or analysis — and the provision's consequences are stated on the deck's own slide. Failure to produce a certificate of conformity results in a penalty for its absence; the consignment cannot be cleared until samples have been tested and found to fulfil the requirements of the standard; and the importer pays the costs associated with the testing.

For high-risk goods, the rule tightens: the deck lists them — electrical cables, CFL lamps, LPG cylinders, lubricants, fertilizers, steel coils, sheets and iron rods, and plastic granules — and for these, only certificates of conformity from the deck's named partners (BIVAC/Intertek) are accepted; otherwise the goods are inspected, sampled and tested at the border.

The learner's discipline: quote the deck's lists with the slide references. The high-risk list is the deck's; the partners are the deck's; and the rule's consequence for a missing certificate is the deck's — each claim traceable to its slide, and none extended from memory.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The labelling rules: LI 1541:1992
BODY:
The labelling requirement is the standards rule most visible on the shelf, and the notes deck quotes it from its source: the Ghana Standards Board General Labelling Rules, 1992 (L.I. 1541). For imported products, the deck lists the required label fields.

Name of the product. Instructions or directions for use, and warnings. Name and address of the manufacturer, producer or distributor. Country of origin. Date of manufacture. Batch or lot number.

The deck adds the language rule the border enforces: all information on the label in a foreign language other than English must be accompanied — the deck's slides carry the rule's full terms, and the learner quotes them with the slide reference.

The fields are the officer's checklist: a label missing its country of origin, or its batch number, is a label that fails the rule — and the inspection the goods' category receives will find it. The lesson states the fields verbatim from the deck; their statutory text waits for the legal-anchors fact sheet, which the plan's research list carries.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Pattern approval: weighing and measuring instruments
BODY:
The standards deck's final section covers weighing and measuring instruments — legal metrology, the 1975 custodianship's modern work — and the pattern-approval requirement their importation carries.

The deck's document list for the approval states the regime's shape: an application to the GSA; the Registrar-General's certificate of registration; the Ghana Revenue Authority certificate; the SSNIT certificate; the qualifications of technical staff; the installation and operation manual; and the OIML certificate of conformity. The instrument itself must display, on an affixed plate: the manufacturer's name and address, the manufacturer's mark, the model identification, the serial number, and the year of manufacture — with provisions for locking on failure during verification and availability for inspection at the manufacturer's premises.

The deck's compliance slide states the timing rule plainly: obtain the pattern approval before importing the instrument, and present the certificate at the port for clearance — otherwise the consequences follow, which the deck states and the learner cites without extending.

The regime is this lesson's worked example of a standards condition: a requirement that exists before the goods ship, evidenced by a certificate that must be at the port.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: How the GSA thread meets the clearance
BODY:
The GSA requirements meet the customs clearance at the file: the goods whose categories carry standards requirements need their conformity evidence in the declaration's supporting documents, where the officer's check reads it.

The mechanics mirror Module 5's attachments: the certificate of conformity or analysis enters the file as an electronic record; the officer reads the goods' description and classification, sees the standards category, and verifies the evidence against the goods. The red-channel selection (the deck's term) is the inspection route where the physical check and the sampling happen; the certificate route is where the documentary check confirms the compliance before the goods arrive.

The learner's integration: the customs clearance does not evaluate the standards — it verifies their evidence. The GSA's inspection and testing are the standards thread's own machinery; the customs file carries its output. Module 5's permit logic applies: the certificate evidences the allowance or compliance; it does not waive the customs machinery around it.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Imported cooking oil: labels, conformity and release
BODY:
Step 1. The goods and their categories. An importer brings in cooking oil bottled for the Ghanaian market. The product is food — the health regulator's orbit — and a standards-labelled product: the GSA thread's labelling rules apply to its bottles.

Step 2. The labels checked. Against LI 1541's fields as the deck quotes them: the name of the product, the instructions for use, the manufacturer's name and address, the country of origin, the date of manufacture, the batch or lot number. The importer verifies the labels before shipping, because a missing field is a border finding.

Step 3. The conformity evidence. The consignment arrives on the red channel: the certificate of conformity or analysis accompanies it. The officer reads the certificate against the goods — and had it been missing, the deck's consequences would follow: the penalty for its absence, the consignment held until samples were tested, the testing costs on the importer.

Step 4. The release chain. The customs declaration's file carries the conformity evidence beside the commercial documents; the health thread's registration evidence (Module 6.3's subject) sits beside it for the food regulator's requirements; the conditions of section 6(1) assemble; the goods are released.

What does this example demonstrate? The standards thread's evidence is in the file where the officer reads it — labels verified before shipping, certificates accompanying the consignment, and the release assembling every thread's condition.

Key lesson: the standards requirements are met before the goods ship; the border only verifies what the file proves.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A certificate of conformity issued at origin
BODY:
Step 1. The regime. A Ghanaian importer buys electrical cables from a foreign manufacturer. The cables are on the deck's high-risk list, so the conformity certificate must come from the deck's named partners — or the goods face inspection, sampling and testing at the border.

Step 2. The certificate at origin. Before shipment, the importer obtains the certificate of conformity from the named partner: the assessment of the cables against the applicable standard, performed at origin. The certificate travels with the consignment.

Step 3. The border. The consignment arrives on the red channel; the officer reads the certificate — the partner's name, the goods' identity, the standard assessed — and the certificate route clears the goods that the sampling route would have held.

Step 4. The counterfactual. Had the certificate come from a body the deck's list does not name, the rule's consequence applies: inspection, sampling, testing at the border, the costs on the importer, and the clearance held until the tests fulfil the standard.

What does this example demonstrate? The high-risk rule is evidentiary discipline: the allowance's certificate must come from the named source, and the border verifies the source before it trusts the paper.

Key lesson: the certificate's issuer matters as much as the certificate's content — the deck names the accepted partners, and the learner quotes the list, not a guess.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Two decks, one subject: the disagreement rule
BODY:
This lesson draws on two sources for the same subject — the GRA-course GSA deck and the GSA inspectorate's own notes — and where they differ, the discipline is to say so, not to pick silently. The example the notes deck gives: its objectives list includes the labelling rule LI 1541 and the prohibited items, while the course deck's sections run from mandate through pattern approval — overlapping, not identical. The learner's FACTS & SOURCES section states which deck each claim came from, with the slide; a claim both decks support is cited to both. The rule generalises: two sources for one subject is a strength when their agreement is shown and a flag when it is not — and the professional's file states which case it is.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Quote the requirement; never quote the penalty
BODY:
The decks' compliance slides state consequences — the deck's own slide says "comply to avoid delays and penalties", and the notes deck's clearing procedure names a penalty for a missing certificate of conformity. The spec's rule C4.4 applies with full force: the requirement and the mechanism are quoted; the amounts are not. The Act 891 front matter's unapplied amendments are one reason; the other is that deck-stated figures are teaching figures, not verified current amounts. The learner's file states: the certificate requirement (deck slide cited), the consequences' shape (clearance held, testing costs on the importer), and — where an amount matters to the client — the verified figure from the current source, with its citation. The requirement travels; the amount waits for its verification.

QUESTION:
A consignment of electrical cables arrives on the red channel with a certificate of conformity issued by a body that is not on the deck's accepted-partners list. Which option best states the position under the standards deck's rule?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The certificate is acceptable, because any conformity certificate proves the standard.
2. The certificate is unacceptable for high-risk goods: the deck's rule states that for the listed high-risk goods — electrical cables among them — only certificates from the named partners are accepted, and otherwise the goods are inspected, sampled and tested at the border.
3. The certificate is acceptable if the importer pays an additional fee.
4. The certificate's issuer is irrelevant; only the certificate's date matters.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. The notes deck's clearing procedure states the high-risk goods rule: for the listed goods — electrical cables, CFL lamps, LPG cylinders, lubricants, fertilizers, steel coils, sheets and iron rods, and plastic granules — only certificates of conformity from the deck's named partners (BIVAC/Intertek) are accepted; otherwise the goods are inspected, sampled and tested at the border, with the costs on the importer.

Answer 1 substitutes a generic certificate for the named-partner rule — the rule's whole point is whose assessment the border trusts for the high-risk categories. Answer 3 invents a fee-based cure the deck does not carry; the stated alternative is inspection, sampling and testing. Answer 4 dissolves the rule's issuer requirement into a date check — the rule names the partners because the partners' assessments are the ones the border accepts for those goods.

The takeaway: for the high-risk list, the certificate's source is the rule — the deck names the partners, the learner quotes the list, and the border's inspection is the stated alternative.
