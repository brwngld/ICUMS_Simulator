=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The FDA's mandate and functions, as the deck presents them
BODY:
The FDA deck states the Authority's legal basis in its own words: the Food and Drugs Authority draws its mandate from Parts 6, 7 and 8 of the Public Health Act, 2012 (Act 851). Its functions, the deck quotes from section 82 of the Act: to ensure adequate and effective standards for food, drugs, cosmetics, household chemicals and medical devices; to monitor, through the District Assemblies and any other agency of State, compliance with the provisions of that Part; to advise the Minister on measures for the protection of the health of consumers; to advise on the preparation of Regulations for the Part's implementation; to approve the initiation and conduct of clinical trials; and to perform any other functions ancillary to the Authority's objects.

The learner's reading: the mandate is statutory, the functions are quoted, and the categories are named in the statute's own words — food, drugs, cosmetics, household chemicals, medical devices. Module 6.1's generic "health regulator" becomes here the named authority with its quoted functions, and every claim in this lesson traces to the deck's slides or the Act's sections the deck quotes.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The Centre for Import and Export Control: the border mandates
BODY:
The border work belongs to the Authority's Centre for Import and Export Control (CIEC), and the deck grounds its mandates in the Act's own sections, quoted.

Section 97(1) of Act 851: a person shall not manufacture, import, export, distribute, sell or expose food for sale unless the Authority has registered the food. Section 118(1): a person shall not manufacture, prepare, import, export, distribute, sell, supply or exhibit for sale a drug, herbal medicinal product, cosmetic, medical device or household chemical substance unless the article has been registered by the Authority.

Two registration regimes, stated in the statute's own words: food registered before it is imported or sold; the drugs, cosmetics, devices and household-chemicals families registered before any of those acts. The learner's reading: the registration is the allowance's root — the product's registration number is what the border's evidence chain carries, because the statute's condition is registration, not goodwill.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Registration before import, and the licensed warehouses
BODY:
The deck's general requirements state the import-side conditions in its own words.

Registration first: FDA-regulated products to be imported, distributed or sold for local consumption must first be registered with the Authority, under Parts 7, sections 97 and 118 of Act 851. The registration precedes the shipment — it is the product's qualification for the market, obtained before the border is reached.

Licensed warehouses: warehouses for the storage of FDA-regulated products must be licensed by the Authority, in accordance with Part 7, sections 103 and 131(a) of Act 851 — and the deck notes the licensing is renewable annually. The storage condition is the regime's second leg: the registered product must also sit in licensed storage once it enters.

Detention as the response: all non-conforming products shall be detained for further regulatory actions — the deck's own line, and the enforcement frame the border's inspection feeds.

The learner's order of operations: register the product, license the storage, import with the permit, and hold the evidence in the file.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The eMDA: the permit in the system, as the deck names it
BODY:
The deck's application section states the import permit's process: importers shall be required to secure an electronic permit (eMDA) in the Integrated Customs Management System (ICUMS) for all import consignments prior to import — and all relevant import documents and information shall be uploaded into the ICUMS. The deck lists the information: the full name of the product including the brand name; the FDA product registration number in full; the name, address and relevant details of the manufacturer; the unit of the quantity; and the appropriate HS code for the product.

The lesson quotes the deck and stops where the deck stops: the eMDA is the permit's electronic shape, in the system the deck names, carrying the details the deck lists. The system's screens, the permit's format, and the process's steps beyond the deck's description belong to the systems fact sheet (ghana-electronic-systems.md, planned) and the agencies' own published processes — cited, not remembered.

The learner's file discipline: the eMDA's evidence is in the declaration's supporting file, where the officer's check reads it against the goods — Module 5's permit logic, with the FDA's own named instance.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Inspection and the release chain
BODY:
The FDA thread's inspection is the border's second control layer for regulated products: the goods' registration is checked, the permit is verified, and the products themselves are inspected for conformity — with the deck's detention rule as the non-conforming product's destination: all non-conforming products shall be detained for further regulatory actions.

The release chain assembles when the threads close: the declaration's file carries the registration evidence and the permit; the goods' inspection finds them conforming; and the customs release under section 6(1) follows — the conditions met, including the stated condition the FDA's regime contributes. A regulated product whose registration, permit and conformity are all evidenced is a clearance whose FDA thread closes cleanly.

The learner's diagnostic for a stalled regulated clearance: name the thread that is open — registration missing, permit unsecured, conformity questioned — because each thread's remedy lives with its own agency, and the customs file carries their evidence rather than their remedies.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The product categories, in the statute's own words
BODY:
The regulated categories are worth stating exactly, because the officer's check reads the goods into them. From the deck's quoted sections: food (section 97's regime); and drugs, herbal medicinal products, cosmetics, medical devices and household chemical substances (section 118's regime). The deck's mandate slide adds the section 82 functions' categories: food, drugs, cosmetics, household chemicals and medical devices.

The categories are the border's triage: a consignment's goods are read into these families, and each family's registration requirement attaches. A product outside the families follows its own regulator's statute; a product inside them carries the registration condition the deck's quoted sections state.

The learner's discipline: read the goods into the categories from the statute's own words — quoted from the deck with its slides — and never extend the families by guesswork. A product the families do not name is not thereby unregulated: it follows whatever statute governs it, and the institutions fact sheet will map the rest when written.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A pharmaceutical import, end to end
BODY:
Step 1. The registration. A Ghanaian pharmaceutical importer stocks a new drug product. Before any importation, the product is registered with the FDA — section 118(1) of Act 851's condition, quoted from the deck: the article must be registered by the Authority before it is imported, distributed, sold, supplied or exhibited for sale.

Step 2. The storage licence. The importer's warehouse is licensed by the Authority for the storage of FDA-regulated products — sections 103 and 131(a)'s condition — with the licence's annual renewal diarised.

Step 3. The permit. Before the consignment ships, the importer secures the electronic permit (eMDA) in ICUMS, as the deck instructs — uploading the product's full name and brand, the FDA registration number in full, the manufacturer's details, the unit quantities, and the HS code.

Step 4. The lodgement. The consignment arrives; the agent lodges the declaration with the file: the invoice, the transport document, the packing list, and the FDA evidence — the registration numbers and the permit. The officer reads the description into the regulated categories, and verifies the allowance's evidence against the goods.

Step 5. The conformity and release. The goods' inspection finds them conforming — the registered products, correctly labelled and stored as the regime requires. Section 6(1)'s conditions assemble, and the goods are released into the licensed warehouse's custody.

What does this example demonstrate? The regulated product's clearance is the registration, the storage licence, the permit and the customs machinery — four papers, one release.

Key lesson: the FDA thread closes before the border sees the goods; the border verifies what the registration and the permit already prove.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A food consignment refused abroad: the labelling lesson from abroad
BODY:
Step 1. The scene. At a foreign border, a food consignment is refused entry: the product labels lack the destination market's required declarations, and the refusal is documentary — the labels fail the rules before the goods are tasted.

Step 2. The read. The refusal is the labelling regime's check working: the labels are evidence of compliance, and their gaps are findings. The consignment's registration and safety were in order; the labels were the thread that failed.

Step 3. The correction's cost. The consignment returns for relabelling — days lost, carriage repeated, the buyer's timeline broken. The failure was checkable before shipment: a label review against the destination's rules would have found the gaps at the factory.

Step 4. The transfer. Ghana's labelling rules — LI 1541's fields the standards lesson quotes — are the same discipline at Ghana's border: labels checked against the required fields before the goods ship. The FDA thread and the standards thread both read the label; the consignment that prepared for both clears both.

What does this example demonstrate? The label is border evidence in every regime that requires it — and a label failure is a border failure, however safe the product.

Key lesson: the label review happens at the factory, because the border's label check is not a negotiating step.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
An importer brings in a new cosmetic product without registering it with the FDA, intending to register it "once sales begin". Which option best states the position under the deck's quoted provisions?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The position is lawful, because registration is required only when sales begin.
2. The position breaches section 118(1) of Act 851 as the deck quotes it: a person shall not import a cosmetic unless the article has been registered by the Authority — registration precedes the importation, the distribution, the sale and the exhibition for sale.
3. The position is lawful if the product is registered within a year.
4. The position is lawful because cosmetics are regulated by the Ghana Standards Authority instead.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. The FDA deck quotes section 118(1) of Act 851 in its own words: a person shall not manufacture, prepare, import, export, distribute, sell, supply or exhibit for sale a drug, herbal medicinal product, cosmetic, medical device or household chemical substance unless the article has been registered by the Authority. The registration condition precedes every act in the list — importation included — so the unregistered importation is the breach, whatever the registration's intended timing.

Answer 1 moves the condition to the sales stage; the statute's list starts at manufacture and includes importation, which happens first. Answer 3 invents a grace period the quoted provision does not carry. Answer 4 misassigns the regime: the deck's quoted provisions place cosmetics in the FDA's registration regime, and the standards thread's labelling rules are additional, not alternative.

The takeaway: the registration condition is in the statute's own words and precedes the importation — the deck's quotation is the border's rule, and the product's registration number is what the file carries.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
An FDA-regulated consignment arrives with its registration and eMDA in order, but the inspection finds the products non-conforming. Which option best states what the deck's rule provides?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The goods are released with a warning, because the registration was in order.
2. The goods are detained for further regulatory actions — the deck's rule for non-conforming products — and the customs release waits for the FDA thread's resolution, because section 6(1)'s stated conditions include the regime's evidence being in order.
3. The goods are automatically re-exported at the seller's cost.
4. The goods are released into the market with labelling corrected later.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. The deck's general requirements state the response in its own words: all non-conforming products shall be detained for further regulatory actions. The scenario's registration and eMDA satisfied the documentary thread — but the conformity thread is the inspection's subject, and its failure is the detention's trigger. The customs release under section 6(1) requires the stated conditions met; a detained regulated product's condition is unmet until the FDA thread resolves.

Answer 1 lets the documentary thread override the conformity finding — the inspection's purpose is to catch exactly this. Answer 3 invents an automatic re-exportation the deck does not state; the detention is "for further regulatory actions", whose content is the agency's machinery. Answer 4 defers the condition past release — the non-conformity is the release's blocker, not a market-stage correction.

The takeaway: the FDA thread's inspection is a real control — non-conforming products are detained under the deck's own rule, and the customs release waits for the thread the detention belongs to.
