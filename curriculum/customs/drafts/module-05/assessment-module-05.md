=== ASSESSMENT Q1 | tests lesson 5.1 ===
QUESTION:
A trader's colleague asserts: "An electronic lodgement is only a draft of the declaration — the legal declaration exists when the paper documents are stamped." Which option best evaluates the assertion under Act 891?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The assertion is correct: the Act recognises only stamped paper as declarations.
2. The assertion is correct for imports but not for exports.
3. The assertion is wrong: section 151 defines the electronic declaration as a declaration made electronically to the Commissioner-General, whether personally or on behalf of any person as agent — and the electronic record and electronic signature definitions give the lodgement its force; the electronic form is the declaration, not a draft of one.
4. The assertion is wrong, but only because the Commissioner-General has discretion to accept electronic declarations in special circumstances.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 151 of Act 891 defines the electronic declaration as a declaration made electronically to the Commissioner-General — whether personally or on behalf of any person as agent — of the particulars of a consignment of goods imported or exported. The electronic record definition (a record created, generated, sent, communicated, received, or stored by electronic means) makes the lodged data a record, and the electronic signature definition (the electronic method of authentication prescribed by the Commissioner-General) authenticates the maker. The three definitions together make the electronic lodgement the declaration itself.

Answer 1 invents a paper-stamping requirement the definitions replaced — the medium is defined into the law, not tolerated by it. Answer 2 invents a direction-of-trade split with no basis. Answer 4 demotes a defined medium to a discretionary tolerance — the definition makes the electronic form the lawful declaration, and the Commissioner-General's prescription governs the authentication method, not the form's existence.

The takeaway: the electronic declaration is the declaration — the definitions are its force, and no paper shadow is required to make it real.

=== ASSESSMENT Q2 | tests lesson 5.1 ===
QUESTION:
A learner asks what makes an electronically lodged declaration attributable to a specific maker. Which option best states the answer?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Nothing — electronic records are anonymous by nature.
2. The electronic signature: section 151 defines it as the electronic method of authentication prescribed by the Commissioner-General — the prescribed method identifies the maker of the record, and the electronic record definition preserves the lodgement as a record of the particulars.
3. The importer's physical presence at the customs office at lodgement time.
4. The shipping line's confirmation that the goods were loaded.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 151 of Act 891 defines the electronic signature as the electronic method of authentication prescribed by the Commissioner-General — the prescription is the design: every declaration in the environment is authenticated the same lawful way, and the authentication identifies the maker of the record. The electronic record definition — created, generated, sent, communicated, received, or stored by electronic means — preserves the lodgement with its particulars, and together the two definitions make the trail attributable: statement, maker, moment.

Answer 1 is the anonymity claim the electronic signature definition exists to refute. Answer 3 imports a physical-presence requirement that the electronic medium's whole purpose removes. Answer 4 confuses the transport document's movement facts with the declaration's authentication — the carrier confirms the goods' carriage, not the lodgement's maker.

The takeaway: the prescribed authentication is the attribution — the maker is identifiable, the moment is datable, and the audit's trail depends on both.

=== ASSESSMENT Q3 | tests lesson 5.1 ===
QUESTION:
An agent lodges declarations electronically for a client under a written authority the client granted two years ago for "one consignment of tiles". The current lodgement is for a consignment of tyres. Which option best states the position under section 44?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The lodgement is valid: the licence covers all customs business, so the scope of the authority is irrelevant.
2. The lodgement is invalid: the written authority's scope was "one consignment of tiles", and section 44 requires the agent to produce the authority for the specific act — an authority whose scope does not cover this consignment does not authorise this lodgement, whatever the medium.
3. The lodgement is valid because electronic declarations do not require written authority.
4. The lodgement is valid if the client would have consented.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 44 of Act 891 requires the agent to produce authority — the principal's written authorisation for the act performed on the principal's behalf — and the electronic declaration's definition in section 151 carries the duty into the environment ("on behalf of any person as agent"). The authority's scope in the scenario covers one consignment of tiles; the current lodgement is tyres. The licence authorises the profession; the authority authorises the engagement — and this engagement is outside the paper's scope.

Answer 1 makes the licence answer the engagement question — the exact merge the two papers exist to prevent. Answer 3 invents a medium-based exemption: the electronic declaration's definition carries the agency duty into the environment, it does not retire it. Answer 4 substitutes hypothetical consent for the written paper the section requires.

The takeaway: the environment carries section 44 intact — the authority's scope must cover the lodgement, and the paper must be producible on demand, whatever the medium.

=== ASSESSMENT Q4 | tests lesson 5.2 ===
QUESTION:
A declaration's consignment reference does not match any arrival report in the system. Which option best states why the system refuses to proceed, and what the refusal protects?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The refusal is bureaucratic; the reference can be reconciled after release.
2. The refusal enforces the linkage rule: the declaration must link to the consignment the border actually received (reported under section 17) — without the link, the declaration describes goods nobody reported, and the assessment and release would flow for phantom goods.
3. The refusal enforces the false-statement rule only; a wrong reference is not material.
4. The refusal is an error; the goods' description identifies the consignment sufficiently.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. The lesson's linkage rule is the substance behind the unique consignment reference: the declaration must be linked to the consignment the border actually received — the goods a conveyance reported on arrival under section 17 of Act 891. A reference matching no report describes goods nobody brought in, and the refusal prevents the declaration's consequences — assessment, payment, release — from flowing for goods that never arrived.

Answer 1 treats a protection as bureaucracy: the linkage is what stops phantom clearances, and its enforcement is cheapest at lodgement. Answer 3 narrows the rule to false statements; the linkage is a prior question — the declaration's target must exist before the particulars' truth even arises. Answer 4 dissolves the thread on the strength of descriptions; the description is the assertion, and the reference is what ties the assertion to reported goods.

The takeaway: the linkage refusal protects the system's first ordering — report brings the goods in, declaration claims goods from the report — and nothing downstream should flow ahead of it.

=== ASSESSMENT Q5 | tests lesson 5.2 ===
QUESTION:
An agent lodges a declaration prepared from the client's email summary rather than from the source documents, and the email's quantities were wrong. Which option best states the agent's position?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The agent bears no responsibility, because the client supplied the figures.
2. The agent's position is the lesson's verification failure: the lodged particulars are the declarant's statements under the false-statement definition — verified against the supporting file before lodgement — and the electronic record preserves the wrong figures with the agent's authentication for the audit trail.
3. The agent's responsibility attaches only to the value, not to quantities.
4. The agent's responsibility is removed because the environment validated the lodgement.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 151 of Act 891 defines a false statement as a statement made with respect to goods subject to customs control that is false or misleading in a material respect, or a material omission from a required statement — and the lodged particulars are those statements, whoever supplied the raw figures. The electronic record definition preserves the lodgement, and the electronic signature authenticates the agent as the maker. The preparation rule — verification before lodgement — exists because the source documents, not an email summary, are what the particulars must be verified against.

Answer 1 forwards the client's figures as the agent's defence; the authority under section 44 authorises the lodgement, not the accuracy of unverified particulars. Answer 3 narrows the statements to value; the definition reaches every material particular — description, quantity, origin, procedure. Answer 4 mistakes the environment's validation for a verification substitute — the wired check catches what it is built to catch, and the professional's verification is the discipline the trail records.

The takeaway: the trail preserves the agent's statements with the agent's authentication — the verification before lodgement is the agent's own protection, exercised at the source documents.

=== ASSESSMENT Q6 | tests lesson 5.2 ===
QUESTION:
A trainee describes the pre-entry as "a draft declaration saved in the system before the goods arrive". Which option best corrects the trainee?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The trainee is right: the pre-entry has no legal effect until the goods arrive.
2. The trainee is wrong: section 151 defines the pre-entry as the entry of goods prior to their importation into Ghana — a full entry made early, whose particulars take legal effect within the entry machinery; the lesson's preparation rule (verification before lodgement) applies to it exactly as to any declaration.
3. The trainee is wrong because pre-entries are prohibited under the Act.
4. The trainee is right, provided the pre-entry omits the value.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 151 of Act 891 defines the pre-entry as the entry of goods prior to their importation into Ghana, and section 50 provides the machinery. The pre-entry is a full entry made early — its particulars take legal effect — which is why the lesson's preparation rule applies without modification: the consignment fixed, the particulars prepared from the source documents, the verification chain completed, and only then the lodgement. The draft framing would defeat the pre-entry's whole purpose: the speed it buys exists because the statement was made and checked before the border saw the goods.

Answer 1 accepts the draft framing, which contradicts the definition — an entry is an entry, whenever it is made. Answer 3 asserts a prohibition the Act's own provision refutes. Answer 4 compounds the error with an omission the false-statement definition reaches — a material omission from a required statement.

The takeaway: the pre-entry is an entry made early, not a draft saved late-coming — and its particulars carry the same weight, verified the same way.

=== ASSESSMENT Q7 | tests lesson 5.3 ===
QUESTION:
A client holding an agency permit for restricted goods asks his agent whether the permit means the goods "skip customs". Which option best states the accurate answer?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Yes — the permit replaces the clearance for restricted goods.
2. Yes, provided the permit is attached to the declaration.
3. No — the permit evidences the allowance the restriction's condition requires; it satisfies one of section 6(1)'s stated conditions, while the rest of the clearance runs as it would for any goods: declared, examined or exempted under the risk-based control, and released when the conditions are met.
4. No — the permit means the goods are prohibited and cannot be cleared at all.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. The lesson's permit block states the documentary shape: restricted goods — defined in section 151 as goods whose importation or exportation is restricted by law — move under the condition the restriction imposes, and the permit is the evidence that the condition is satisfied. Section 6(1)'s release conditions are conjunctive — examined or exempted, no offence found, required documents acquired, stated conditions met — and the permit satisfies the stated-condition element for the restriction. The rest of the clearance is unaffected: the declaration is lodged, the control decision is made, the release follows the conditions.

Answer 1 turns the allowance into a waiver of the machinery — the goods remain under the clearance's full process. Answer 2 treats attachment as the whole requirement: the permit is attached and it evidences one condition, not all of them. Answer 4 confuses restriction with prohibition — prohibited goods are a different defined state (section 151), and the permit's existence proves the goods are in the restricted category, where an allowance is possible.

The takeaway: the permit is the allowance's evidence, not a control's waiver — the restricted good's clearance runs the whole machinery with the permit in the file.

=== ASSESSMENT Q8 | tests lesson 5.3 ===
QUESTION:
A manifest lists the vessel's bunker fuel among the cargo entries for its next voyage. Which option best states the position under section 151?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The entry is correct: fuel on a vessel is always cargo.
2. The entry is a miscategory: cargo is defined as goods other than stores or passenger or crew baggage — the bunker fuel is a store, the craft's own supply, which section 24's regime governs; the manifest's cargo entries should carry only the consigned goods the declarations claim.
3. The entry is correct if the fuel was purchased abroad.
4. The entry is a miscategory because fuel is prohibited on vessels.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 151 of Act 891 defines cargo as goods other than stores or passenger or crew baggage — a definition that teaches by exclusion. The vessel's bunker fuel is a store: the craft's own supply, governed by the aircraft and ship stores regime of section 24, not by the cargo declarations. The manifest chain's cargo entries feed the declarations — a stores item listed as cargo would present goods for declaration that the procedures do not declare, and the reconciliation the chain performs assumes the categories are right.

Answer 1 ignores the carve-out that is the definition's operative part. Answer 3 invents a purchase-origin test; the fuel's character as the craft's own supply is what makes it a store, wherever it was bought. Answer 4 invents a prohibition that does not exist — stores are lawful, under their own provisions.

The takeaway: cargo, stores, baggage — three categories with three regimes — and the manifest that keeps them straight is the manifest whose declarations reconcile.

QUESTION:
A lodgement is refused because the certificate its goods' procedure requires is missing from the attachments. Which option best states what the refusal means and the professional response?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The refusal means the goods are prohibited; they must be re-exported.
2. The refusal means the environment is faulty; the lodgement should be retried unchanged.
3. The refusal is section 51's missing-documents provision wired into the environment — the attachment requirement is checkable at lodgement — and the professional response is to obtain the certificate from the issuing body, attach it as an electronic record, and re-lodge: the correction is cheapest at lodgement, before the declaration's consequences flow.
4. The refusal means the declaration must be abandoned and re-created from scratch.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 51 of Act 891 provides for missing or incomplete documents, and the lesson's worked example shows the wired check: the goods' procedure demands the certificate, the declaration's particulars indicate it, the file lacks it — and the lodgement is not accepted into the process as complete. The electronic environment makes the attachment requirement checkable at lodgement, which is where the correction is cheapest: obtain the certificate, attach it as an authenticated electronic record, re-lodge.

Answer 1 confuses an evidentiary gap with a prohibition — the goods are restricted or conditioned, not prohibited, and the certificate's absence is a file failure, not a goods fate. Answer 2 retries without correcting — the same missing attachment produces the same refusal, because the check is the provision working. Answer 4 invents an abandonment requirement; the lodgement is corrected and resubmitted, not destroyed.

The takeaway: the wired check is section 51 at work — the professional arrives with the file complete, and when the check catches a gap, the correction is obtained and attached before the consequences flow.

=== ASSESSMENT Q10 | tests lesson 5.4 ===
QUESTION:
A colleague reads a declaration's screen status "validated" and tells the client: "Your goods are cleared." Which option best evaluates the colleague's statement?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The statement is correct: validated means cleared.
2. The statement is correct if the status is displayed in green.
3. The statement conflates two states: "validated" is the particulars' completeness and consistency check having passed — one condition in the lifecycle — while release under section 6(1) is a later state requiring examination or exemption, no offence found, the documents acquired, and the stated conditions met, with the duty settled. Statuses are legal conditions read against their provisions, not conclusions.
4. The statement is correct because validation happens after release.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. The lesson's lifecycle teaches the states as legal conditions in the provisions' order: lodged (section 49's declaration in effect), validated (the particulars' check serving section 14 and section 51), assessed (sections 71 and 72's computation), paid (section 73's settlement), released (section 6(1)'s conditions met). The colleague's statement jumps from the second state to the last — release's conditions include the duty settled and the stated conditions met, none of which validation asserts.

Answer 1 accepts the conflation; the states are distinct because the provisions they express are distinct. Answer 2 imports a display detail into a legal conclusion — the lesson's Important Note warns exactly against reading the label's presentation as the condition. Answer 4 inverts the order: validation precedes release in the provisions' sequence.

The takeaway: statuses are legal conditions read against their provisions — the professional checks the condition, not the label, before telling a client what their clearance has achieved.

The correct answer is Answer 1. === ASSESSMENT Q12 | tests lesson 5.4 ===
QUESTION:
Three years after a clearance, the Authority opens a post-clearance audit. The trader's agent had verified all particulars before lodgement and the file was retained. Which option best states what the audit will find and why the position is prepared?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The audit will find nothing to read, because three years have passed and records expire on release.
2. The audit will read the electronic trail — the lodged particulars, the validations, the attachments, the assessment and the payment, preserved as electronic records under section 151 — and the trader's records kept under the section 9 duty; the retained file matches the trail, which is exactly the prepared position the lesson's verification discipline builds.
3. The audit will re-examine the goods physically, which have long left the country.
4. The audit applies only to traders who have been previously convicted.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 7 of Act 891 provides the post-clearance audit — the mechanism that revisits a clearance after release — and the trail it reads is complete: the lodged declaration and its particulars, the validations, the attachments, the assessment and the payment, preserved as electronic records within section 151's definition (records created, generated, sent, received or stored by electronic means). The trader's side is section 9's record-keeping duty — the source documents, correspondence and accounts kept for the retention period the provision states. The lesson's verification discipline is what makes the position prepared: the lodged particulars were verified against the file, so the trail and the file agree.

Answer 1 invents an expiry that the audit's window denies — section 9's retention period is exactly what keeps the records available for it. Answer 3 mistakes the audit for a physical re-examination; the audit reads records, not released goods. Answer 4 invents a conviction precondition that section 7 does not carry.

The takeaway: the lifecycle's trail is the audit's reading material — the environment preserves it, the trader's duty keeps the trader's side, and the verification discipline is what makes the two match.
