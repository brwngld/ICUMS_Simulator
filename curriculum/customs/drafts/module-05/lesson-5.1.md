=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: What makes a declaration electronic
BODY:
Module 4 introduced the declaration and the pre-entry; this module opens with the medium, because the modern declaration's home is the electronic environment. Section 151 defines the "electronic declaration" as a declaration made electronically to the Commissioner-General, whether personally or on behalf of any person as agent, of the particulars of a consignment of goods imported or exported — or any other declaration the Commissioner-General authorises in connection with the import or export of goods under the Act.

Three features of the definition carry the module. The addressee is fixed: the declaration is made to the Commissioner-General — the officer whose name the Act's powers carry. The agency route is built in: "on behalf of any person as agent" is inside the definition itself, so the profession's representational work is lawfully electronic. And the scope is the consignment's particulars — the same particulars the statutory form has always carried, lodged through a different medium.

The lesson this definition teaches is bigger than its words: the electronic declaration is the declaration. Its particulars are the goods' legal statements, its lodgement starts the same provisions, and its consequences are the same as any paper form's. The medium changed; the law did not.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The electronic record and the electronic signature
BODY:
Two definitions give the electronic environment its legal force.

An "electronic record" is a record created, generated, sent, communicated, received, or stored by electronic means. The definition's verbs cover the whole life of the document: made, sent, received, kept — all electronically, and all equally a record. The paper-era question "is this a real document?" is answered by the definition: a record's medium does not affect its status, because the Act defines the electronic form in.

An "electronic signature" is the electronic method of authentication prescribed by the Commissioner-General. The definition delegates the method to the Commissioner-General's prescription — and the learner should read that delegation as design: the authentication method is prescribed centrally so that every declaration in the environment is authenticated the same lawful way.

Together the two definitions do what the wet-ink world did with paper and signature: they make an electronically lodged, electronically authenticated declaration a legal act. The particulars are statements; the authentication identifies the maker; and the record persists. Nothing in this module would be lawful without these two definitions — they are the foundation the environment is built on.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The roles the law fixes
BODY:
The electronic environment does not create its roles; it inherits them from the Act's definitions, and the learner already knows them from Module 1.

The declarant — the owner of the goods, the importer, the exporter, or the agent — is the person whose statement the declaration is. The agent performs customs business on behalf of another, under the licence of section 43 and the written authority of section 44. The proper officer is the officer whose right or duty it is to perform the act referred to — the examination, the release decision, the demand for documents. The Commissioner-General is the official whose name the Act's powers invoke: the designation of areas, the release of goods, the prescription of the electronic signature's method.

What the electronic environment adds is speed, not persons. The declarant lodges; the agent represents; the proper officer decides; the Commissioner-General's powers operate — each role doing electronically what the Act assigned it in text. The learner's discipline is to keep the roles distinct in every scenario: an agent's lodgement is not the proper officer's decision, and a system's automated response is not an officer's determination unless a provision makes it one. The definitions, not the software, decide who acted.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The agent's authority, carried into the environment
BODY:
Section 44's written-authority requirement does not retire when the declaration goes electronic — it carries into the environment, because the electronic declaration's definition expressly includes agency lodgement ("on behalf of any person as agent").

The carried duty has three parts. The agent must hold the licence (section 43) — the authority to perform customs business at all. The agent must hold the principal's written authority for the engagement — the paper section 44 requires the agent to produce on demand, which is as producible when the declaration is electronic as when it was paper. And the agent's lodgement must stay inside the authority's scope — the particulars lodged are the principal's instructions executed, not the agent's inventions.

The environment changes the speed of all three, not their existence. The licence is verified centrally; the authority document is kept with the file and producible; the scope is the agent's own discipline. What the environment cannot do is replace any of them: a lodgement by an unlicensed person, or outside the authority's scope, is an act the Act does not recognise — however smooth the software made it.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Access is competence, by definition
BODY:
The learner's question in an electronic environment is "who may do what?" — and the Act's answer is the same as the paper era's: competence follows the definitions.

The declarant may lodge because the declarant is one of the four lawful identities. The agent may lodge for another because the licence and the authority say so. The proper officer may examine, demand and release because the provisions assign those acts to the proper officer's competence. The Commissioner-General may release, designate, prescribe — because the provisions invoke that office.

The environment's access rules are the administrative expression of those competences: the system's accounts, permissions and workflows exist so that each role can do what the law assigns and cannot do what the law assigns to someone else. The learner should therefore read every access question backward into the Act: not "which account may press this?" but "which role does the law assign this act to?" — because the software's permissions are lawful only insofar as they express the Act's assignments.

That is the module's key to everything that follows: the environment is the Act's provisions, wired.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Navigation as rules: what the environment requires, and why
BODY:
The last block states the module's boundary positively, because it is a teaching point and not just a limit.

Navigation in the electronic environment — how a user moves through the system's steps — exists because the Act's provisions have an order: the goods arrive before they are declared; the declaration's particulars are checked before they are assessed; the duty is settled before release. The environment's steps are those provisions, sequenced. The learner who knows the provisions' order can navigate any system built on them, because the steps cannot run in an order the provisions do not allow.

So this module teaches navigation as rules: what must be true before the goods can move to the next state — the consignment identified, the particulars lodged, the documents attached, the duty settled, the release conditions met. The screens that express those rules belong to the practical track and to the systems fact sheet when written; the rules themselves belong here, and they are stable knowledge no interface change disturbs.

The learner who finishes this module can reason about any electronic customs system from the provisions alone — which is the point.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A declaration lodged for a client, end to end in law
BODY:
Step 1. The authority. A licensed agent receives an importer's instruction to clear a consignment of tiles. Before anything electronic happens, the file gains the paper the Act requires: the written authority under section 44 — the principal's instruction for this engagement — kept with the file and producible on demand.

Step 2. The identity. The agent logs into the environment under the firm's registered account — the access rules exist because the law assigns lodgement to the declarant's lawful identities, and the agent lodges as the fourth identity: the agent acting for the principal.

Step 3. The lodgement. The agent lodges the declaration electronically — the medium section 151 defines — of the consignment's particulars. The electronic record definition makes the lodged data a record; the electronic signature — the authentication method the Commissioner-General prescribes — identifies the maker.

Step 4. The statement's weight. The particulars lodged are the agent's statements about the goods — the false-statement definition reaches them as it reaches any paper declaration. The agent has verified them against the invoice and packing list before lodging, because the environment's speed does not dilute the statement's weight.

Step 5. The officer's side. The proper officer's environment shows the lodged declaration for the control decision; the officer's acts — demand, examination, release — are the provisions' acts, performed in the medium the environment provides.

What does this example demonstrate? The electronic lodgement is the paper lodgement's twin: same definitions, same authority, same statement weight — wired.

Key lesson: the environment changes the speed and the medium; the Act still decides who may act and what the acts mean.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: The same architecture at a foreign single window
BODY:
Step 1. The scene. At a national single window abroad, an importer's broker lodges a declaration through the government's electronic platform: one account, one authentication, one lodgement to the customs authority.

Step 2. The recognisable definitions. The broker lodges as an agent for a principal; the platform authenticates the lodgement; the lodged particulars become the legal statement; the customs authority's officers decide control and release. Every element matches Ghana's definitions — the electronic declaration, the electronic record, the electronic signature, the agent, the proper officer — under that jurisdiction's own statute.

Step 3. The architecture's universality. The single window's steps run in the provisions' order: goods reported, declaration lodged, documents attached, duty assessed and settled, release granted. No step runs before its provision allows, because the environment is the statute sequenced.

Step 4. The transfer. A learner who has learned the rules from Act 891 can read the foreign platform's flow without training on the software: the states and their order are the provisions' order, and the provisions' order is what this module teaches.

What does this example demonstrate? Electronic customs environments worldwide are their statutes, wired — and the wired statute is learnable from the definitions and the provisions' order.

Key lesson: learn the rules here, and any system's screens become translations of knowledge you already hold.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Electronic is not informal
BODY:
The environment's speed can suggest informality, and the learner must resist the suggestion. The electronic declaration's particulars are statements under the false-statement definition — false or misleading in a material respect, or a material omission, with respect to goods subject to customs control. The electronic record persists: the lodgement, its particulars, its authentication and its attachments are records the audit provisions can read years later. The agent's lodgement carries the same verification duty the paper era imposed, at higher speed; the officer's decisions carry the same accountability. Electronic is the medium's name, not a weakening of the law's demands — and the professional who treats the environment's speed as a licence for unverified particulars has misunderstood the definitions this module is built on.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A trader asks whether a declaration lodged electronically through a licensed agent is "a real legal declaration, or just a data entry". Which option best answers the question under Act 891?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. It is just data entry until the Commissioner-General signs it personally.
2. It is a real legal declaration: section 151 defines the electronic declaration as a declaration made electronically to the Commissioner-General, whether personally or on behalf of any person as agent — and the electronic record and electronic signature definitions give the lodgement its legal force.
3. It is a legal declaration only if a paper copy is also filed afterwards.
4. It is a real declaration, but the agent's licence becomes irrelevant because the system verifies everything.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 151 of Act 891 defines the electronic declaration as a declaration made electronically to the Commissioner-General, whether personally or on behalf of any person as agent, of the particulars of a consignment of goods imported or exported. The electronic record definition — a record created, generated, sent, communicated, received, or stored by electronic means — makes the lodged data a record, and the electronic signature definition — the electronic method of authentication prescribed by the Commissioner-General — authenticates the maker. Together the three definitions make the electronic lodgement the declaration, with full legal force.

Answer 1 invents a personal-signature requirement that the definition's delegation to the prescribed authentication method replaces. Answer 3 demands a paper shadow the Act does not require — the medium is defined in, not tolerated. Answer 4 is the dangerous half-truth: the system's checks do not retire the agent's licence or the written authority of section 44, which the electronic declaration's own definition carries ("on behalf of any person as agent").

The takeaway: the medium is defined into the law — the electronic declaration is the declaration, and the definitions, not the software, are what make it real.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
In an electronic environment, a lodged declaration is shown to an officer for a control decision, and the officer exempts the goods from examination. Which option best identifies the roles performing each act?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The system performed both acts, because the software presented and processed them.
2. The agent performed both acts, because the agent lodged the declaration.
3. The declarant-agent lodged the declaration as the lawful fourth identity; the proper officer made the control decision — competence follows the Act's definitions (declarant, agent, proper officer, Commissioner-General), and the environment expresses those assignments.
4. The importer performed both acts personally, because the goods are the importer's.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. The lesson's roles block carries the answer: the declarant — the owner, importer, exporter, or the agent — is the person whose statement the declaration is; the agent performing customs business on behalf of another is the lawful lodger under section 43's licence and section 44's authority; and the proper officer — defined as the officer whose right or duty it is to perform the act referred to — is the competence behind the control decision. The electronic environment expresses those assignments: lodgement by the lawful lodger, decisions by the assigned officer.

Answer 1 assigns the acts to the software; the environment is the medium, and the legal acts belong to the persons the definitions name. Answer 2 assigns the officer's decision to the agent — the collapse of roles the module's discipline prevents. Answer 4 collapses the agent's representational lodgement into the importer's personal act, which the electronic declaration's own definition distinguishes ("on behalf of any person as agent").

The takeaway: the environment wires the Act's assignments — it does not replace them; who acted is always answered by the definitions, never by the software's flow.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
An agent lodges declarations for a client under the firm's account. The client never gave written authority for this particular consignment, although the agent has cleared for the client before. Which option best states the position under Act 891?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The lodgement is valid, because prior engagements imply standing authority for all future consignments.
2. The lodgement is valid, because the electronic environment verifies the agent's licence automatically.
3. The lodgement lacks the authority section 44 requires: the written authority for the specific engagement must exist and be producible on demand — the environment carries the duty into electronic lodgement, and the electronic declaration's own definition includes agency lodgement "on behalf of any person as agent".
4. The lodgement is valid if the client verbally approves it by telephone afterwards.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 44 of Act 891 requires the agent to produce authority — the principal's written authorisation for the act performed on the principal's behalf — and the electronic declaration's definition in section 151 expressly includes lodgement "on behalf of any person as agent", which carries the duty into the environment. Prior engagements do not create a standing authority the section does not provide; each engagement's authority is the paper the officer's demand is answered with.

Answer 1 invents an implied standing authority that the section's written-authority requirement does not recognise — the paper must exist, not be presumed. Answer 2 substitutes the system's licence verification for the engagement's authority; the licence authorises the profession, the authority authorises the engagement, and the module keeps the two papers separate. Answer 4 invents a verbal cure for a written-authority requirement.

The takeaway: the environment carries section 44's duty intact — the written authority for the engagement exists before the electronic lodgement, or the lodgement is an act the Act does not recognise.
