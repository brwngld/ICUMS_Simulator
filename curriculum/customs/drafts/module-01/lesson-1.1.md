=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: What customs is, and what the law imposes
BODY:
Under section 1(1) of the Customs Act, 2015 (Act 891), duties and taxes are imposed on prescribed goods imported into the country or exported from it, at the rate of duty and tax specified in the Harmonised Commodity Description and Coding System — the Harmonised System. Subsection (2)(a) adds that the imposed duties and taxes may include any other tax, duty or levy on goods prescribed under any other enactment. That single provision is the revenue mandate: it is the legal reason a consignment pays duty at Tema or Kotoka.

Customs is the branch of government that administers those provisions. Its work is the control of what crosses the national border: collecting the duty and tax the Act imposes, examining and releasing consignments against declared particulars, stopping what the law prohibits or restricts, and keeping records that allow a clearance to be revisited after the goods have gone.

The Act also gives the work a name. "Customs business" is defined in section 151 as an activity that has to do with the importation or exportation of goods — so the term covers the whole operational field this programme teaches, from the arrival report to the release of the goods.

What customs may do at the border, then, is exactly what sections 1 to 152 of Act 891 and the Regulations made under it authorise. Everything in this programme traces back to those provisions.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The functions of customs, derived from the Act
BODY:
Teaching materials often list customs functions as a neat set of labels. This lesson derives each function from the provisions that create it, so every claim can be traced.

1. Revenue collection. Section 1(1) imposes the duties and taxes; sections 71 to 73 make the importer or exporter liable, fix how the duty is calculated, and provide for payment.

2. Facilitation of legitimate trade. Section 6(1) directs that the Commissioner-General may release declared goods as soon as they have been examined or exempted from examination — release is the clearance goal, and the provision is written to allow it promptly.

3. Enforcement and control. Section 37 gives the prohibited and restricted goods powers; section 99 prohibits imports the law forbids; sections 112 to 120 give officers the powers they need at the border, from search of premises to boarding a conveyance.

4. Protection of society. The same provisions keep dangerous and prohibited goods out: section 99 operates for safety and public health, and the agencies' controls (foods, drugs, standards) sit inside the clearance.

5. Record and accountability duties. Section 9 requires records to be kept; section 7 provides the post-clearance audit that revisits a clearance after release.

The takeaway: the functions are not a memory list. Each one names the section of Act 891 that gives it force.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The persons the Act empowers
BODY:
Section 151 of Act 891, the Interpretation section, fixes the legal cast of a clearance, and the definitions matter because duties attach to the named person.

"Authority" means the Ghana Revenue Authority established under the Ghana Revenue Authority Act, 2009 (Act 791) — the institution that owns the customs function. "Commissioner-General" means the person appointed under section 13 of that same Act 791 — the official whose name the Act's powers invoke: designating customs-controlled areas, releasing goods, demanding documents.

"Officer" means a person employed by the Ghana Revenue Authority and assigned to perform customs duties — the working staff. "Proper officer" is narrower and legally sharper: an officer whose right or duty it is to exact the performance of, or to perform, the act referred to. An act the law assigns to the proper officer is not validly done by just any officer; the words "the proper officer" in a provision name a specific competence, not a job title.

Two further definitions complete the cast. "Customs business" covers every activity connected with importation or exportation, and "customs house agent" means a person granted a licence to carry out that customs business on others' behalf — the profession the clearing industry is built on.

When a provision says the Commissioner-General may release goods, or the proper officer may examine them, read those words as legal assignments of power to a named person.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Risk-based control: the primary basis of customs work
BODY:
Section 3(1) of Act 891 provides that the Authority shall conduct customs controls including random checks. Subsection (2) then fixes the method: a customs control shall primarily be based on risk management, with the objective of identifying and evaluating risks, and developing counter-measures on the basis of risk-management criteria developed and updated regularly from international, national and local level data.

Two consequences follow. First, examination is selective by law: the law does not promise that every consignment is opened, because the Act itself makes risk-based control the primary basis of what customs does. Second, random checks remain part of the design — the word "including" in subsection (1) keeps them in the toolkit precisely so that a trader cannot predict every intervention.

The provision also looks outward and keeps its own secrets. Subsection (3) permits the Authority, in conjunction with cooperating foreign customs administrations, to carry out joint control and targeting activities. Subsection (4) declares specific risk-management information — the risk register, the risk profile and other risk assessment data — confidential.

For the learner at the desk, the practical meaning is this: whether a consignment is examined is decided by a lawful system of risk, not by an officer's mood, and the data behind that system is protected by statute.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Controlled places and the moment of arrival
BODY:
Customs control is anchored to places and moments the Act defines.

The place: section 4(1) empowers the Commissioner-General to designate an area within the country as a customs-controlled area, where the Authority has control over goods, persons and conveyances. Section 5(1) then lists what may lawfully happen inside one: the loading, unloading, inspection, assessment and clearing of imported or exported goods; the temporary storage of goods not yet released, including goods not yet declared and goods seized and liable to forfeiture; the embarkation, disembarkation or control of persons; and the storage and processing of imported goods under bond. Those four permitted activities are the legal floor of everything a port terminal does.

The moment: "arrival" is defined in section 151 as the point in time when a conveyance crosses the borders into the country. From that moment, the Act's arrival and reporting provisions begin to apply to the master of the conveyance — which is why the definition matters even though it is one sentence.

Between the place and the moment, customs holds the goods: from arrival to release, goods are under customs control, and every act performed on them must trace to a provision.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Duty as the Act defines it, and what it is not
BODY:
"Duty" is defined in section 151 of Act 891: customs duty, excise duty, tax or other charges collectible on imported or exported goods. Three features of that definition carry the whole curriculum.

First, the scope is goods that cross the border. The definition attaches to goods imported or exported — which is why a tax charged on a purely domestic transaction, with no crossing of the border, is not customs duty even when it is collected by the same revenue authority. The VAT charged on a shop sale in Kumasi sits outside this definition; the import VAT charged at Tema sits inside it.

Second, the definition is plural by design: customs duty, excise duty, tax, and other charges. A single consignment may settle several charges at once, each on its own base — which is why later modules separate import duty from import VAT from levies and fees.

Third, the rate is not in the Act. Section 1(1) imposes the duties and taxes at the rate specified in the Harmonised System, which is the legal route to the tariff schedule — the Tariff Schedule of Ghana. The learner's skill is to read that schedule against the goods, not to memorise it.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A container of frozen mackerel lands at Tema
BODY:
Step 1. The arrival. A reefer vessel crosses into Ghanaian waters bound for the Port of Tema. Under section 151, arrival is the point in time when the conveyance crosses the borders into the country — the legal clock for this consignment starts here.

Step 2. The persons. At the terminal, an officer of the Ghana Revenue Authority — the Authority established under Act 791 — receives the declaration lodged by a licensed customs house agent acting for the importer. The examination, if one is ordered, is performed by the proper officer: the officer whose right or duty it is to perform that act.

Step 3. The control decision. The consignment is not automatically opened. Under section 3(2), control is primarily risk-based; the declaration sits in the risk system, and the consignment is released to examination or exempted from it accordingly.

Step 4. The revenue. The goods are frozen mackerel under the tariff — classifiable, dutiable. Section 1(1) imposes the duties and taxes at the Harmonised System rate; the duty, the import VAT and the prescribed charges are assessed on the customs value.

Step 5. The release. The required documents are in place, no offence is found, and the Commissioner-General may release the goods under section 6(1). The agent books the container out; the terminal releases it against the cleared declaration.

What does this example demonstrate? Every step of an ordinary clearance is an Act 891 provision in motion — arrival, persons, risk, revenue, release — and each actor acts under a definition from section 151.

Key lesson: a clearance is not paperwork around trade; it is the Act executing itself, one provision at a time.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: The same goods at Rotterdam: risk-based control in another administration
BODY:
Step 1. The arrival. A feeder vessel carrying frozen fish berths at Rotterdam, one of Europe's largest ports. The European customs administration that controls the goods is different from Ghana's, but the design of the control is recognisably the same idea Act 891 encodes: declare, assess risk, release.

Step 2. The selection. The declaration is lodged electronically before the vessel berths. The administration's risk system selects a small proportion of containers for physical examination; the rest are released on documents. Ghana's section 3(2) expresses the same principle in statutory words: control shall primarily be based on risk management.

Step 3. The examination. The selected container is opened by an authorised officer. Frozen fish is a controlled commodity in both jurisdictions: temperature, packaging and declared weight are checked against the documents.

Step 4. The difference to notice. What varies between Rotterdam and Tema is the tariff schedule, the agencies' product lists and the local procedure — not the legal architecture. Every customs administration works from a statute that names its authority, defines its powers, and orders its release conditions.

What does this example demonstrate? The mandate this lesson derives from Act 891 — revenue, control, facilitation, protection — is the international shape of customs work, which is why the concepts travel across borders even though the statute does not.

Key lesson: learn the architecture from Act 891 and you can read any customs law; learn only local habits and nothing transfers.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Function lists: derive them, do not recite them
BODY:
Customs teaching often presents a four-function list — revenue, trade facilitation, enforcement, community protection — as a framework to memorise. Treat such lists with care. The Act itself never states them as a list, and a list recited without sources cannot be checked against anything. The World Customs Organization's own mission statement, verified from its website, is a three-part sentence: it facilitates legitimate trade, ensures fair revenue collection and protects society. Even that wording came from a named source, which is exactly the discipline to copy. The safest professional habit is the one this lesson used throughout: state the function, then cite the section of Act 891 that creates it — revenue from section 1, release from section 6, enforcement from sections 37 and 99. If an examiner or a client wants a named framework, produce the source, not a paraphrase from memory.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Officer versus proper officer
BODY:
Every proper officer is an officer, but the reverse does not hold, and the difference decides whether an act is lawful. "Officer" in section 151 is any person employed by the Ghana Revenue Authority and assigned to perform customs duties. "Proper officer" is the officer whose right or duty it is to perform the specific act referred to — so when a provision authorises the proper officer to do something, an officer outside that competence cannot do it validly, however senior or experienced. The same distinction reaches the private sector: a customs house agent performs customs business under a licence, but the agent never acquires the proper officer's statutory powers by holding it. In practice, when you read an Act provision or a Commissioner-General's instruction, ask the first question about any power: who, exactly, is the proper officer for this act?

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
At a customs-controlled area in Tema, a junior officer who has not been assigned examination duties stops a consignment and begins a physical examination. A senior colleague objects that the act is invalid. On what ground is the objection correct?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Only the Commissioner-General may personally order an examination, so the junior officer acted without any authority at all.
2. An examination is only valid when the importer's agent consents to it beforehand.
3. The act belongs to the proper officer — the officer whose right or duty it is to perform it — and an officer outside that competence cannot perform it validly.
4. Physical examinations are no longer permitted, because section 3 makes risk management the only basis of customs control.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 151 of Act 891 defines a proper officer as an officer whose right or duty it is to exact the performance of, or to perform, the act referred to. When a provision assigns an act to the proper officer, the assignment is a legal competence, not a job description: an officer to whom the act has not been assigned cannot perform it validly, however well-intentioned or senior.

Answer 1 fails because the Commissioner-General's involvement is not the test; the question is which officer the act belongs to. Answer 2 invents a consent requirement that exists nowhere in the Act — the importer's agent has no veto over a lawful examination. Answer 3's ground is the correct one. Answer 4 misreads section 3: the provision makes risk management the primary basis of control, it does not abolish examination — physical inspection remains a treatment decision, and subsection (1) even keeps random checks within the toolkit.

The takeaway: in customs law, the question "who may do this?" is answered by the definition of proper officer, and reading a power correctly starts by asking which officer it belongs to.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
An importer complains that his container was selected for examination "by chance" and argues that the law requires every consignment to be examined equally. Which statement best describes the position under Act 891?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. He is right: section 3 requires uniform examination of all imports so that no trader is treated differently.
2. He is wrong: section 3(2) makes risk management the primary basis of customs control, and section 3(1) expressly includes random checks, so examination is selective by law.
3. He is right in part: examinations require the written consent of the importer whenever risk criteria are applied.
4. He is wrong because examinations may only occur after the goods have been auctioned.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 3(2) of Act 891 provides that a customs control shall primarily be based on risk management, with the objective of identifying and evaluating risks and developing counter-measures from international, national and local level data. Selective examination is therefore the law's design, not an irregularity. Section 3(1) also provides that controls include random checks, which keeps unpredictable interventions in the system precisely so that traders cannot game a purely predictable selection.

Answer 1 states the exact opposite of the provision: uniform examination is not required, and would defeat the risk-based design the Act chose. Answer 3 invents a consent rule with no statutory basis — the importer's rights run through release conditions and objection machinery, not through vetoing examinations. Answer 4 confuses the examination stage with the auction machinery that applies to goods that are never cleared, a completely different part of the Act.

The takeaway: a risk-based selection is lawful control working as designed; the trader's protection lies in the release conditions and the objection process, not in a right to uniform treatment.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A trader's accountant asks why the VAT charged on a shop sale in Kumasi is not treated as customs duty, when import VAT paid at Tema is part of the customs charges. Which option best explains the distinction?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The distinction is the collecting authority: VAT collected by a different office becomes customs duty automatically.
2. Both charges are customs duty, but the domestic one is at a lower rate.
3. The definition of duty in section 151 attaches to charges collectible on imported or exported goods — goods crossing the border — so domestic-transaction VAT falls outside it, while import VAT at the border falls inside.
4. The distinction is the goods: VAT on any goods at all is customs duty if the goods are later resold.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 151 of Act 891 defines duty as customs duty, excise duty, tax or other charges collectible on imported or exported goods. The definition's scope is the border crossing: what makes a charge a customs charge is that it is collected on goods as they are imported or exported, not the name of the charge or the identity of the trader. Import VAT is collected on goods at importation, so it sits inside the definition; the VAT on a domestic shop sale involves no importation or exportation, so it falls outside it — even though the same revenue authority administers both.

Answer 1 confuses administration with legal scope; the same authority collecting a charge does not make the charge a customs charge. Answer 2 misstates the basis entirely — the split is not about rates. Answer 4 invents a resale test that exists nowhere in the definition.

The takeaway: the border crossing is the hook. Duty, in the Act's own words, is what the law collects on goods because they are imported or exported.
