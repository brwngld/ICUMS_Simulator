12 questions, 1 point each (platform default pass mark 70%). Questions are arranged in lesson order: Q1-3 test lesson 1.1, Q4-6 lesson 1.2, Q7-9 lesson 1.3, Q10-12 lesson 1.4.

=== ASSESSMENT Q1 | tests lesson 1.1 ===
QUESTION:
At the baggage hall of Kotoka International Airport, a customs officer who has been assigned to cargo examination duties at Tema stops a passenger and examines her luggage, although the standing instructions assign passenger examination at the airport to a different team. Which option best describes the legal position?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The examination is valid, because every officer employed by the Ghana Revenue Authority and assigned to perform customs duties is a proper officer for every customs act.
2. The examination is invalid, because "proper officer" means the officer whose right or duty it is to perform the act referred to — and the officer here has not been assigned passenger examination at that post.
3. The examination is valid only if the passenger consented to it before it began.
4. The examination is invalid because baggage cannot be examined before release under any provision.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 151 of Act 891 defines an officer as a person employed by the Ghana Revenue Authority and assigned to perform customs duties, and a proper officer as an officer whose right or duty it is to exact the performance of, or to perform, the act referred to. The second definition is a competence rule: where a provision or standing instruction assigns an act, the officer outside the assignment does not hold it.

Answer 1 collapses the two definitions into one, which is precisely the misconception the lesson dismantles — employment and a general assignment do not make every officer proper for every act. Answer 3 invents a consent requirement with no basis in the Act. Answer 4 is wrong on the law: examination is a core customs activity under section 5(1)(a), and the defect in the scenario is the examiner, not the existence of the power.

The takeaway: read every power assignment as naming its holder — the question "which officer is proper for this act?" is the first question of legality, not the last.

=== ASSESSMENT Q2 | tests lesson 1.1 ===
QUESTION:
A shipping line's operations manager complains to the Customs Division that her vessels' containers are "examined arbitrarily" and demands a written rule that every container be examined equally. Which option best evaluates her demand against section 3 of Act 891?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The demand is justified, because equality of examination is a statutory right of every trader.
2. The demand is justified only for containers carrying food and drugs, which the agencies examine exclusively.
3. The demand misreads the law: section 3(2) makes risk management the primary basis of customs control, with counter-measures developed from international, national and local data, and section 3(1) expressly includes random checks — selectivity is the law's design, and the risk data behind it is confidential under section 3(4).
4. The demand misreads the law, because containers may never be examined at all before release under section 6.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 3(1) of Act 891 provides that the Authority shall conduct customs controls including random checks; subsection (2) provides that control shall primarily be based on risk management, with the objective of identifying and evaluating risks and developing counter-measures from risk-management criteria updated regularly from international, national and local level data. Subsection (4) makes the risk register, profiles and other assessment data confidential — so the manager is not entitled to the criteria behind a selection either.

Answer 1 asserts the uniformity the Act deliberately rejects; the design is selective control, with random checks kept in the toolkit to preserve unpredictability. Answer 2 invents an agency carve-out that has no basis in section 3. Answer 4 contradicts section 6(1), which contemplates goods examined or exempted from examination — examination remains a lawful treatment.

The takeaway: risk-based, partially random, confidential by statute — that is the lawful shape of control, and it is a poor candidate for a trader's demand for uniformity.

=== ASSESSMENT Q3 | tests lesson 1.1 ===
QUESTION:
A trader receives two invoices in one month: import VAT paid at the Port of Tema on a consignment of tiles, and VAT charged by a plumber for repairs to his warehouse in Accra. Both were collected by entities within the revenue system. Which option best applies the Act's definition of duty to the two charges?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Both charges are customs duty, because both were collected within the same revenue system.
2. Neither charge is customs duty, because VAT is never a customs charge.
3. The import VAT at Tema falls within the definition of duty — charges collectible on imported or exported goods — while the plumber's VAT on a domestic transaction involves no importation or exportation and falls outside it.
4. The plumber's VAT is customs duty because building materials are dutiable goods when they are imported.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 151 of Act 891 defines duty as customs duty, excise duty, tax or other charges collectible on imported or exported goods. The hook is the border crossing: the definition's scope is the goods' importation or exportation, not the identity of the collector or the type of tax. Import VAT is collected on goods at importation, so it sits inside the definition; the plumber's VAT is charged on a domestic transaction with no crossing of the border, so it sits outside it — however similar the two charges look on an invoice.

Answer 1 mistakes administration for legal scope. Answer 2 overcorrects: VAT charged at importation is expressly within the definition's "tax or other charges". Answer 4 invents a goods-based test that exists nowhere in the definition — resalability of the goods plays no part.

The takeaway: duty, in the Act's own words, is what the law collects on goods because they are imported or exported — the crossing is the hook, and everything else is classification work.

=== ASSESSMENT Q4 | tests lesson 1.2 ===
QUESTION:
A compliance officer is asked to state where the rule on customs record keeping comes from, and she replies: "From the Commissioner-General's Orders, which are the highest source of customs law in Ghana." Which option best assesses her answer?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Her answer is correct: the Orders are the highest source because the Commissioner-General heads the Customs Division.
2. Her answer is partially correct: the Orders are the highest source for offences, while the Act governs records.
3. Her answer is wrong in its hierarchy claim: the Act is the primary statute and the Regulations (L.I. 2248) are made under it by legislative Instrument, as section 151 defines; the Orders direct practice inside the powers the Act already granted, so the record-keeping duty traces to the Act (section 9) and its subordinate instruments.
4. Her answer is wrong because record keeping is governed only by the trader's internal policy.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. The GRA training deck sets the sources in order: the Constitution, then Act 891 with its amendment Act 905 and the Revenue Administration Act 915, then the Customs Regulations (L.I. 2248), then the Commissioner-General's Orders and the Customs Codes. Section 151 defines Regulations as those made under the Act by legislative Instrument — beneath the statute that authorises them. The record-keeping duty itself is in section 9 of the Act; the Orders and Regulations operationalise it.

Answer 1 confuses institutional seniority inside the Authority with legal hierarchy among instruments; heading an administration does not elevate an instruction above its enabling statute. Answer 2 invents a subject-matter split with no basis in the sources. Answer 4 ignores the statutory duty entirely — section 9 is the anchor, and the lesson's habit is to trace the claim to it.

The takeaway: name the layer, then the provision — a compliance answer that cannot trace its rule upward is not yet an answer.

=== ASSESSMENT Q5 | tests lesson 1.2 ===
QUESTION:
A consultant writes to a client: "The fine for this offence under section 121 of Act 891 is exactly 750 penalty units — it is printed in the Act." The client asks you to check. Which option best describes what you find and conclude?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The consultant is right, because a printed statute is the current law.
2. The consultant is right, because penalty units are fixed by the Constitution and cannot change.
3. The consultant's figure cannot be relied on as printed: the Act's text states it is legislation as at 18 May 2015 with five amendments not yet applied — Act 923 of 2016, Act 949 of 2017, Act 957 of 2017, Act 1014 of 2020 and Act 1083 of 2022 — and monetary penalties are exactly what such amendments move; the figure must be verified against the current law and cited before it is stated.
4. The consultant's figure is wrong because section 121 was repealed by Act 905.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. The supplied Act 891 states on its first page that it is legislation as at 18 May 2015 and lists the outstanding amendments not yet applied: Act 923 of 2016, Act 949 of 2017, Act 957 of 2017, Act 1014 of 2020 and Act 1083 of 2022. Monetary penalties are precisely the content amendment Acts of this kind move, so a figure printed in the 2015 text is a candidate for change — the professional states the offence and the mechanism from the Act, and verifies the amount against the current law before stating it, citing the source.

Answer 1 is the trap the front matter exists to disarm. Answer 2 invents a constitutional fixity that is false — penalty-unit values are exactly what amendment legislation adjusts. Answer 4 asserts a repeal with no basis in the sources; Act 905 is an applied amendment, not a repealer of section 121.

The takeaway: the unapplied amendments turn every printed penalty figure into an unverified figure; verify, cite, or withhold the number.

=== ASSESSMENT Q6 | tests lesson 1.2 ===
QUESTION:
An exporter's foreign buyer asserts: "Ghana's customs law incorporates the Revised Kyoto Convention, the WTO Trade Facilitation Agreement and the ECOWAS Common External Tariff directly, exactly as it incorporates the TIR convention." Which option best evaluates the assertion?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The assertion is correct: all international instruments are incorporated into Act 891 automatically once Ghana signs them.
2. The assertion is correct for the TIR convention only, and the others do not apply to Ghana at all.
3. The assertion conflates two routes: Act 891 incorporates the Harmonised System convention (section 151's definition, with duty at its rates under section 1(1)) and names the TIR and ATA conventions in section 95(1); the Revised Kyoto Convention (Ghana acceded 29 June 2019), the WTO TFA (accepted 4 January 2017, in force 22 February 2017) and the ECOWAS CET (implemented 1 February 2016) apply through their own adoption, verified from official records — not through Act 891's text.
4. The assertion is wrong because Act 891 incorporates no international instrument at all.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Act 891 incorporates instruments by naming them: section 151's definition of the Harmonised Commodity Description and Coding System brings the convention nomenclature into the Act, and section 1(1) imposes duty at its rates; section 95(1)(b) and (c) name the TIR Carnets convention and the ATA carnet and temporary-admission conventions for transit movements. The other instruments the buyer lists are real and verified — Ghana acceded to the Revised Kyoto Convention on 29 June 2019 per the WCO's contracting-parties list, accepted the WTO Trade Facilitation Agreement on 4 January 2017 per the WTO's facility, and implemented the ECOWAS Common External Tariff on 1 February 2016 — but their force comes from their own adoption, not from Act 891's text.

Answer 1 invents automatic incorporation. Answer 2 understates Ghana's actual participation. Answer 4 ignores the sections that name the instruments on the Act's face.

The takeaway: incorporation is a citation fact. Find the section that names the instrument; for the rest, verify status from the official record and content from the instrument itself.

=== ASSESSMENT Q7 | tests lesson 1.3 ===
QUESTION:
A district assembly proposes to declare a riverside landing site "a customs port" by passing a local resolution, and asks the Customs Division to begin operations there. Which option best describes the lawful route under Act 891?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The assembly's resolution is sufficient, because local government controls landing sites.
2. A port is designated by the Minister — the Minister responsible for Finance — by notice published in the Gazette and at least two daily newspapers of national circulation; the assembly's resolution cannot create it.
3. The Commissioner-General may designate the landing site as a port by notice in writing, as with transit sheds.
4. No route exists: riverside landing sites can never be customs ports.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 151 of Act 891 defines a port as a place, whether on the coast or elsewhere, designated by the Minister — the Minister responsible for Finance — by notice published in the Gazette and at least two daily newspapers of national circulation, subject to conditions or limitations in the notice. The definition fixes both the office and the publication, and a local resolution is neither.

Answer 1 confuses local-government authority over land use with the statutory designation power. Answer 3 misassigns the power: the Commissioner-General's written notices designate transit sheds and customs-controlled areas — different places, different instruments, different publication. Answer 4 overstates the negative: nothing in the Act prevents a riverside site from being designated a port; it simply must be designated by the Minister in the prescribed manner.

The takeaway: the definitions do not just describe places — they name the only office that can create them, and the publication that makes it real.

=== ASSESSMENT Q8 | tests lesson 1.3 ===
QUESTION:
A licensed terminal operator proposes to open a shop inside its licensed customs-controlled area selling directly to the public from goods that have been imported but not yet released. Which option best assesses the proposal against section 5(1)?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The proposal is lawful, because the operator holds a licence and a licence permits everything inside the area.
2. The proposal is lawful if the goods have been in the area for more than fourteen days.
3. The proposal is not within the permitted activities: section 5(1) permits loading, unloading, inspection, assessment and clearing; temporary storage of unreleased goods including undeclared and seized goods; embarkation, disembarkation or control of persons; and storage and processing under bond — a public retail sale of unreleased goods is none of these.
4. The proposal is lawful provided the shop sells only to the operator's employees.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 5(1) of Act 891 lists the activities that may be conducted in a customs-controlled area, and the list is exhaustive in structure: handling and clearing of goods; temporary storage of unreleased goods expressly including undeclared and seized goods; embarkation, disembarkation or control of persons; and storage and processing of imported goods under bond. Goods inside the perimeter are under customs control from arrival to release — they leave through clearance, not through a shop counter.

Answer 1 mistakes the licence's effect: it brings the area inside the legal perimeter, it does not waive the limits on activities within it. Answer 2 drags the fourteen-day rule of section 4(3) into an unrelated question — that rule governs how long entered goods may remain, not what may be sold. Answer 4 narrows the customers without changing the activity, which remains a retail sale outside section 5(1).

The takeaway: the licence creates the perimeter; section 5(1) defines what the perimeter is for — and retail is not on the list.

=== ASSESSMENT Q9 | tests lesson 1.3 ===
QUESTION:
A bonded warehouse keeper and a terminal operator compare their licences. Which option best states the true difference between the keeper's position under section 47 and the operator's under section 46?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. They hold the same licence under different names; the sections are interchangeable.
2. The keeper's licence additionally requires a deposited bond and prescribed fees, creates a place where goods to be warehoused are secured (a private bonded warehouse under section 151), and the licence is in an approved form and revocable — while the section 46 licence brings an area inside customs control for handling and storage in the ordinary clearance flow.
3. The keeper needs no licence, only a bond; the operator needs a licence but no bond.
4. The operator's licence is permanent, while the keeper's licence must be renewed annually by law.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 47(1) requires three conditions for a private bonded warehouse: due licensing by the Commissioner-General, the keeper's deposit of the required bond, and payment of the prescribed fees; section 151 defines the private bonded warehouse as a building or place appointed by the Commissioner-General and licensed to be a place where goods to be warehoused are secured, with the warehouse keeper as its licensed operator; subsection (2) requires the licence to be in an approved form and subsection (3) provides for revocation. Section 46(1), by contrast, licenses an area to operate as a customs-controlled area for handling, storage of unreleased goods, or processing of persons — the ordinary clearance perimeter, not a duty-suspension regime.

Answer 1 erases the regime difference the two sections exist to draw. Answer 3 inverts the requirements: the keeper's bond is statutory (s.47(1)(b)), and nothing in section 46 exempts a licensee from conditions the Act or Regulations attach. Answer 4 invents a renewal rule with no basis in the sections cited.

The takeaway: same officer, two different regimes — a licensed perimeter for clearance flow, and a bonded regime for duty suspension. The provisions, not the paperwork's appearance, tell them apart.

=== ASSESSMENT Q10 | tests lesson 1.4 ===
QUESTION:
A new clearing clerk is handed a file containing: the master's arrival report, the bill of lading, the pre-entry lodged at sea, the discharge list, and the release confirmation. Which option best arranges the events these documents record, in the order the Act provides?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Release, discharge, arrival report, pre-entry, bill of lading.
2. Bill of lading (with the pre-entry lodged), arrival and report, discharge under section 21, entry taking effect, examination or exemption, release under section 6(1).
3. Arrival report, release, pre-entry, discharge, bill of lading.
4. Pre-entry, release, discharge, bill of lading, arrival report.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. The Act's spine runs: the pre-entry lodged before importation under section 50, with section 14's documents; arrival under section 16 and the master's report under section 17; discharge under the mooring and unloading provisions of section 21; the entry taking effect at the time of entry under section 48; examination or exemption from examination; and release under section 6(1) — the Commissioner-General may release declared goods as soon as they have been examined or exempted, where no offence is found and the required documents are acquired.

Answers 1, 3 and 4 scramble the spine in ways that would be impossible in practice — release cannot precede discharge, and the pre-entry by definition precedes arrival — but the assessment point is not puzzle-solving: the clerk who knows the spine can place any missing document by asking which stage it serves.

The takeaway: the lifecycle is a fixed order of provisions, and every clearance document belongs to exactly one stage of it.

=== ASSESSMENT Q11 | tests lesson 1.4 ===
QUESTION:
Two movements leave the Port of Tema without duty payment on the same morning: one container is trucked to a bonded warehouse inland under a transit declaration; another is lifted from a feeder vessel directly onto an ocean-going vessel for Europe. Which option best distinguishes the two procedures?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Both are transit under section 95, because neither consignment pays duty.
2. Both are transhipment under section 96, because neither consignment enters home use.
3. The inland movement is transit under section 95(1) — goods moved from one port of entry to another port under a bond or by a bonded carrier, or under the TIR/ATA conventions; the direct transfer is transhipment under section 96(1) — goods moved from an importing conveyance to an exporting conveyance within the same port of entry, with the declarant responsible for unloading, securing, identification and loading.
4. The inland movement is transhipment and the direct transfer is transit, because the container changed vessels.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 95(1) of Act 891 provides transit for goods moved from one port of entry to another port within the country — under a bond or by a bonded carrier or both without duty payment, or under the TIR and ATA conventions the Act names. Section 96(1) provides transhipment for goods transferred from an importing conveyance to an exporting conveyance within the same port of entry, without payment of duty; section 96(2) makes the declarant responsible for unloading, securing the goods in an approved customs-controlled area, identification, and loading.

Answer 1 and Answer 2 both erase the geographic distinction the two sections are built on: inland movement between ports is transit; same-port, conveyance-to-conveyance transfer is transhipment. Answer 4 is simply the map read backwards — the vessel change is transhipment's defining fact, not transit's.

The takeaway: the geography identifies the procedure — cross-country under security is transit; within-port between conveyances is transhipment — and each carries its own responsibility rules.

=== ASSESSMENT Q12 | tests lesson 1.4 ===
QUESTION:
A consignment's owner walks away from a clearance. Months later the client asks his new agent what became of the goods, and the agent replies: "They were deposited in the State warehouse, so they are lost for good." Which option best evaluates the agent's statement?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The statement is correct: deposit in the State warehouse under section 53 is the final disposal of the goods.
2. The statement is wrong in its finality: non-clearance makes the goods uncleared (section 52) and uncustomed within the meaning of section 151, and the State warehouse deposit (section 53) secures the goods and the duties that may be due — the deposit is a stage, with the subsequent machinery running on its own provisions.
3. The statement is wrong because abandoned goods must be returned to their owner on request at any time.
4. The statement is correct only if the goods were prohibited goods; otherwise they are destroyed.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 52 of Act 891 deals with uncleared goods, and the abandoned consignment sits squarely in section 151's definition of uncustomed goods — goods liable to duty on which the full duties have not been paid, and goods dealt with contrary to the customs provisions whether liable to duty or not. Section 53 then provides the deposit of goods in the State warehouse, defined as the place approved by the Commissioner-General for the deposit of uncustomed, un-entered, un-examined, abandoned, detained or seized goods, for the security of the goods or the payment of duties that may be due. Deposit secures; it does not conclude.

Answer 1 is the conflation the lesson warns against — deposit is a stage, and the auction and disposal machinery that may follow runs with its own procedure. Answer 3 invents an unconditional right of return that the provisions do not grant. Answer 4 is false on both branches: abandonment is expressly a deposit ground, and destruction is not the stated consequence.

The takeaway: the failure mode of the lifecycle is a path of provisions — uncleared, uncustomed, deposited — and correct advice names the stage, not the ending.
