=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The stack of customs law in Ghana
BODY:
Customs powers in Ghana come from a stack of instruments, and the GRA's own training deck for the customs proficiency course sets the order out plainly: the Constitution of the Republic of Ghana, 1992; the Customs Act, 2015 (Act 891); the Customs (Amendment) Act, 2015 (Act 905); the Revenue Administration Act, 2016 (Act 915); the Customs Regulations (Legislative Instrument 2248); the Commissioner-General's Orders and the Customs Codes; and international instruments regarding customs operations.

Read the stack from the top. The Constitution authorises the state to impose taxation generally. Act 891 is the primary statute: it imposes the duties and taxes on imported and exported goods (section 1), creates the machinery of control, and defines the terms the whole system uses. Act 905 amended it; Act 915, the Revenue Administration Act, governs how revenue administration itself works across the Authority.

Beneath the Acts sit the Regulations — subordinate legislation made under the Act by legislative Instrument. Section 151 of Act 891 defines "Regulations" exactly that way: the Regulations made under this Act by legislative Instrument. L.I. 2248 is the customs set. Beneath them sit the Commissioner-General's Orders and the Customs Codes, which direct practice inside the powers the Act already granted.

The lesson's habit: for any customs claim, name the layer it comes from.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: What the Act does, and what the Regulations do
BODY:
The division of labour between an Act and its Regulations is easiest to see in what each document actually contains.

The Act creates the powers and the offences. Act 891 imposes the duties and taxes (section 1), designates customs-controlled areas (section 4), provides for release (section 6), defines the offences in sections 121 to 143, and fixes the interpretation of every term in section 151. When you need to know whether a power exists, you look to the Act.

The Regulations supply the operational detail the Act deliberately leaves open. Throughout Act 891 the draftsman writes "as prescribed by Regulations" — the retention of records, the licence forms, the procedures for examinations. That phrase is the Act delegating detail to a legislative instrument, which can be updated without returning to Parliament for a whole new Act. Section 151's definition of Regulations — those made under this Act by legislative Instrument — is what makes L.I. 2248 lawful.

The discipline this teaches: a rule found only in practice, a circular or an order, is not automatically law. It is lawful because a provision of the Act authorised it to be made. Trace every operational rule upward until it reaches its enabling provision — if the trace fails, the rule is the sort of thing a professional verifies before relying on it.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The unapplied amendments, and why they change what you may state
BODY:
The supplied text of Act 891 carries its own warning on its first page. It reads: Legislation as at 18 May 2015. Note: there are outstanding amendments that have not yet been applied: Act 923 of 2016, Act 949 of 2017, Act 957 of 2017, Act 1014 of 2020, Act 1083 of 2022.

Five amendment Acts, none of them incorporated into the text the learner is reading. The practical consequence is exact and serious: any provision those amendments touched may have changed — and monetary penalties are precisely what amendment Acts of this kind move. A penalty figure quoted from the 2015 text may be years out of date; a procedure the amendments re-wrote may no longer operate as printed.

The professional rule that follows: state the offence and the mechanism from the Act; do not state a fine, a penalty-unit value or an imprisonment term unless you have verified it against the amending Act or the current schedule, and cite which. This rule is not pedantry — learners will repeat these numbers to clients, and a stale figure quoted with confidence is worse than no figure at all.

One related trap to avoid: Act 905 of 2015 amended Act 891 and IS applied — it does not appear on the outstanding list. Check the front matter before calling any amendment unapplied.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The instruments the Act itself incorporates
BODY:
Act 891 does not leave international instruments outside its own text; it pulls specific ones in.

First and most important: the Harmonised System. Section 151 defines the Harmonised Commodity Description and Coding System as the nomenclature comprising the headings, sub-headings and their related numerical codes, the section, chapter and sub-heading notes and the general rules for the interpretation of the Harmonised System, contained in the text of the International Convention on the Harmonised Commodity Description and Coding System approved by the Customs Co-operation Council on 14th June, 1983 and the relevant Schedules attached to it. Section 1(1) then imposes duty at the rate that nomenclature specifies. The convention is inside Ghanaian law because the Act says so.

Second: the transit provisions. Section 95(1)(b) allows goods to move in transit in accordance with the Customs Convention on the International Transport of Goods under Cover of TIR Carnets, where the movement began or ends outside Ghana; subsection (c) invokes the ATA carnet and temporary-admission conventions. The Act names them; the procedures exist because the Act references them.

A note on the word "tariff": section 151 defines it simply as the Tariff Schedule of Ghana — the schedule of rates the Act's imposition provision points to. When later modules speak of reading the tariff, they mean working through that schedule's rows against the goods.

The lesson: when an instrument is incorporated, its text binds through the section that cites it — cite the section, then the instrument.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Facilitation instruments Ghana has joined outside the Act
BODY:
Beyond the instruments the Act incorporates, Ghana has acceded to facilitation instruments whose force comes from their own ratification. Three verified facts anchor this lesson.

The Revised Kyoto Convention — the World Customs Organization's modernised framework for simplified and effective customs procedures — was acceded to by Ghana on 29 June 2019, as recorded on the WCO's official list of contracting parties. The WTO Trade Facilitation Agreement was accepted by Ghana on 4 January 2017, and entered into force generally on 22 February 2017, as the WTO's TFA facility records. And within the region, Ghana began implementing the ECOWAS Common External Tariff on 1 February 2016, moving the tariff from four bands to five: 0 percent for essential social goods, 5 percent, 10 percent, 20 percent, and 35 percent for specific goods for economic development.

What these instruments change for the professional is expectation-setting: they commit the administration to predictability, transparency and facilitation standards, and they shape how the tariff is structured. What they do not change is the citation discipline — an instrument's force is one fact (with its source), and its content is another (which must be quoted from the instrument, never paraphrased).

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The institutions the definitions name
BODY:
Two section 151 definitions place the people and bodies around the law, and both recur in every module that follows.

"Minister" means the Minister responsible for Finance. Provisions that run "the Minister may, by legislative instrument..." or designations "by notice published in the Gazette" at a port (section 151's definition of port names the Minister) are assignments to that office — the political authority over parts of the customs framework.

"Board" means the governing body of the Ghana Revenue Authority — the institution above the Commissioner-General inside the Authority's own governance, established under Act 791.

The lesson from both definitions is the same one customs professionals apply daily: powers in this field are always held by someone whose identity the law fixes. When a provision names the Minister, the Commissioner-General, the Board, the Authority, an officer or a proper officer, the name is not decoration — it is the answer to who may lawfully act. Read every power assignment as naming its holder, and you will never wonder whose signature a document needs.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Checking a penalty figure before advising a client
BODY:
Step 1. The question. An importer's vehicle was seized for a declaration offence, and the client asks his customs house agent what fine he faces. The agent opens her copy of Act 891 and finds the offence provision.

Step 2. The trap. The printed text states a penalty in an amount of money. A fast answer would quote it — and would be wrong, because the agent's first duty is to the text she is holding: Legislation as at 18 May 2015, with five outstanding amendments not yet applied (Act 923 of 2016, Act 949 of 2017, Act 957 of 2017, Act 1014 of 2020, Act 1083 of 2022). Monetary penalties are exactly what such amendment Acts move.

Step 3. The check. The agent verifies the figure against the current law — the amending Acts and the schedule in force — before she says anything. Where she cannot verify the figure, she says so plainly: the offence is X under section Y of Act 891; the amount is subject to the amendments; here is the verified figure, with its source.

Step 4. The advice. The client receives the offence, the mechanism, the verified amount with its citation, and the objection route if he disagrees with the assessment.

What does this example demonstrate? The unapplied amendments turn every penalty figure in the 2015 text into an unverified figure until checked — and the professional's credibility is the citation.

Key lesson: quote the offence from the Act; verify the amount before stating it; name your source or say nothing.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Verifying an instrument's status before it reaches the client
BODY:
Step 1. The question. An exporter moving goods through a neighbouring corridor asks his consultant whether Ghana has adopted the international facilitation conventions his foreign buyer keeps citing, and what the ECOWAS tariff bands are.

Step 2. The discipline. The consultant treats each claim as two separate facts: is the instrument in force for Ghana, and what does it say. Status claims come from official lists; content claims come from the instrument's text.

Step 3. The status check. The consultant consults the World Customs Organization's published list of contracting parties to the Revised Kyoto Convention, and finds Ghana's accession recorded on 29 June 2019. On the WTO's Trade Facilitation Agreement facility, Ghana's acceptance is recorded on 4 January 2017, with the agreement in force from 22 February 2017. Each fact carries its source.

Step 4. The content boundary. The buyer's letter summarises a convention's provisions loosely. The consultant does not adopt the summary: she quotes the convention's own text where a provision matters, and labels the regional fact — the ECOWAS Common External Tariff, implemented in Ghana on 1 February 2016 with five bands from 0 to 35 percent — with its verified source.

What does this example demonstrate? Instrument work is two checks, never one: status from the official record, content from the instrument itself.

Key lesson: status and content are different facts; verify each against its own source, and the client gets advice that survives scrutiny.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Act 905 is applied — do not call it unapplied
BODY:
The front matter's outstanding list names Act 923 of 2016, Act 949 of 2017, Act 957 of 2017, Act 1014 of 2020 and Act 1083 of 2022. Act 905 of 2015 — the Customs (Amendment) Act, 2015 — is not on that list, because it is an applied amendment: the GRA training deck lists it as part of the operative law, alongside Act 891 and the Revenue Administration Act, 2016 (Act 915). The distinction matters professionally: describing an applied amendment as unapplied — or the reverse — sends a client checking the wrong law. The rule is mechanical: an amendment is unapplied only if the text you are holding says so in its own front matter. Check the front matter every time, because a new printing can change the list.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Instrument wording is quoted, never paraphrased
BODY:
When a lesson, a letter or a client memo needs an instrument's rule — a convention provision, a band definition, a facilitation standard — the wording must come from the instrument itself, matched exactly, or from the training deck's own slides, cited. The reason is practical rather than pedantic: instruments are negotiated texts in which a single word carries the obligation, and a confident paraphrase quietly replaces the negotiated word with a guess. Where this programme could not verify an instrument's text, it says so in the facts section and writes the underlying rule without naming the instrument. Do the same in practice: a client advised "the convention probably allows this" is a client being set up for a border surprise.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A new trainee arranges the sources of customs law in this order: Customs Regulations (L.I. 2248), then the Constitution, then the Commissioner-General's Orders, then Act 891. Which option best assesses the arrangement?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The arrangement is correct, because operational instruments always stand above the statutes that authorise them.
2. The arrangement is incorrect: the Constitution stands first, then Acts of Parliament (Act 891, its amendment Act 905, and Act 915), then the Regulations made by legislative Instrument under the Act, then the Commissioner-General's Orders and Customs Codes.
3. The arrangement is incorrect only because the Commissioner-General's Orders outrank the Constitution within the customs field.
4. The arrangement is correct for practice purposes, because officers work daily with the Orders and the Regulations rather than with the Act itself.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. The GRA training deck sets the stack out in order: the Constitution of the Republic of Ghana, 1992; the Customs Act, 2015 (Act 891); the Customs (Amendment) Act, 2015 (Act 905); the Revenue Administration Act, 2016 (Act 915); the Customs Regulations (L.I. 2248); the Commissioner-General's Orders and Customs Codes; and the international instruments regarding customs operations. Section 151 reinforces the structure from below: Regulations are "made under this Act by legislative Instrument", which places them beneath the Act that authorises them.

Answer 1 inverts the constitutional logic — an instrument cannot outrank the statute that empowers it. Answer 3 is constitutionally absurd: no Commissioner-General's instruction overrides the Constitution. Answer 4 mistakes daily working habits for legal hierarchy; the officer uses the Orders daily because they operationalise the Act, not because they rank above it.

The takeaway: trace every rule upward to its enabling layer, and you will know both what the rule is and where its authority ends.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
A memo to a client states: "Under section 121 of Act 891, the penalty for the offence is 1,200 penalty units." Which option best evaluates the memo?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The memo is safe, because section 121 of the Act is printed with that figure and printed text is always current.
2. The memo is safe only if the agent personally believes the figure is correct.
3. The memo is unreliable as written: the text of Act 891 is stated as at 18 May 2015 with five amendments unapplied (Acts 923, 949, 957, 1014 and 1083), and penalty figures are exactly what such amendments change — the amount must be verified against the current law and cited before it is stated.
4. The memo is unreliable because penalty amounts may never be discussed with clients in any form.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. The supplied Act 891 states on its first page that it is legislation as at 18 May 2015 and lists five outstanding amendments not yet applied: Act 923 of 2016, Act 949 of 2017, Act 957 of 2017, Act 1014 of 2020 and Act 1083 of 2022. Monetary penalties are precisely the content such amendment Acts move, so a figure printed in the 2015 text is a candidate for change, not a fact to forward.

Answer 1 mistakes printing for currency — the front matter exists precisely to warn against that reading. Answer 2 substitutes confidence for verification: personal belief is not a source, and the lesson's rule is to verify against the amending Act or the current schedule and cite which. Answer 3 is the lesson's position exactly. Answer 4 overcorrects into an absurd rule: offence mechanisms are stated and cited freely; only the unverified amount is withheld.

The takeaway: quote the offence from the Act; verify the amount, cite the source, or say plainly that it is unverified.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
Which set of international instruments is incorporated into Ghanaian customs law by the text of Act 891 itself?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The Revised Kyoto Convention, the WTO Trade Facilitation Agreement and the ECOWAS Common External Tariff.
2. The Harmonised System convention (section 151's definition, with duty imposed at its rates by section 1(1)), the TIR Carnets convention and the ATA carnet and temporary-admission conventions (section 95(1)).
3. Only the Harmonised System convention; the Act references no other instrument by name.
4. All customs conventions worldwide that Ghana has signed, incorporated automatically.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Act 891 incorporates instruments by naming them in its own text: section 151 defines the Harmonised Commodity Description and Coding System by reference to the International Convention approved by the Customs Co-operation Council on 14th June 1983, and section 1(1) imposes duty at that nomenclature's rates; section 95(1)(b) permits transit under the Customs Convention on the International Transport of Goods under Cover of TIR Carnets, and section 95(1)(c) invokes the ATA carnet and temporary-admission conventions.

Answer 1 lists real and important instruments — the Revised Kyoto Convention (Ghana acceded 29 June 2019), the WTO Trade Facilitation Agreement (accepted 4 January 2017) and the ECOWAS Common External Tariff (implemented 1 February 2016) — but their force comes from their own adoption, not from Act 891's text incorporating them. Answer 3 understates the Act. Answer 4 states a rule that exists in no legal system.

The takeaway: incorporation is a citation fact — find the section that names the instrument, and you have found the instrument's foothold in Ghanaian law.
