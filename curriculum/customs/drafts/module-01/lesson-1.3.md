=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Who designates which customs place
BODY:
Every controlled place in Ghanaian customs has a designated authority, and the fastest way to learn them is to read each definition and note the name inside it.

The Minister — defined in section 151 as the Minister responsible for Finance — designates, by notice published in the Gazette and at least two daily newspapers of national circulation: a port, meaning a place on the coast or elsewhere designated to be a port for the purposes of the Act; an approved place of unloading or loading, which includes a quay, jetty, wharf or a part of an airport; and a boarding station, where conveyances bring to for the boarding or setting down of officers.

The Commissioner-General designates, by notice in writing, a transit-shed: a building in a customs-controlled area for the deposit of un-entered, un-declared goods. The same officer designates customs-controlled areas under section 4(1). Two lesser-known definitions complete the map: "sufferance wharf", a place other than an approved place at which the Commissioner-General may allow goods to be loaded or unloaded under conditions he directs; and "approved", which simply means approved by the Commissioner-General or the proper officer.

The pattern is the lesson: political designation by public notice belongs to the Minister; operational designation by written notice belongs to the Commissioner-General.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The customs-controlled area and its designation
BODY:
Section 4(1) of Act 891 empowers the Commissioner-General to designate an area within the country as a customs-controlled area for the administration or enforcement of the Act, and to prescribe by directive the measures necessary for its establishment, the protection of the Government's interests in its conduct and operation, and the withdrawal from and accounting for goods deposited there.

Section 151 gives the definition that travels with the name: a customs-controlled area is a place in the country, designated by the Commissioner-General, where the Authority has control over goods, persons and conveyances. Notice the three objects of control — goods, persons and conveyances — because they are wider than the cargo alone: the people walking the quay and the vessels alongside are inside the legal perimeter too.

Subsection (2) adds a cost rule with a compliance edge: an importer, owner or depositor of goods pays the expenses incurred in respect of goods under the section, as prescribed by Regulations.

The designation is therefore not a physical fence but a legal perimeter: once an area is designated, everything inside it is subject to the Authority's control, and every activity performed there must fit within the provisions the next block sets out.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: What may lawfully happen inside a customs-controlled area
BODY:
Section 5(1) lists the activities that may be conducted in a customs-controlled area, and the list is the legal floor of terminal operations. Four activities, verbatim in substance.

First, the loading, unloading, inspection, assessment and clearing of imported or exported goods — the whole operational cycle of a clearance, permitted in one line.

Second, the temporary storage of goods imported at a port of entry and not yet released by customs, including goods that are not yet declared in accordance with the Act, and goods that are seized and liable to forfeiture. Storage therefore covers undeclared goods and seized goods, not just goods awaiting collection.

Third, the embarkation, disembarkation or control of persons arriving in or departing from the country — the passenger side of a border post.

Fourth, the storage and processing of imported goods under bond — the seed of the warehousing regime that Module 10 develops fully.

Subsection (2) adds the risk rule: goods stored in a customs-controlled area with the permission of the proper officer before shipment are stored at the risk and expense of the exporter, and are subject to rent and other charges as prescribed.

Anything a terminal does with cargo should trace to one of these four permitted activities.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Licensing a private area to operate as customs-controlled
BODY:
Section 46(1) opens the regime to private operators. Despite section 4, a person in charge of any of three kinds of area may apply to the Commissioner-General for a licence to operate the area as a customs-controlled area: an area for the processing of a conveyance arriving in or departing from Ghana, or the loading or unloading of goods onto or from a conveyance; an area for the temporary storage of goods imported at a port of entry and not yet released, including goods not yet declared and goods seized as liable to forfeiture; and an area used for the embarkation, disembarkation or processing of persons arriving in or departing from the country.

The licence is what puts a private yard inside the legal perimeter: once licensed, the operator's area is a customs-controlled area in its own right, and the Authority's control over goods, persons and conveyances extends into it. The application is to the Commissioner-General — the same officer who designates public areas — and the licence carries the obligations and revocation powers the Act and the Regulations attach to it.

The professional reading: an operator without a licence cannot lawfully run a customs-controlled area, however suitable the yard. The perimeter is created by designation or licence, never by fences alone.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The fourteen-day rule
BODY:
Section 4(3) is the provision everyone eventually meets, and it rewards being quoted exactly. Where goods remain in a customs-controlled area for more than fourteen days after being — and the provision names three triggering events — entered under another customs procedure, entered for export, or sold by public auction under the Act, the goods are liable to forfeiture to the State, unless the failure to move the goods is explained to the satisfaction of the Commissioner-General.

Three features carry the rule's force. The period is fourteen days, a statutory deadline under section 4(3) of Act 891 — state it with its provision. The triggers are specific: the goods must have been entered under a procedure, entered for export, or sold at auction; goods that simply sit undeclared are handled by other provisions, such as section 52 on uncleared goods. And the exception is real: an explanation accepted by the Commissioner-General prevents the forfeiture, which makes the rule an incentive to communicate, not a trap.

What forfeiture means in practice — seizure, claims, auction and the State warehouse — belongs to Module 10. What this lesson requires is the rule itself: fourteen days, three triggers, one exception, all in section 4(3).

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The bonded warehouse contrast
BODY:
Section 47(1) governs a different kind of licensed place, and the contrast with section 46 is the point. A person shall not use a building or place as a private bonded warehouse unless three conditions are met: the building or place has been duly licensed by the Commissioner-General; the keeper of the warehouse deposits the required bond; and the keeper pays the prescribed fees.

Section 151 defines the institution the licence creates: a private bonded warehouse is a building or place appointed by the Commissioner-General and licensed to be a place where goods to be warehoused are secured, and the warehouse keeper is the person licensed to operate it.

Subsection (2) requires the licence to be in an approved form — and "approved", remember, means approved by the Commissioner-General or the proper officer. Subsection (3) lets the Commissioner-General revoke a licence at any time, with a constrained refund of the licence fee.

The contrast to carry forward: a section 46 licence brings an area inside customs control for handling and storage in the ordinary clearance flow; a section 47 licence creates a bonded warehouse — a duty-suspension regime in which goods sit secured until duty or export settles them. Same officer, different regimes, and Module 10 builds on this difference.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A terminal operator applies for a licence, and meets the fourteen-day rule
BODY:
Step 1. The application. A logistics company leases a yard two kilometres from the Port of Tema and wants to deconsolidate import containers there before the goods are declared. The activity it plans — temporary storage of goods imported at a port of entry and not yet released, including goods not yet declared — is the second kind of area in section 46(1), so the company applies to the Commissioner-General for a licence to operate the yard as a customs-controlled area.

Step 2. The perimeter. The licence is granted. From that moment the yard is legally a customs-controlled area under section 4: the Authority has control over the goods, persons and conveyances inside it, and the company's staff work inside a legal perimeter, not merely a fenced compound.

Step 3. The clock. A consignment of machinery is trucked in and its declaration stalls in a valuation query. The company's compliance clerk watches the dates: the goods were entered under a customs procedure, so section 4(3)'s fourteen-day clock is running. On day eleven the clerk writes to the Commissioner-General explaining the delay and attaching the query correspondence.

Step 4. The exception. The explanation is accepted. The goods pass day fourteen without becoming liable to forfeiture, because section 4(3)'s exception exists precisely for a failure to move that is explained to the Commissioner-General's satisfaction.

What does this example demonstrate? The licence, the perimeter and the fourteen-day rule are one connected system: the licence put the goods in a controlled area, and the rule governs how long they may stay.

Key lesson: watch the clock the moment goods are entered, and communicate before it expires — the exception rewards the operator who explains.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Approved places and sufferance wharves at a foreign port
BODY:
Step 1. The scene. At a busy European seaport, two berths sit side by side: one is a designated terminal within the port's approved places; the other is a heritage quay occasionally used for occasional cargo when the main terminals are congested. The port's own legal instruments distinguish them exactly the way Ghana's definitions do.

Step 2. The approved place. The designated berth corresponds to Ghana's approved place of unloading — a quay designated by public notice where imported and export goods may be unloaded or loaded. Cargo worked there moves under the ordinary provisions.

Step 3. The special permission. The heritage quay corresponds to the sufferance wharf concept: a place which is not an approved place, at which goods may be loaded or unloaded only when the customs authority allows it, under conditions it directs, either generally or for a particular case. No general permission, no cargo — the permission is the law, not the practice.

Step 4. The transferable lesson. Whatever the country, the learner now asks the same two questions of any berth, shed or yard: is it an approved place or a specially permitted place, and who designated it. The answers decide which provisions govern the cargo worked there.

What does this example demonstrate? The distinction between designated places and specially permitted places is the international grammar of port control, and Ghana's definitions express it precisely.

Key lesson: before cargo works any place, establish the place's legal status — approved, sufferance, or licensed — because the status determines the rules.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: The definition names the decider
BODY:
When you cannot remember who designates a customs place, do not guess — read the definition. The section 151 definitions carry their designating authority inside their own text: the port, the approved place of unloading and the boarding station are designated by the Minister by notice in the Gazette and at least two daily newspapers; the transit-shed is designated by the Commissioner-General by notice in writing; the customs-controlled area is designated by the Commissioner-General. The publication requirement is itself information: a designation the public must see twice in newspapers is a political act, while a notice in writing to a named area is an administrative one. Reading definitions this way — as assignments of power to a named office — turns the Interpretation section from a glossary into a map of who may decide what.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Liability to forfeiture is not disposal
BODY:
Section 4(3) makes goods that overstay liable to forfeiture to the State unless the failure to move them is explained to the Commissioner-General's satisfaction. Liable is the operative word: the rule creates exposure to forfeiture, not an automatic loss of the goods. The machinery that follows — seizure, claims for seized goods, auction and the State warehouse — is a separate set of provisions with their own procedure, and Module 10 teaches it there. The practical discipline for an operator is therefore twofold: treat day fourteen as a hard deadline to act against, and treat an explanation as a legal remedy to be submitted with evidence, not a conversation to be had. Goods whose owner communicates before the deadline passes do not reach the forfeiture machinery at all.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A port operator wants to publish a notice declaring a new quay "a port for the purposes of the Act". Which option best describes the lawful position under Act 891?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The operator may designate the port by notice in the Gazette, being the person in charge of the area.
2. A port is designated by the Minister, by notice published in the Gazette and at least two daily newspapers of national circulation; a private operator cannot designate it.
3. The Commissioner-General may designate the port in writing, as with transit sheds.
4. No designation is needed, because any place where cargo is worked becomes a port automatically.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 151 of Act 891 defines a port as a place, whether on the coast or elsewhere, designated by the Minister — the Minister responsible for Finance — by notice published in the Gazette and at least two daily newspapers of national circulation, subject to conditions or limitations specified in the notice. The definition places the power in one office and prescribes its publication.

Answer 1 gives the operator a power the definition grants to the Minister; a person in charge of an area may apply for a licence under section 46, but that is a different mechanism with a different result. Answer 3 confuses the two designation tracks: the Commissioner-General's written notices designate transit sheds and customs-controlled areas, not ports, and the publication requirements differ accordingly. Answer 4 dissolves the distinction the definitions exist to draw.

The takeaway: designation powers name their holder; the port is the Minister's, the shed and the controlled area are the Commissioner-General's, and neither can be assumed.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
Inside a customs-controlled area at Takoradi, which of the following activities is NOT within the activities section 5(1) permits?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The loading, unloading, inspection, assessment and clearing of imported or exported goods.
2. The temporary storage of goods not yet released, including goods not yet declared and goods seized and liable to forfeiture.
3. The unrestricted retail sale of the stored goods to walk-in members of the public inside the area.
4. The embarkation, disembarkation or control of persons arriving in or departing from the country.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 5(1) of Act 891 permits four activities in a customs-controlled area: the loading, unloading, inspection, assessment and clearing of imported or exported goods; the temporary storage of goods imported at a port of entry and not yet released, including goods not yet declared and goods seized and liable to forfeiture; the embarkation, disembarkation or control of persons; and the storage and processing of imported goods under bond. A general retail sale to the public is none of these — goods inside a controlled area are under customs control from arrival to release, and they leave by clearance, not by shop counter.

Answers 1, 2 and 4 restate the provision's permitted activities in substance, including the point learners most often miss: storage expressly covers undeclared and seized goods, not merely goods awaiting collection.

The takeaway: a customs-controlled area is a working perimeter for the clearance cycle, not a marketplace; every activity inside it must trace to section 5(1).

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
Goods entered for export have sat in a licensed customs-controlled area for thirteen days, and the exporter now realises the vessel's deadline has slipped by three weeks. Which option best states the position under section 4(3)?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The goods are forfeited automatically on day fourteen, and nothing the exporter does can change that.
2. The fourteen-day rule does not apply, because it covers only goods entered under another customs procedure.
3. On day fourteen the goods become liable to forfeiture to the State unless the failure to move them is explained to the satisfaction of the Commissioner-General — so the exporter should submit an explanation now, with evidence.
4. The rule is suspended whenever the delay is caused by a shipping line's schedule change.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 4(3) of Act 891 provides that where goods remain in a customs-controlled area for more than fourteen days after being entered under another customs procedure, entered for export, or sold by public auction, they are liable to forfeiture to the State unless the failure to move the goods is explained to the satisfaction of the Commissioner-General. Goods entered for export are squarely the second trigger, so day fourteen brings liability unless an explanation is accepted.

Answer 1 skips the exception that is written into the provision itself — liability is not automatic disposal, and an accepted explanation prevents forfeiture. Answer 2 misreads the triggers: entered for export is expressly one of them. Answer 4 invents a suspension that the section does not contain; the remedy the section provides is the explanation, and a commercial excuse may form part of it, but nothing suspends the rule.

The takeaway: the fourteen-day rule rewards early communication — the exporter who explains before the deadline, with evidence, never meets the forfeiture machinery.
