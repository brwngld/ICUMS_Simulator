=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The import sequence as the Act orders it
BODY:
A consignment's legal journey has a fixed order, and each stage is a provision.

It begins with arrival: section 16(1) requires the conveyance to come to a port, or another place allowed by the Commissioner-General in special circumstances, without touching at any other place in the country, and on arriving to proceed quickly to the proper place of mooring or unloading. The master then reports: section 17 provides the report to be made on arrival, and section 18 supplies the consequence of failing to report or account for packages reported.

The goods are discharged: section 21 governs mooring and unloading, under customs supervision.

Then the entry stage: section 48 fixes the time of entry, section 49 the declaration, section 50 the pre-entry, and section 14 the documents to be submitted alongside it.

Then the decision: section 6(1) provides that the Commissioner-General may release declared goods as soon as they have been examined or exempted from examination, where no offence has been found, the required documents have been acquired, and the stated conditions are met.

Release is the goal; delivery follows it. Every stage the learner will ever work sits somewhere on this spine, and the rest of the programme attaches to one stage or another.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The legal moments: time of import, time of entry, time of export
BODY:
Act 891 fixes precise moments, and liability hangs on them.

"Time of import" is defined in section 151 as the time of arrival of a conveyance within the limits of Ghana from outside the country, with the intent to unlade merchandise. "Time of export" is when goods within Ghana are placed on board a conveyance for consignment to a place outside Ghana and the international voyage to that place commences — and for overland exportation, section 97(2) provides that the export occurs when the declaration is passed and the goods cross the boundaries of the country. Between the two sits the entry moment: section 48 governs the time of entry, the point at which the declaration takes legal effect for the goods.

Why the precision matters: the duty charge attaches to goods because they are imported or exported (section 1(1)), so the moment that qualifies the movement as an import or an export is the moment the obligations crystallise — the rate in force, the party liable, and the records that must exist all hang on it. A consignment's paperwork can be right and its timing wrong, and the timing is what the law reads first.

The learner's habit: for any movement, fix the legal moment before fixing anything else.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Declaration, declarant and pre-entry
BODY:
Three definitions carry the entry stage.

A "declaration" is a statutory form filed or to be filed with respect to imported or exported goods at the time of entry or export — a statutory form, which is why its particulars are legal statements and not administrative notes. The "declarant" is the person who makes a declaration for customs purposes: the owner of the goods, the importer, the exporter, or the agent. Those four identities are the only lawful hands the declaration can come from, and each carries its own responsibility for the particulars.

A "pre-entry" is the entry of goods prior to their importation into Ghana. Section 50 provides for it, and its commercial value is speed: the declaration work is done before the vessel arrives, so that release can follow quickly once the goods land and are examined or exempted from examination.

The declaration does not travel alone. Section 14 requires documents to be submitted with it, and section 51 provides the consequences of missing or incomplete documents — the entry stage fails on paperwork before it fails on anything else.

An "electronic declaration" is a declaration made electronically to the Commissioner-General, whether personally or on behalf of any person as agent — the form Module 5 develops.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Transit: movement without duty payment
BODY:
Transit moves goods through Ghana without bringing them into home consumption. Section 95(1) provides that a person may, under the transit procedure, move goods imported into the country from one port of entry to another port within the country in three lawful ways. Note the Act's word for the goods themselves: "cargo", defined in section 151 as goods other than stores or passenger or crew baggage — the movement procedures are written for cargo, and stores follow their own provisions.

First, under a bond or by a bonded carrier, or by both, without the payment of duty — the duty is secured rather than paid, and a bonded carrier is defined in section 151 as a carrier licensed to transport bonded goods within the country.

Second, in accordance with the Customs Convention on the International Transport of Goods under Cover of TIR Carnets, where the movement began or is to end outside Ghana, or runs between two points in Ghana through the territory of another country — the Act incorporating an international convention for corridor movements.

Third, in accordance with the ATA carnet and temporary-admission conventions.

The unifying idea is control without collection: the goods stay under customs procedure, the revenue is secured by bond or convention guarantee, and the goods must reach the declared destination for the procedure to close. Transit is not an import into free circulation, and the duty question is deferred, not cancelled.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Transhipment: the same-port transfer
BODY:
Transhipment is transit's compact cousin. Section 96(1) provides that a person may transfer goods that are imported from an importing conveyance to an exporting conveyance within the same port of entry, without payment of duty — no inland movement at all, which is why the security is structural: the goods never leave the port.

The person who declares the goods and places them under the transhipment procedure is responsible, under section 96(2), for the unloading of the goods and securing them in a customs-controlled area approved for that purpose; for compliance with any measures required to ensure the identification of the goods; and for their loading onto the exporting conveyance.

The contrast with transit is the lesson: transit moves goods between ports under bond or convention; transhipment moves them between conveyances inside one port, under the declarant's personal responsibility for identification and secure handling. Both defer duty; both require the goods to leave as declared; neither places the goods in free circulation.

A container discharged from a feeder vessel and loaded onto a larger vessel for Europe, without ever entering Ghanaian commerce, is transhipment working exactly as section 96 designed it.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: When the lifecycle fails: uncleared goods and the State warehouse
BODY:
The lifecycle's failure mode has its own provisions, and they begin gently.

Section 52 deals with uncleared goods: goods that have not been cleared after the lawful period, which the Commissioner-General may cause to be removed to a place of security — and "uncustomed goods" is the defined state that marks them: goods liable to duty on which the full duties due have not been paid, and goods, whether liable to duty or not, imported, exported or dealt with contrary to the Act's customs provisions.

Section 53 then provides the deposit of goods in the State warehouse — defined in section 151 as a place approved by the Commissioner-General for the deposit of uncustomed, un-entered, un-examined, abandoned, detained or seized goods, for the security of the goods or the payment of duties that may be due. The definition's list is worth reading twice: uncustomed, un-entered, un-examined, abandoned, detained or seized — six ways goods can end up in the State's keeping.

The deposit is not the end of the story; what follows — auction, allocation and the disposal machinery — is Module 10's territory. What this lesson requires is the connection: the lifecycle that ends in non-clearance hands the goods to a defined place, under a defined provision.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A frozen-mackerel container clears Tema, end to end
BODY:
Step 1. Before arrival. A Ghanaian importer's agent lodges a pre-entry for a container of frozen mackerel still at sea — section 50 allows entry before importation, so the declaration work is finished before the vessel berths. Section 14's documents travel with it: the bill of lading, the commercial invoice, the packing list.

Step 2. Arrival and report. The reefer vessel crosses the border — time of import under section 151 — comes directly to the Port of Tema, and the master makes the report section 17 requires. The vessel moors at the terminal, and the container is discharged under section 21's supervision.

Step 3. The entry takes effect. The pre-entry becomes the goods' declaration at the time of entry under section 48. The declarant is the agent, acting for the importer; the particulars match the documents, and nothing in section 51's missing-documents list applies.

Step 4. The control decision. Under the risk-based control of section 3, the consignment is selected for document check only — no physical examination. Section 6(1) applies: the goods have been exempted from examination, no offence is found, the required documents are acquired.

Step 5. Release and delivery. The Commissioner-General releases the goods; the duty, import VAT and charges are settled on the customs value; the terminal releases the container against the cleared declaration, and the reefer leaves the port.

What does this example demonstrate? The whole sequence — pre-entry, arrival, report, discharge, entry, control, release — is one spine of provisions, and each step satisfies the next.

Key lesson: preparation before arrival is why fast clearances exist; the pre-entry turns waiting time into processing time.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A bonded transit movement crosses Ghana to a landlocked buyer
BODY:
Step 1. The corridor. A freight forwarder moves a consignment of machinery from a European port, discharged at Tema, for a buyer in a landlocked country beyond Ghana's northern border. The goods will not enter Ghanaian commerce: the movement is transit under section 95(1).

Step 2. The security. The forwarder lodges the transit declaration with the bond in place — the first lawful mode in section 95(1)(a): movement under a bond or by a bonded carrier, or both, without payment of duty. The carrier transporting the goods is licensed: a bonded carrier, defined in section 151.

Step 3. The identification. The goods are sealed and the accompanying documents identify the consignment; the responsible person ensures the identification measures comply, exactly as the transhipment provisions in section 96(2) expect of their declarants — the same discipline applies.

Step 4. The journey and the exit. The truck crosses Ghana without the goods entering free circulation. At the border post, the customs administration confirms the goods leaving the country match the goods that entered: the transit procedure is acquitted, the security is released, and section 110's guarantee machinery closes it.

What does this example demonstrate? Transit is import's opposite twin: the same control spine, with duty secured instead of collected, and acquittal instead of release into the market.

Key lesson: corridor trade lives on identification and acquittal — the goods must be provably the same at exit as they were at entry.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: The legal moment decides the legal question
BODY:
Because duty attaches to goods when they are imported or exported (section 1(1)), the fixed moments in section 151 — time of import, time of export — are not calendar trivia; they are the points at which obligations, rates and records attach. A consignment that arrives under one tariff schedule and is declared under another is resolved by asking which moment controls. The same discipline reaches storage: goods waiting in a customs-controlled area are still under customs control, so time keeps running for provisions like the fourteen-day rule from section 4(3), which the earlier lessons introduced. The professional habit is to date-stamp every movement with its legal moment — arrival, entry, export — before analysing anything else about it, and to keep those dates in the file the records provisions require.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: The State warehouse is a stage, not a sentence
BODY:
Section 53's State warehouse deposit sounds final, but it is a stage in a procedure, not the end of the goods. The deposit secures the goods and the duties that may be due; what follows — the auction machinery, the claims process for seized goods, and the disposal rules — runs on its own provisions with its own protections, and Module 10 teaches them. The reason this lesson stops at the deposit is scope discipline: a learner who conflates deposit with disposal will misadvise a client whose goods have merely been moved to security, and a client told the wrong stage of a procedure acts at the wrong time. Deposit means the State holds the goods under a defined provision; it does not mean the State has finished with them, and it does not mean the owner's remedies are gone.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A vessel carrying containers bound for Tema suffers a delay and waits three days at an anchorage before berthing. The agent's pre-entry has already been lodged. Which option best states when time of import began for the goods?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. At the moment the pre-entry was lodged, because the declaration fixes the import.
2. When the vessel was built, because the goods were always destined for Ghana.
3. At the time of arrival of the conveyance within the limits of Ghana from outside the country with the intent to unlade merchandise — the section 151 definition — regardless of the berthing delay.
4. Only when the goods are released, because importation is complete on release.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 151 of Act 891 defines time of import as the time of arrival of a conveyance within the limits of Ghana from outside the country with the intent to unlade merchandise. The moment belongs to the conveyance's crossing, not to the paperwork or the berth: the anchorage delay changes when unloading begins, but the goods were imported when the vessel crossed into Ghana with intent to unlade.

Answer 1 confuses the declaration with the import: the pre-entry is lodged before importation by design — section 50's whole purpose — so it cannot also be the import moment. Answer 2 is absurd on its face. Answer 4 confuses importation with release: release is the end of customs control, not the definition of importation, and the duty obligations attach well before it.

The takeaway: the Act fixes the moment by the conveyance's crossing with intent to unlade — date-stamp that moment first, and the paperwork's timing follows its own provisions.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
A consolidator at the Port of Tema discharges forty containers from a feeder vessel and loads thirty of them directly onto a larger vessel bound for Antwerp, without the goods ever entering Ghanaian commerce. Which option best identifies the procedure?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Transit under section 95, because the goods leave Ghana without duty being paid.
2. Transhipment under section 96: goods transferred from an importing conveyance to an exporting conveyance within the same port of entry, without payment of duty, with the declarant responsible for unloading, securing, identification and loading.
3. Home use under section 74, because the goods were declared on arrival.
4. Pre-entry under section 50, because the goods were declared before the vessel arrived.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 96(1) of Act 891 provides that a person may transfer goods that are imported from an importing conveyance to an exporting conveyance within the same port of entry, without payment of duty — which is exactly the feeder-to-ocean transfer described. Section 96(2) places the responsibility for unloading, securing in an approved customs-controlled area, identification, and loading on the person who declared the goods under the procedure.

Answer 1 misidentifies the geography: transit moves goods from one port of entry to another within the country, under bond or bonded carrier — an inland movement, not a same-port transfer. Answer 3 contradicts the facts: goods in transhipment never enter home use, which is precisely why no duty is paid. Answer 4 confuses a lodging mechanism with a procedure; the pre-entry may have been lodged, but the goods' legal state is transhipment.

The takeaway: same port, two conveyances, no duty — transhipment. Cross-country, secured movement — transit. The geography identifies the procedure.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
An importer abandons a consignment of furniture at the port and stops responding. The goods have not been cleared within the lawful period. Which option best describes what follows under Act 891?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The goods are destroyed immediately, because abandoned goods have no legal protection.
2. The goods become uncustomed goods and the Commissioner-General may cause them to be removed to a place of security, with deposit in the State warehouse under section 53 — a stage that secures the goods and the duties due, with the further machinery following its own provisions.
3. The goods revert to the shipping line automatically, and customs takes no further interest.
4. The goods are released to the port operator as compensation for storage costs.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 52 of Act 891 deals with uncleared goods, and the goods in the scenario have entered the state section 151 defines as uncustomed: goods liable to duty on which the full duties due have not been paid, and goods dealt with contrary to the customs provisions whether liable to duty or not. Section 53 then provides the deposit of goods in the State warehouse — defined as the place approved by the Commissioner-General for the deposit of uncustomed, un-entered, un-examined, abandoned, detained or seized goods, for the security of the goods or the payment of duties that may be due. Abandoned goods are expressly in that list.

Answer 1 skips the entire procedure that protects both the revenue and the trader's residual rights. Answers 3 and 4 invent outcomes with no statutory basis — the shipping line's liabilities and the port's charges are separate matters governed elsewhere.

The takeaway: non-clearance moves goods along a defined path — uncleared, uncustomed, deposited — and each stage has its own provision; advise the client before the path is chosen for them.
