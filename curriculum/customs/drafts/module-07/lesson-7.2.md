=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The GRI: the legal method, incorporated by definition
BODY:
Classification is decided by a legal method, and the method is inside the Act. Section 151's definition of the Harmonised Commodity Description and Coding System includes "the general rules for the interpretation of the Harmonised System" in the nomenclature the Act incorporates — so the General Rules for Interpretation bind in Ghana because the definition names them.

The GRI's role is to decide where goods land when the schedule's words could be read more than one way. The rules are applied in order: GRI 1 first, then 2, then 3, and so on — a later rule operates only where the earlier rules leave the goods unclassified. The workbook's own text confirms the notes' and rules' place: the schedules carry the chapter notes, and the Act's definition carries the rules.

The verified wording this lesson quotes comes from the WCO's published General Rules text, checked 2026-11-17 against the WCO and IMF tariff-teaching sources — cited rule by rule. The learner's discipline begins here: quote the rule, cite the source, apply it in order.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: GRI 1: the headings and the notes decide first
BODY:
GRI 1, quoted from the verified text: the titles of sections, chapters and sub-chapters are provided for ease of reference only; for legal purposes, classification shall be determined according to the terms of the headings and any relative section or chapter notes and, provided such headings or notes do not otherwise require, according to the following provisions.

Two teachings sit in the rule's first sentence. The titles are signposts: Chapter 2's title, "Meat and edible meat offal", points the learner to the chapter, but the title is not the legal test. The headings and the notes are: the heading's own words — "Meat of bovine animals, fresh or chilled" — and the chapter's notes — Table 4's Note 1 exclusions — are what legally decide.

The rule's final clause is its engine: "provided such headings or notes do not otherwise require" — the rules that follow operate only where the headings and notes leave the question open. GRI 1 is therefore the method's first and hardest step: read the heading's terms and the notes completely, and only if they leave the goods unplaced do the later rules engage.

The workbook's Chapter 2 note is the local demonstration: its exclusions move goods the title would gather.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: GRI 2: the incomplete, the unassembled, the mixed
BODY:
GRI 2, quoted from the verified text, has two limbs.

Limb (a): any reference in a heading to an article shall be taken to include a reference to that article incomplete or unfinished, provided that, as entered, the incomplete or unfinished article has the essential character of the complete or finished article; it shall also include a reference to that article complete or finished, entered unassembled or disassembled.

Limb (b): any reference in a heading to a material or substance shall be taken to include a reference to mixtures or combinations of that material or substance with other materials or substances; and any reference to goods of a given material or substance shall be taken also to include goods composed wholly or partly of such material or substance.

The limbs widen the headings: the unassembled machine is classified as the machine; the mixture containing the named substance reaches the substance's heading. Limb (b)'s final sentence routes the mixed goods' classification to GRI 3(b) — the essential-character rule — which is the bridge between the two rules.

The learner's reading: GRI 2 answers the goods that are not quite what the heading says — unfinished, unassembled, mixed — and widens the heading to reach them, subject to the essential character.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: GRI 3: the competing headings, resolved
BODY:
GRI 3, quoted from the verified text, resolves the goods that are prima facie classifiable under two or more headings, in three steps.

Step (a): the heading which provides the most specific description shall be preferred to headings providing a more general description — with the rule's own qualification: where two or more headings each refer to part only of the materials in mixed or composite goods, or to part only of the items in a set put up for retail sale, those headings are regarded as equally specific.

Step (b): mixtures, composite goods, and retail sets which cannot be classified by 3(a) shall be classified as if they consisted of the material or component which gives them their essential character, insofar as this criterion is applicable.

Step (c): where the goods cannot be classified by 3(a) or 3(b), they shall be classified under the heading which occurs last in numerical order among those which equally merit consideration.

The steps are exhaustive and ordered: specificity first, essential character second, last-in-order third. The learner applies them in that order and stops at the first that decides.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: GRI 6: the subheading comparison
BODY:
GRI 6 governs the descent into the subheadings, and it is the rule the TSN classification lives by. Quoted from the verified text: for legal purposes, the classification of goods in the subheadings of a heading shall be determined according to the terms of those subheadings and any related subheading notes and, mutatis mutandis, to the above rules, on the understanding that only subheadings at the same level are comparable; and the relative section and chapter notes also apply, unless the context otherwise requires.

Two teachings sit in the rule. The subheading's terms and notes decide at that level, with the earlier rules applied mutatis mutandis — the same method, one level down. And only subheadings at the same level are comparable: a dash-one subheading is compared with its dash-one siblings, never with a dash-two row, until the level's comparison has placed the goods.

The workbook's bovine-meat rows demonstrate: heading 2.01's dash rows (carcasses, cuts with bone in, boneless) are the first level; the goods' state — boneless — is compared at that level, and the TSN beneath it carries the rates.

GRI 6 is why the descent of lesson 7.1 is legal and not merely visual: the comparison's legality is the rule's own words.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The notes: their force demonstrated in the workbook
BODY:
The section and chapter notes bind at GRI 1's level, and the workbook carries a real note to demonstrate. Table 4's Chapter 2 Note 1, quoted from the workbook: this Chapter does not cover products of the kinds described in headings 02.01 to 02.08 or 02.10 unfit or unsuitable for human consumption; guts, bladders or stomachs of animals (heading 05.04) or animal blood (heading 05.11 or 30.02); or animal fat, other than products of heading 02.09 (Chapter 15).

The note does three things a classification must respect: it excludes goods from the chapter by their condition (unfit for human consumption); it re-routes goods by naming the headings they belong to (05.04, 05.11, 30.02); and it keeps one product in by exception (animal fat of heading 02.09). A classification that matched the heading text but ignored the note would be legally wrong at GRI 1 — the note is part of the terms classification is determined by.

The learner's method: the notes are read as part of the heading's terms, and Table 7's Chapter 3 note (excluding unfit fish and caviar to their proper headings) demonstrates the same force in the fish chapter.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Boneless bovine meat, classified by the rules
BODY:
Step 1. The goods. Boneless fresh beef, imported for the Ghanaian market. The question: which TSN?

Step 2. GRI 1. The chapter title points to Chapter 2; the heading's terms decide: 2.01, "Meat of bovine animals, fresh or chilled" — the goods' description matches. Chapter 2's Note 1 is read: the goods are fit for human consumption, not within the note's exclusions — the chapter covers them.

Step 3. The subheading level. GRI 6 governs the descent: heading 2.01's dash rows are compared at their level — carcasses and half-carcasses (0201.10.00.00), other cuts with bone in (0201.20.00.00), boneless (0201.30.00.00). The goods' state is boneless: the dash-row terms place them on the third row.

Step 4. The TSN. The row 0201.30.00.00 carries the answer: SU Kg, CET ID 35, VAT 15 — figures labelled illustrative of the structure, verify the rate in force.

Step 5. The rule audit. Every step names its rule: GRI 1's headings and notes; GRI 6's same-level comparison. No later rule was needed, because GRI 1 and 6 decided.

What does this example demonstrate? The rules in order, the descent at level, the row as the landing — classification is a rule audit, not a lookup.

Key lesson: each step names its rule; the classification that cannot name its rule is not yet a classification.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A mixture abroad: GRI 3(b)'s essential character
BODY:
Step 1. The goods. A foreign trader imports a gift set put up for retail sale: a stainless-steel thermos flask with a set of branded ceramic mugs packed in one box. Both components appear in different headings.

Step 2. GRI 1 and 3(a). The headings' terms each describe part of the set — the flask's heading speaks to vacuum vessels, the mugs' heading to ceramic tableware. Under 3(a)'s own qualification, headings each referring to part only of the items in a set put up for retail sale are regarded as equally specific — so specificity does not decide.

Step 3. GRI 3(b). The set is classified as if it consisted of the component giving it its essential character. The trader's assessment — the flask's function and value dominate the set's purpose — places the essential character in the flask, and the set takes the flask's heading.

Step 4. The discipline. The essential-character finding is reasoned and documented: which component, why it dominates, on what the trader relies. The finding is the classification's justification.

What does this example demonstrate? GRI 3's steps in order — 3(a)'s equal-specificity trap, 3(b)'s essential character as the decider — applied to a set, with the reasoning documented.

Key lesson: the essential character is a finding, not an intuition — and the finding's file is what survives the officer's review.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
Goods enter that are an unassembled complete bookcase. The heading for assembled bookcases exists. Which option best states the classification route under the verified GRI text?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The unassembled bookcase cannot be classified, because the heading covers only assembled furniture.
2. GRI 2(a) applies: any reference in a heading to an article includes that article complete or finished entered unassembled or disassembled — so the unassembled bookcase takes the assembled bookcase's heading.
3. The bookcase is classified under the heading for its parts, because unassembled goods are always parts.
4. GRI 3(c) applies, and the bookcase takes the last heading in numerical order.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. GRI 2(a), quoted from the verified text: any reference in a heading to an article shall be taken to include a reference to that article complete or finished entered unassembled or disassembled. The rule widens the assembled-furniture heading to reach the unassembled bookcase, provided the goods are the article entered unassembled — which the scenario states.

Answer 1 reads the heading's words without GRI 2's widening — the rule exists precisely for goods entered other than assembled. Answer 3 inverts the rule: unassembled goods are classified as the article, not as parts. Answer 4 reaches for GRI 3(c), which operates only where goods are prima facie classifiable under two or more headings and 3(a) and 3(b) have not decided — not where GRI 2 has placed the goods in one heading.

The takeaway: GRI 2 is the incomplete, the unfinished, the unassembled and the mixed — and it widens the heading before any competition question arises.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
A retail set contains a stainless-steel flask and two ceramic mugs, packed for retail sale. The flask's heading and the mugs' heading each describe part only of the set. Under the verified GRI text, which route decides the set's classification?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. GRI 3(a) decides by the most specific description, which is the flask's heading.
2. GRI 3(a)'s own qualification applies — headings each referring to part only of the items in a retail set are equally specific — so 3(a) does not decide; GRI 3(b) classifies the set as if it consisted of the component giving it its essential character, insofar as applicable.
3. GRI 3(c) decides immediately, because retail sets are always classified last in numerical order.
4. GRI 6 decides, because retail sets are a subheading question.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. GRI 3(a)'s qualification is in the verified text: when two or more headings each refer to part only of the materials or substances contained in mixed or composite goods or to part only of the items in a set put up for retail sale, those headings are to be regarded as referring with equal specificity to such goods, even if one gives a more complete or precise description. The scenario's set is exactly that case — so specificity does not decide, and the route moves to 3(b): the set is classified as if it consisted of the material or component which gives it its essential character, insofar as the criterion is applicable.

Answer 1 ignores the qualification and applies 3(a) where the rule says it does not decide. Answer 3 skips 3(b) — 3(c) operates only where 3(a) and 3(b) have not classified the goods. Answer 4 confuses the heading-level competition with the subheading descent GRI 6 governs.

The takeaway: GRI 3's steps are ordered and exhaustive — equal specificity first, essential character second, last-in-order third — and the essential-character finding is reasoned and documented.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A classification places goods under a subheading by comparing a dash-one row with a dash-two row's terms. Which option best states the position under GRI 6?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The comparison is valid: all rows in the heading are comparable at once.
2. The comparison is invalid: GRI 6 provides that only subheadings at the same level are comparable — the dash-one row is compared with its dash-one siblings, and the descent to dash-two happens only after the dash-one level has placed the goods; the related subheading notes and the earlier rules apply mutatis mutandis.
3. The comparison is valid if the goods' value matches the row's rates.
4. GRI 6 does not govern subheadings; it governs only chapters.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. GRI 6, quoted from the verified text: the classification of goods in the subheadings of a heading shall be determined according to the terms of those subheadings and any related subheading notes and, mutatis mutandis, to the above rules, on the understanding that only subheadings at the same level are comparable. The scenario's cross-level comparison — a dash-one row's terms against a dash-two row's — is precisely what the rule's comparability clause forbids: the dash-one level's siblings decide first, and the descent happens level by level.

Answer 1 reads the schedule visually; GRI 6 makes the descent legal, level by level. Answer 3 invents a value test that belongs to valuation, not classification. Answer 4 misstates the rule's scope — GRI 6 is the subheading rule, mutatis mutandis applying the earlier rules at each level.

The takeaway: the descent is level by level, siblings only — GRI 6 is what makes the workbook's dash structure a legal method rather than a layout.
