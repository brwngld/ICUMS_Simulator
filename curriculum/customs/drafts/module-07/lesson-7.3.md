=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The classification workflow: five steps in order
BODY:
A classification is a workflow, and the steps run in order.

Step one, the product description: what the goods actually are — composition, function, form, packaging — written precisely enough that a stranger could find them. Step two, the candidate headings: the schedule's headings whose terms could cover the description. Step three, the notes check: the section and chapter notes read against each candidate — Table 4's Chapter 2 note is the model, excluding goods the title would gather. Step four, the GRI applied: the rules in order, from GRI 1's headings-and-notes through the descent of GRI 6. Step five, the TSN: the row the descent lands on, whose columns carry the rates.

The order is the method: a learner who starts at step five — looking for a TSN that "seems right" — is guessing, and the guess is what the officer's review tests. The learner who runs the steps can show the work: description, candidates, notes, rules, row — each step's output feeding the next.

Section 12's advance-ruling machinery is the workflow's institutional echo: the statute's own request requirements (the next blocks) demand exactly the description the workflow's step one produces.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: What makes a description classification-grade
BODY:
The workflow's first step is where classifications are won or lost, and the Act itself states what a classification-grade description contains. Section 12(6)(a) — the advance-ruling provision's own list for classification requests — requires a complete description of the merchandise including: the packing weight; the chemical analysis; the description of the goods; the production and expiration date; the name and brand of the goods; the physical description; the function of the goods; the composition of the goods; and the characteristics of components.

Read the list as the workflow's template. The composition and chemical analysis speak to what the goods are made of — the material questions GRI 2(b) and 3(b) ask. The function speaks to what the goods do — the purpose questions the headings' terms ask. The physical description and the packing weight speak to form and quantity. The production and expiration dates speak to condition. The name and brand identify the product in the market.

A description carrying these elements is classification-grade: every question the headings, the notes and the rules ask can be answered from it. A description that is only a trade name is the opposite — and the statute's own list is the standard the learner's step one is measured against.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The advance ruling: the Act's own provision
BODY:
Section 12 provides the advance ruling, and its words are the provision's teaching. Subsection (1): the Commissioner-General may issue a written ruling applying the customs law to a particular set of facts submitted by an interested party. Subsection (2) defines the interested party: a person, or authorised agent of a person, who has a demonstrable interest in the questions presented, and an importer or exporter. Subsection (3): the ruling may relate to the tariff classification, customs value, country of origin of the goods, or to any other activity to which the customs law applies. Subsection (4): an interested party may request the ruling from the Commissioner-General.

The provision's scope is the lesson's scope: classification is one of the ruling's subjects, and the ruling's written form is its force — a written statement of the law's application to stated facts, issued before the transaction. The learner's reading: the advance ruling is the classification workflow's institutional version — the same facts, the same method, decided in advance and in writing.

Where the provision defers detail to the Regulations, the lesson writes "as prescribed" — the same discipline the deferral family taught.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The request's contents: what section 12(5)-(6) demands
BODY:
Section 12(5) states the request's base contents: the request shall be in writing and include a statement of all relevant facts; the names and addresses of interested parties; the name of the port where the goods are expected to arrive or depart; and a description of the transaction in sufficient detail to allow the application of the customs laws.

Subsection (6) adds the classification request's extra requirements: in addition to subsection (5)'s contents, the request shall include a complete description of the merchandise — the packing weight, the chemical analysis, the description of the goods, the production and expiration date, the name and brand of the goods, the physical description, the function of the goods, the composition of the goods, and the characteristics of components.

The subsection (6) list is the classification-grade description from the earlier block, in the statute's own words — the statute demands for a ruling request exactly what the workflow demands for a classification. The learner's discipline: a ruling request assembled to subsection (6)'s list is a request the facts can be decided on; anything less is a request the ruling cannot lawfully answer.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: When a trader seeks a ruling
BODY:
The advance ruling's commercial logic is uncertainty priced in advance. The trader seeks the ruling when the goods are new or unusual — a composition the schedule's headings do not obviously cover; a product whose function crosses two headings' terms; a mixture whose essential character is arguable. In each case, the trader's choice is between declaring on a guess (and living with the assessment's consequences) or asking the law's question before the goods ship.

The ruling's subjects — section 12(3) — cover the trader's three technical questions: tariff classification, customs value, country of origin. The classification ruling is this module's subject; the valuation and origin rulings are Modules 8 and 9's, where their provisions are taught.

The learner's advice formula: where the goods' classification is genuinely uncertain and the transaction is prospective, the ruling is the lawful route to certainty — requested in writing, under subsection (5)'s and (6)'s contents, with the facts complete. The alternative — declaring and objecting later — is lesson 7.4's dispute machinery, at the cost of a dispute.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The ruling's force: binding on the stated facts
BODY:
Section 151's definition gives the ruling its name and nature: a "customs advance ruling" is a binding written statement issued by customs that interprets and applies customs laws and regulations to a specific set of facts upon application by a trade about a prospective transaction.

Three limits are inside the definition, and they are the ruling's proper use. Binding: the ruling holds — the trader may rely on it. Specific set of facts: the ruling's force is exactly as wide as the facts stated in the request; a new composition, a different process, a changed product is a new question. Prospective transaction: the ruling speaks to what will be imported, not to disputes about what was.

The learner's practice: a client relying on a ruling is advised to check the facts match — the ruling's facts are the ruling's boundary — and to keep the ruling in the consignment's file, because the officer's check reads it against the goods. A ruling applied beyond its facts is not a ruling; it is a misstatement with a stamp.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A ruling request for a new product composition
BODY:
Step 1. The uncertainty. A Ghanaian manufacturer develops a new snack bar: a baked cereal base with a yoghurt coating and dried fruit inclusions. The schedule's headings could be read toward cereals, toward yoghurt products, or toward confectionery — the composition is genuinely novel for classification.

Step 2. The decision to ask. The trader's transaction is prospective — the product will be exported to a partner and imported back for packaging — and the classification's uncertainty is real. The agent advises the advance ruling: the lawful route to certainty before the goods move.

Step 3. The request assembled. To section 12(5)'s contents: the written request, the statement of all relevant facts, the parties' names and addresses, the port of arrival, the transaction's description. To subsection (6)'s list: the complete merchandise description — packing weight, the chemical analysis of the bar, the physical description, the function, the composition (cereal base, yoghurt coating, fruit inclusions with percentages), the components' characteristics, the production and expiration dates, the name and brand.

Step 4. The ruling applied. The Commissioner-General issues the written ruling applying the customs law to the stated facts. The trader classifies the product under the ruling for the prospective transactions, and the ruling's paper travels in the consignment's file.

Step 5. The boundary kept. When the trader later changes the composition — a new coating — the agent advises a new request: the ruling's facts are the old composition, and the new facts are a new question.

What does this example demonstrate? The ruling request is the classification-grade description, assembled to the statute's own list — and the ruling's force is exactly its facts.

Key lesson: the ruling is certainty for stated facts; the facts change, the certainty lapses — and the file carries both.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A binding ruling abroad, and its limits
BODY:
Step 1. The scene. At a European administration, an importer requests a binding tariff information decision for a composite machine — a packaging machine with an integrated printer. The application carries the machine's technical documentation: function, composition, the components' characteristics.

Step 2. The ruling issued. The administration issues its written decision: the machine's classification, with the reasoning under the GRI — the function's dominance, the heading's terms, the notes' application.

Step 3. The limits in operation. A second importer later relies on the first's decision for a machine with a different printer type — and the administration distinguishes the cases: the facts differ, the decision's facts do not cover the new machine. The second importer requests its own decision.

Step 4. The transfer. The pattern is section 12's: the ruling binds on its facts; the new facts are the new request. The learner who understands the specificity limit reads any ruling correctly — as an answer to stated facts, not as a general licence.

What does this example demonstrate? The ruling's force is real and bounded: binding on the facts, prospective in application — and the boundary is enforced by the administrations that issue them.

Key lesson: a ruling is an answer, not a doctrine — and the facts are what the answer belongs to.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: The ruling binds the facts — new facts, new ruling
BODY:
Section 151's definition and section 12's words both fix the ruling's boundary to its facts: a binding written statement interpreting and applying the customs law to a specific set of facts. The learner's advice discipline is the boundary's enforcement: before a client relies on a ruling, the client's goods are checked against the ruling's stated facts — the composition, the function, the process. A match means the ruling applies; a difference means a new request. The failure mode is the extended reliance: a ruling for the 2024 composition applied to the 2026 reformulation, discovered at an audit or a border query. The ruling's paper in the file is not the protection; the facts' match is — and the file that notes both is the file the audit trusts.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Rulings are prospective; objections are retrospective
BODY:
The advance ruling and the objection are different instruments for different moments, and the learner's advice picks by the moment. Section 12's ruling speaks to a prospective transaction — the facts are submitted before the goods move, and the certainty is bought before the border. Section 13's objection speaks to a decision already made — the assessment the trader disagrees with, already taken. The confusion's cost is real: a trader with an uncertain classification for a future shipment who waits to object at the border has bought a dispute instead of a ruling; a trader who requests a ruling for goods already assessed has asked the wrong instrument. The lesson's formula: prospective facts, ruling (s.12); existing decision, objection (s.13) — and Module 14's dispute machinery extends the ladder beyond both.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A trader requests an advance ruling for a classification but submits only the product's trade name and a brochure photograph. Which option best states the position under section 12(6)?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The request is complete: the trade name identifies the product in the market.
2. The request is incomplete: section 12(6)(a) requires a complete description of the merchandise — the packing weight, the chemical analysis, the description, the production and expiration date, the name and brand, the physical description, the function, the composition, and the characteristics of components — and a trade name with a photograph carries almost none of it.
3. The request is complete because photographs are better than descriptions.
4. The request is incomplete only if the goods are restricted.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 12(6)(a) of Act 891 states the classification request's description requirements in its own words: a complete description of the merchandise including the packing weight, the chemical analysis, the description of the goods, the production and expiration date, the name and brand of the goods, the physical description, the function of the goods, the composition of the goods, and the characteristics of components. The list is the classification-grade description — the statute demanding for a ruling request exactly what the workflow's step one produces.

Answer 1 substitutes market identification for the merchandise description; the trade name tells the ruling nothing about composition or function. Answer 3 prefers a photograph over the statutory elements — the photograph may show form, but the chemical analysis and composition are stated requirements. Answer 4 invents a restricted-goods condition; subsection (6) applies to classification requests as such.

The takeaway: the ruling request is measured against subsection (6)'s own list — the statute's template for a description the facts can be decided on.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
A client relies on a ruling obtained for a snack bar composition, then reformulates the product with a new coating. The agent advises continued reliance on the ruling. Which option best evaluates the advice?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The advice is correct: the ruling binds the product's classification permanently.
2. The advice is wrong: the ruling binds on the specific set of facts stated in the request — section 151's definition and section 12's words — and a reformulated product presents new facts, which require a new request.
3. The advice is wrong because rulings last only six months.
4. The advice is correct if the coating's percentage is small.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 151 of Act 891 defines the customs advance ruling as a binding written statement interpreting and applying the customs laws to a specific set of facts upon application by a trade about a prospective transaction — and section 12(1) states the same boundary in the provision's own words: a written ruling applying the customs law to a particular set of facts submitted by an interested party. The reformulation's new coating is a new fact set; the ruling's force does not extend to facts it was not issued on.

Answer 1 converts a facts-bound ruling into a permanent licence — the boundary is the definition's own words. Answer 3 invents a six-month validity that no provision states; the ruling's life is its facts'. Answer 4 adds a materiality test for the reliance that the boundary does not carry — the new facts are a new question, whatever the percentage.

The takeaway: the ruling's facts are the ruling's boundary — the client's check before reliance is the goods against the stated facts, and the new facts are the new request.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A trader's goods have already been assessed at a classification the trader disagrees with. The trader asks for "an advance ruling to fix the assessment". Which option best states the correct instrument and route?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The advance ruling is the correct instrument: it can overturn an existing assessment.
2. The objection under section 13 is the correct instrument: the advance ruling addresses prospective transactions — section 151's definition — while the disputed assessment is an existing decision, whose remedy is the objection machinery the Act provides.
3. No remedy exists once an assessment has been made.
4. The correct instrument is the bonded warehouse procedure.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. The lesson's prospective-versus-retrospective distinction carries the answer. Section 151 defines the advance ruling as a statement applying the customs laws to a specific set of facts about a prospective transaction — and section 12(3) states its subjects (classification, value, origin) for facts submitted in advance. An existing assessment is a decision already taken: its remedy is the objection machinery of section 13 — the dispute route Module 14 extends — not the prospective ruling.

Answer 1 gives the ruling a retrospective force its definition refuses. Answer 3 is false: section 13's objection exists for exactly this disagreement. Answer 4 confuses a warehousing procedure with a dispute instrument — the goods' storage does not address the classification's legality.

The takeaway: prospective facts get rulings (s.12); existing decisions get objections (s.13) — and the moment of the question picks the instrument.
