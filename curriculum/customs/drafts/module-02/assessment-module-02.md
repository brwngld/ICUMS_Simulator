MODULE 2 ASSESSMENT — Trade, Shipping and Clearance Documents
12 questions, 1 point each (platform default pass mark 70%). Questions are arranged in lesson order: Q1-3 test lesson 2.1, Q4-6 lesson 2.2, Q7-9 lesson 2.3, Q10-12 lesson 2.4.

Q6  — lesson 2.2 — Term: false statement (s.151) — materiality and omission; Important Note: assemble the file before lodging, keep it past release.
Q7  — lesson 2.3 — Objective: state the transport document's function per mode (bill of lading for sea, air waybill for air) and the three checks it feeds.
Q8  — lesson 2.3 — Objective: explain what a certificate of origin evidences and why a preference claim without one is unevidenced; hook: s.70.

=== ASSESSMENT Q1 | tests lesson 2.1 ===
QUESTION:
An officer at Kotoka holds a power expressed in a provision about inspecting "a ship". A cargo aircraft is on the apron with undeclared goods aboard. A colleague says the power covers the aircraft "because both are conveyances carrying trade". Which option best evaluates the colleague's reasoning?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The reasoning is correct: any craft carrying goods for trade is a ship within the meaning of the definition, whatever it flies on.
2. The reasoning is correct, provided the aircraft is registered for commercial cargo.
3. The reasoning fails: a ship is defined as a floating craft of every description but does not include aircraft, and aircraft are separately defined — so a ship power reaches only what the ship definition covers, and an aircraft inspection needs its own provision.
4. The reasoning fails because customs has no aircraft examination powers anywhere in the law.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 151 of Act 891 defines a ship as a floating craft of every description, with an express exclusion: but does not include aircraft. Aircraft are separately defined — an apparatus, whether or not mechanically propelled, used for the transport by air of human beings or goods. The colleague's argument runs on everyday word width; the law runs on the defined width, and the defined width of "ship" stops short of the apron.

Answer 1 repeats the error the exclusion exists to prevent — "every description" is bounded by its own carve-out. Answer 2 invents a registration test that appears in no definition; the dividing line is the craft's legal name, not its paperwork. Answer 4 overcorrects into a falsehood: air cargo examination is routine customs work, performed under the provisions that address aircraft and goods.

The takeaway: the definitions are the law's vocabulary, and their exclusions bind as hard as their inclusions — match the craft to the defined word, then find the provision for that word.

=== ASSESSMENT Q2 | tests lesson 2.1 ===
QUESTION:
A trainee compiles a list of everything aboard an arriving container ship and labels every item "cargo": the containers in the hold, the vessel's bunker fuel, and the crew's personal luggage. Which option best assesses the list?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The list is correct, because everything on board a commercial vessel is cargo by definition.
2. The list is wrong: section 151 defines cargo as goods other than stores or passenger or crew baggage — so the bunker fuel is a store (with its own regime in section 24) and the crew's luggage is passenger or crew baggage, while the containers' goods are cargo.
3. The list is wrong only about the fuel, which is cargo because it is combustible goods.
4. The list is correct for imports but wrong for exports.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 151 of Act 891 defines cargo as goods other than stores or passenger or crew baggage — a definition that teaches by exclusion. The containers' consigned goods are cargo; the bunker fuel is a store, the craft's own supply, which section 24's regime for aircraft and ship stores governs; and the crew's luggage is expressly outside cargo by the definition's own words.

Answer 1 ignores the carve-out that is the definition's entire point. Answer 3 half-corrects and then re-errs: the fuel's combustibility is irrelevant — its character as the craft's own supply is what makes it a store. Answer 4 invents a directional rule; the cargo/stores/baggage split is the same arriving and departing.

The takeaway: three categories travel on every craft — cargo, stores, baggage — and the declarations, manifests and stores lists keep them separate because the provisions treat them separately.

=== ASSESSMENT Q3 | tests lesson 2.1 ===
QUESTION:
A vessel's appointed captain falls ill mid-voyage and the chief officer takes command for the final leg into Tema. The arrival report and the account of cargo are then due. Which option best states who must deliver them?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The sick captain must still sign them, because only an appointed captain can be a master.
2. The person in charge of the conveyance must deliver them: section 15 defines the master functionally — a person who is in charge of a conveyance — so the report under section 17 and the account under section 39 are due from whoever holds command.
3. The shipping company's head office must deliver them, since the company owns the cargo contracts.
4. The importer's agent must deliver them, because the agent lodges the declarations.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 15 of Act 891 defines the master for the purposes of that Part: a person who is in charge of a conveyance. The definition is functional — command, not title — so the officer who took charge is the master for the arrival leg, and the report the section 17 requires and the account of cargo section 39 requires are due from the person holding that command at the relevant moment.

Answer 1 freezes the definition to the appointment paperwork; the law's question is who is in charge, and it changes if command changes. Answer 3 confuses the commercial ownership of the carriage contract with the statutory duties attached to the person in charge of the craft. Answer 4 confuses the cargo's declaration trail (the agent's) with the craft's report trail (the master's) — two different accountability chains the module deliberately keeps separate.

The takeaway: "master" travels with command, not with a certificate — and every report and account duty follows the person who holds it.

=== ASSESSMENT Q4 | tests lesson 2.2 ===
QUESTION:
A new agent assembles a clearance file and asks which document proves the physical facts of a shipment — how the goods are packed and what each package weighs. Which option best answers the question and states the check the file must satisfy?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The commercial invoice proves the physical facts, because it states the total price.
2. The packing list proves the physical facts — packages, contents, weights — and the file's proof is consistency: the packing list's counts and weights must reconcile with the invoice's quantities and the transport document's counts.
3. The declaration proves the physical facts, because it is the statutory form customs reads.
4. The certificate of origin proves the physical facts, because it is issued by an authority.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. The lesson's document system assigns each document its facts: the commercial invoice states the commercial facts (parties, prices, delivery term), the packing list states the physical facts (packages, contents, weights, allocations), and the transport document states the movement facts (craft, route, custody, counts). The file proves itself by consistency — the packing list's total packages and weights reconcile with the invoice's quantities and the transport document's counts, and section 14 requires the documents to be submitted with the entry.

Answer 1 assigns the invoice the wrong facts; its numbers are money, not packages. Answer 3 elevates the declaration while forgetting it merely asserts what the file must corroborate. Answer 4 confuses origin evidence with physical evidence — the certificate speaks to where the goods come from, not how they are packed.

The takeaway: each document proves one layer of facts, and the clearance file is those layers agreeing with each other.

=== ASSESSMENT Q5 | tests lesson 2.2 ===
QUESTION:
An officer requires production of the commercial invoice for a consignment selected for document check, and the trader objects that no offence has been alleged and his records are private. Which option best states the position under Act 891?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The objection is correct: section 101 applies only after an arrest has been made.
2. The objection is correct, because commercial invoices are confidential trading documents outside customs control.
3. The objection fails: section 101 empowers officers to demand invoices and inspect packages, without a prior allegation; and the trader's records — statements, declarations, documents, data, books, papers, correspondence, accounts — are records within section 151's definition, kept under the section 9 duty, which survives release.
4. The objection fails, but only for imported goods; export documents remain private.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 101 of Act 891 empowers officers to demand invoices and inspect packages — the documentary backbone of the supporting-file system — and the power's exercise does not wait for an allegation: the document check is the control. The trader's file is not private in the sense the objection claims: section 151 defines a record as any information kept in the ordinary course of business pertaining to any customs activity — statements, declarations, documents, data, books, papers, correspondence, accounts and financial accounting data — and section 9 imposes the duty to maintain such records, a duty that survives release because section 7's post-clearance audit reads the trail afterwards.

Answer 1 invents an arrest precondition that the provision does not carry. Answer 2 asserts a confidentiality that the record-keeping regime precisely qualifies — the records exist to be produced. Answer 4 splits the power by direction of trade with no textual basis.

The takeaway: the file is the clearance's evidence base and the audit's raw material — the power to demand it is ordinary control, not an accusation.

=== ASSESSMENT Q6 | tests lesson 2.2 ===
QUESTION:
A declaration describes goods as "new industrial boilers". The commercial invoice in the file describes "refurbished industrial boilers, re-tubed 2024", and the condition of the goods affects their duty treatment. The agent lodges anyway. Which option best evaluates the lodgement?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The lodgement is safe, because the declaration is the operative form and the invoice is background.
2. The lodgement is safe because "new" and "refurbished" are trade synonyms.
3. The lodgement is a false-statement risk exactly as section 151 defines the term — a statement false or misleading in a material respect with respect to goods subject to customs control — since the condition difference is material to duty treatment, and the agent's own signature makes the statement the agent's; the file must be reconciled before lodgement, and kept after release for the audit.
4. The lodgement is a false-statement risk only if the importer also signs the declaration.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 151 of Act 891 defines a false statement as a statement made with respect to goods subject to customs control that is false or misleading in a material respect, or a material omission from a required statement or document. The scenario's difference — new versus refurbished — is material on its own facts: condition drives classification, valuation and any concession claimed. A declaration that asserts what its own invoice contradicts is a statement the file disproves, and the agent who lodged it made the statement too.

Answer 1 inverts the documentary system: the declaration is proved by the file, not insulated from it. Answer 2 is wishful equivalence — the trade may blur the words, but the duty treatment does not. Answer 4 misses the agent's own exposure: the definition reaches every statement made with respect to the goods, and the lesson's discipline — reconcile the file, lodge from it, keep it past release for the section 7 audit — exists to prevent exactly this.

The takeaway: a material mismatch lodged is a false statement waiting for its provision; reconcile first, lodge second, retain always.

=== ASSESSMENT Q7 | tests lesson 2.3 ===
QUESTION:
Two consignments arrive the same day: pharmaceuticals by air on an air waybill, and tiles by sea on a bill of lading. A trainee asks why two different documents are used. Which option best explains the system?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The air waybill is used for valuable goods and the bill of lading for cheap goods.
2. The transport document follows the mode: the bill of lading evidences the ocean carriage (vessel, ports, custody, counts), the air waybill evidences the air carriage — and both serve the same three checks: match the arrival report, match the packing list, match the party taking delivery.
3. The documents are interchangeable; the agent chooses whichever is shorter.
4. The bill of lading applies to exports only, and the air waybill to imports only.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. The transport document follows the mode of transport, and the mode picks it: ocean cargo travels on a bill of lading, air cargo on an air waybill. Both documents perform the same three jobs — evidence of the carriage contract, receipt of the goods on board, and identification of the party entitled to delivery — which is why the officer's checks are the same in substance for both: the document against the arrival report, the marks and counts against the packing list, the consignee against the party claiming delivery.

Answer 1 invents a value rule that does not exist; the mode, not the goods' price, picks the document. Answer 3 mistakes convenience for law — the documents differ because the carriers and the modes differ. Answer 4 invents a direction-of-trade split with no basis.

The takeaway: the mode picks the document, the document carries the movement facts, and the checks run the same way whichever document the mode produced.

=== ASSESSMENT Q8 | tests lesson 2.3 ===
QUESTION:
An importer lodges a declaration claiming preferential duty for goods from a partner market. The file contains the invoice and packing list but no certificate of origin. The agent argues: "The certificate is a formality — my declaration states the origin." Which option best evaluates the argument?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The argument is correct: the declarant's statement is sufficient evidence of origin.
2. The argument is correct, because certificates are only required for exports.
3. The argument fails: section 70's origin claim is evidenced by the certificate — which states the parties, the goods, the origin criterion and the invoice reference, and is authenticated by the issuing body — and without it the claim is an unevidenced assertion the officer cannot verify; the document is obtained before lodgement, not after the officer asks.
4. The argument fails, and the goods are therefore seized as prohibited imports.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. The certificate of origin is the evidence that turns an origin assertion into a checkable claim: it names the exporter and consignee, describes the goods identically to the invoice, states the origin criterion, references the commercial invoice, and carries the issuing body's authentication. Section 70 of Act 891 provides the country-of-origin hook the claim feeds, and section 14's document requirement is where the certificate enters the file. Without it, the officer has an assertion with nothing to test against.

Answer 1 confuses the assertion with its evidence — the whole documentary system runs on claims being provable. Answer 2 invents an export-only rule; origin certificates accompany preference claims on imports as well. Answer 4 jumps to seizure, which is a separate machinery for separate failures — an unevidenced claim is refused or queried, not treated as prohibition.

The takeaway: the certificate is the claim's body; the declaration carries it. No certificate, no claim — and the agent who advises otherwise sets the client up for a refused preference.

=== ASSESSMENT Q9 | tests lesson 2.3 ===
QUESTION:
Before a vessel departs Tema, the master's account of cargo under section 39 states 180 containers discharged and loaded in total, but the bills of lading in the agent's file total 178. Which option best states the professional response?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The difference is within a tolerance and can be ignored; the border reconciles totals only at year end.
2. The agent should adjust the bills of lading to read 180 so the file is consistent.
3. The discrepancy must be resolved by establishing the true count from the source documents — the bills, the discharge list, the arrival report — and correcting whichever document is wrong; the report (s.17), the bills of lading and the account of cargo (s.39) are three views of the same goods and must agree.
4. The master's account controls absolutely, and the bills of lading are irrelevant to customs.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. The craft's paper trail is a reconciling system: section 17 of Act 891 provides the master's report on arrival, section 39 the account of cargo before departure, and the bills of lading evidence what was shipped. Three views of the same goods at three moments — and a disagreement between them is a discrepancy in the craft's own papers that must be resolved by finding the truth, then correcting the document that is wrong. An unexplained two-container gap on a departing vessel is exactly the kind of discrepancy the reconciliation exists to catch — goods on board that no document accounts for, or documents for goods that are not there.

Answer 1 normalises an unexplained gap; tolerance is not a source of law. Answer 2 is the professional's cardinal error in document work — aligning the paper to a convenient total instead of establishing the facts, which converts a discrepancy into a false statement. Answer 4 dismisses a document the movement's accountability depends on.

The takeaway: when documents disagree, find the truth first and correct the paper second — never the reverse.

=== ASSESSMENT Q10 | tests lesson 2.4 ===
QUESTION:
A trainee is asked what an Incoterm actually decides, and answers: "the colour of the shipping documents". Which option best corrects the trainee?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The trainee is right: Incoterms govern the paperwork's format.
2. The trainee is wrong: an Incoterm is a three-way allocation — it fixes the place the seller's delivery obligation is performed, the point at which risk of loss or damage transfers, and which party bears which costs; the term on the invoice is that allocation under a standard name.
3. The trainee is wrong because Incoterms set the duty rates on the goods.
4. The trainee is wrong because Incoterms are optional courtesies with no commercial effect.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. The lesson's opening defines the instrument: an Incoterm allocates three things — the delivery place, the risk-transfer point, and the costs. The deck's own definitions show the three allocations in operation: FOB delivers on board the vessel at the named port of shipment, transfers risk on board, and puts all costs from that moment on the buyer; CFR delivers on board and puts the freight on the seller, with risk still on board at loading; DDP delivers the goods cleared for import at the named destination with the seller bearing the costs to that point.

Answer 1 mistakes the trade term for a document standard — the documents follow the mode (lesson 2.3), not the Incoterm. Answer 3 confuses a private allocation of costs with the state's tariff, which no Incoterm sets. Answer 4 is the opposite of their commercial function: the whole point of a standard term is that both parties are bound to a known allocation.

The takeaway: delivery, risk, cost — three allocations under four letters, and every valuation question later in the programme starts by reading them.

=== ASSESSMENT Q11 | tests lesson 2.4 ===
QUESTION:
Two importers buy identical machines from the same foreign seller. Importer A's contract says CFR Tema; importer B's says CIF Tema. The vessel sinks mid-voyage and the goods are lost. Which option best states the risk position of each importer under the deck's definitions?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Both importers bore the risk, because both sellers paid the freight to Tema.
2. Both importers were protected, because the sellers paid for delivery to Tema.
3. Under the deck's definitions, risk transferred to each buyer when the goods were on board the vessel at the loading port — CFR's slide states it expressly, and CIF shares the same on-board risk point — so neither importer can hold the seller liable for the sea loss; the difference between the terms is that CIF's seller also contracts the transit insurance, which is where B's claim lies.
4. The sellers bore the risk in both cases until the goods were discharged at Tema.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. The deck's CFR definition states that risk transfers when the goods are on board the vessel, such that the seller is taken to have performed its obligation whether or not the goods actually arrive at their destination in sound condition, in the stated quantity or, indeed, at all — and the deck's CIF slide repeats the same on-board risk point. The difference between the two terms is the insurance leg: CIF's seller contracts the transit insurance the name announces, so importer B's recourse is the insurance policy; importer A, on CFR, insured the voyage himself or uninsured bore the loss.

Answers 1 and 4 both commit the lesson's named error — equating the payer of freight with the bearer of risk; the cost allocation and the risk allocation are separate. Answer 2 confuses a freight obligation with risk protection.

The takeaway: on CFR and CIF alike the risk passed on board at loading; what changed between the two contracts is who bought the insurance — and that is where the lost goods' claim lives.

=== ASSESSMENT Q12 | tests lesson 2.4 ===
QUESTION:
An agent receives an invoice for goods bought on FOB terms, and the declared value states only the invoice figure. The officer reads the delivery term and adjusts. Which option best explains the officer's reading and the agent's error?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The officer is wrong: the invoice figure is always the customs value.
2. The officer is wrong, because FOB terms mean the invoice already includes the freight.
3. The officer is right to read the term first: FOB shows the invoice excludes the freight and insurance the buyer bore, and the valuation rules' additions bring the value to its legal base — the term explains what the invoice includes and omits, and the value is built from that reading. The agent stopped at the term instead of applying the rules.
4. The officer is right because FOB invoices are legally invalid documents.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. The lesson's bridge is the term-to-value connection: the delivery term tells the officer what the invoice figure already includes and what it omits. The deck's FOB definition puts the ocean freight and the insurance on the buyer from the moment the goods are on board — so an FOB invoice states the value at the loading port, and the additions the valuation rules provide (Module 8) bring the excluded costs into the customs value to reach the legal base. The agent's error was treating the term as the end of the analysis: the term explains the invoice, the rules build the value.

Answer 1 flattens the system — the invoice figure is the starting evidence, not automatically the value. Answer 2 misreads FOB's definition, which puts freight and insurance on the buyer. Answer 4 invents an invalidity that exists nowhere.

The takeaway: the term explains the invoice; the rules build the value — and the professional reads them in that order, every time.
