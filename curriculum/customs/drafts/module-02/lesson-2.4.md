=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: What an Incoterm allocates
BODY:
An Incoterm is a three-way allocation written in four letters: it fixes the place where the seller's delivery obligation is performed, the point at which risk of loss or damage transfers from seller to buyer, and which party bears which costs along the way. Those three allocations — delivery, risk, cost — are the whole instrument, and every trade term the learner will see on an invoice is one of these allocations under a standard name.

Why a standard name matters: two traders in different countries, reading the same four letters, understand the same split without negotiating it clause by clause. The term on the invoice is a shared vocabulary, published and maintained so that a sale agreed in Shenzhen and a sale agreed in Hamburg allocate cost and risk the same way.

Customs enters the picture because the allocation decides the value question: which cost lines — freight, insurance, loading charges — belong inside the customs value of the imported goods, and which belong to the buyer's side of the ledger before the goods were shipped. The delivery term on the invoice is therefore the first thing a valuation reading turns to, and this lesson teaches the terms well enough that the reading is precise.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The two families and the eleven rules
BODY:
The training deck presents the current set — Incoterms 2020 — as eleven rules in two families, and the family tells you the transport mode the rule is written for.

The any-mode family works for every mode of transport, including multimodal container movements: EXW (Ex Works), FCA (Free Carrier), CPT (Carriage Paid To), CIP (Carriage and Insurance Paid To), DAP (Delivered at Place), DPU (Delivered at Place Unloaded), and DDP (Delivered Duty Paid).

The sea and inland-waterway family is written for cargo delivered onto a vessel: FAS (Free Alongside Ship), FOB (Free On Board), CFR (Cost and Freight), and CIF (Cost, Insurance and Freight).

The deck lists the families on its own slide, and the split is not decorative: a container moved door-to-door is awkward under a ship's-rail rule, while a bulk cargo loaded directly into a vessel's hold is exactly what the sea family describes. The learner's first skill is to look at a movement and ask which family fits — and then to read the specific rule's own wording, which the following blocks quote for the three the Ghana trade lane uses most.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: FOB — free on board
BODY:
The deck defines Free On Board in its own words: the seller delivers the goods to the buyer on board the vessel nominated by the buyer at the named port of shipment, or procures the goods already so delivered. The risk of loss of or damage to the goods transfers when the goods are on board the vessel, and the buyer bears all costs from that moment onwards.

Three allocations, read straight off the definition. Delivery: on board the vessel at the named port of shipment. Risk: transfers when the goods are on board — not when the vessel sails, and not at the destination. Costs: the buyer's from that moment onward, which means the ocean freight, the insurance the buyer chooses to buy, and the destination charges all sit on the buyer's side.

For an import into Ghana on FOB terms, the invoice price therefore excludes the freight and the insurance — and the customs-value question those excluded costs raise is exactly the bridge to Module 8: what the law adds back, and on what base. The learner should be able to read FOB and state all three allocations without hesitation, because every cost question later in the programme assumes it.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: CFR and CIF — cost and freight, cost insurance and freight
BODY:
The deck defines Cost and Freight in its own words: the seller delivers the goods to the buyer on board the vessel, or procures the goods already so delivered; the risk of loss or damage transfers when the goods are on board the vessel, such that the seller is taken to have performed its obligation whether or not the goods actually arrive at their destination in sound condition, in the stated quantity or, indeed, at all. The striking feature is the risk-cost split: the seller pays the freight to the destination, but the risk passed at the loading port.

Cost, Insurance and Freight carries the same delivery and risk point — the deck's slide repeats the on-board transfer language — with the insurance leg the name announces: the seller contracts for insurance on the goods' transit, the "I" between the C and the F. The deck's slide states the delivery and risk mechanics; the insurance element is the rule's named content, and the fact-checker should verify the deck's insurance wording against the slide before quoting it further.

The comparison the learner must hold: FOB puts freight and insurance on the buyer; CFR moves the freight to the seller but leaves risk at the load port; CIF adds the seller's insurance contract on top. Same vessel, three different cost allocations — and three different starting points for a customs value.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Why customs reads the delivery term
BODY:
The delivery term on the commercial invoice is the first line of the valuation question, because it says which costs were borne by whom before the goods crossed the border.

An FOB invoice prices the goods at the loading port: freight to Ghana and insurance were the buyer's costs, paid separately. A CIF invoice prices the goods with freight and insurance included. The same goods, the same vessel — two different invoice totals — and the customs value must be built to the same legal base regardless of the term. That is why the delivery term is read: it tells the officer what the invoice figure already includes and what it omits, so the additions and deductions of Module 8 can be applied precisely.

The term also allocates the commissions and charges the valuation rules care about: selling costs sit with the seller's side, the buyer's buying commission with the buyer's. Section 151 defines both — a buying commission is the fee paid by an importer to the agent for representing the importer in the purchase, and a selling commission is the fees paid to the seller's agent for services connected with selling the imported goods — and Module 8 places each on its correct side of the value.

Read the term, then read the costs: that is the order.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The professional habit: quote the rule, cite the slide
BODY:
Incoterms are negotiated texts, like statutes: a single word carries an obligation, and a confident paraphrase can quietly replace the negotiated word with a guess. The habit this programme trains is the one the brief mandates — quote the rule's own wording from the source at hand (here, the training deck's slides, cited), and never reconstruct a term's risk point from memory.

The reason is practical rather than academic: a learner who misremembers whether CIF transfers risk at the loading port or the destination will misallocate the insurance cost in a valuation, and the error reaches the duty. The error is invisible until it is expensive.

So the habit, stated as a rule: name the version (Incoterms 2020), name the rule (FOB, CFR, CIF), quote the allocation from the source, cite the slide or publication. A client told "risk passes on board, here is the wording, here is the source" receives advice that survives scrutiny — and the professional who built the habit on the training deck will recognise the ICC's own texts when they meet them in practice.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Ceramic tiles from abroad: the same goods on two terms
BODY:
Step 1. The trade. A Kumasi trader imports ceramic tiles. Her supplier quotes the same goods two ways: FOB the foreign loading port at 40,000 dollars, or CIF Tema at 43,200 dollars. The freight and insurance costs between the two prices are 3,200 dollars.

Step 2. Reading FOB. On the FOB invoice, the seller's obligation ended on board at the loading port — the deck's definition: risk transferred when the goods were on board the vessel, and the buyer bore all costs from that moment. The 40,000 dollars therefore excludes the freight and insurance, which the trader arranged herself.

Step 3. Reading CIF. On the CIF invoice, the seller delivers on board and contracts the insurance — the price includes the cost, the insurance and the freight to Tema. The 43,200 dollars includes costs the FOB price excluded.

Step 4. The customs reading. The goods cross the border under either term; the customs value must reach the same legal base either way. Under FOB, the excluded freight and insurance are the additions the valuation rules handle; under CIF, they are already inside the invoice figure. The officer reads the term first, then adjusts precisely — and the trader's declared value must state the term honestly, because the term is what the adjustment follows.

Step 5. The client's decision. The trader asks which term to buy on. The agent explains: the term allocates cost and risk between the parties — it does not change the goods, and the valuation rules will treat the costs consistently either way.

What does this example demonstrate? The Incoterm moves costs between the invoice and the buyer's own arrangements, and the customs value is built to the same base under any term — which is why the officer reads the term before the figures.

Key lesson: the term tells you where the price stops; the valuation rules tell you where the value starts.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: EXW and DDP at a European buyer: the allocation's extremes
BODY:
Step 1. The two terms. A Rotterdam buyer imports machines from a supplier who offers the goods Ex Works at his factory gate, or Delivered Duty Paid to the buyer's warehouse. The deck lists EXW and DDP in the any-mode family — the two extremes of the allocation.

Step 2. Reading EXW. Ex Works makes the buyer do everything: the buyer collects at the seller's premises, arranges and pays the export clearance, the carriage, the insurance, and the import clearance at destination. Risk passes at the factory gate. The invoice price is the smallest possible number, and every other cost is the buyer's own line.

Step 3. Reading DDP. Delivered Duty Paid puts everything on the seller: the deck's slide defines it as delivery when the goods are placed at the disposal of the buyer, cleared for import, on the arriving means of transport, ready for unloading, at the named place of destination. The seller bore the carriage, the insurance arrangements, and the import duties abroad.

Step 4. The customs reading. Under EXW, the import clearance at destination is the buyer's own declaration, built from a small invoice price plus the costs the buyer incurred. Under DDP, the seller's machinery has already cleared the goods — and the buyer's file must still show what the term delivered, because the officer reads the term to know which costs the invoice carries.

What does this example demonstrate? The allocation ranges from the buyer doing everything to the seller doing everything — and the customs file must reflect the term either way.

Key lesson: an Incoterm is an allocation of work; read the extremes once and every rule between them is a mix of the same three allocations.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Incoterms 2020 is the set — name the version
BODY:
The current published set is Incoterms 2020, and the professional habit is to name the version whenever a term is used: "FOB (Incoterms 2020)". The reason is that the rules have been revised over the years, and a term's allocations can change between versions — a risk point memorised from an older set may not match the current text, and the difference reaches the valuation. The training deck presents the 2020 set, and this lesson quotes it. The general rule stands: name the version, quote the wording, cite the source. A trade term without a version is a term without a text, and a professional who cannot say which text they are applying cannot say what the allocation is — or answer for it later.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: The term on the invoice must match the reality
BODY:
The delivery term is a factual allocation, not a label chosen for convenience: whichever term appears on the invoice must describe what the parties actually arranged. A CIF invoice on goods whose freight the buyer actually paid is an invoice whose cost lines contradict its own term — and the mismatch surfaces in valuation, where the additions follow the term. The agent's check is practical: ask who paid the freight, who bought the insurance, and where the goods' risk passed; then check the invoice's term against the answers. Where the term and the reality disagree, the file needs correcting before lodgement, because the officer reads the term to decide which cost lines belong in the value — and a wrong term builds the value on the wrong lines.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A Ghanaian importer buys goods on CFR Tema. During the ocean voyage, the goods are damaged by seawater. Which option best states who bore the risk of that damage, and why?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The seller bore the risk, because the seller paid the freight to Tema.
2. The buyer bore the risk: under the deck's definition of CFR, risk transfers when the goods are on board the vessel at the loading port — the seller's freight obligation does not move the risk point.
3. The risk was shared equally, because the seller paid the freight and the buyer paid the duty.
4. The risk is undetermined until the goods are discharged at Tema.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. The training deck's definition of Cost and Freight states the mechanics: the seller delivers the goods on board the vessel, and the risk of loss or damage transfers when the goods are on board the vessel — such that the seller is taken to have performed its obligation whether or not the goods actually arrive at their destination in sound condition, in the stated quantity or, indeed, at all. The seller's freight payment is a cost allocation; it does not move the risk point, which passed at the loading port.

Answer 1 is the classic error — equating the payer of freight with the bearer of risk. Answer 3 invents a split that no rule contains. Answer 4 is the opposite of the definition's express wording: the risk point is on board at loading, not the discharge berth.

The takeaway: cost and risk are separate allocations; a term can put the freight bill on the seller and the sea risk on the buyer, and CFR is the textbook case.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
Which of the following correctly sorts the terms into the two families the training deck presents?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Any mode: EXW, FCA, CPT, CIP, DAP, DPU, DDP. Sea and inland waterway: FAS, FOB, CFR, CIF.
2. Any mode: FAS, FOB, CFR, CIF. Sea and inland waterway: EXW, FCA, CPT, CIP, DAP, DPU, DDP.
3. Both families contain all eleven rules; the split is only presentational.
4. The any-mode family contains only DDP; all other rules are sea rules.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1. The training deck's Incoterms slide presents the eleven rules of Incoterms 2020 in two families: the any-mode family — Ex Works (EXW), Free Carrier (FCA), Carriage Paid To (CPT), Carriage and Insurance Paid To (CIP), Delivered at Place (DAP), Delivered at Place Unloaded (DPU) and Delivered Duty Paid (DDP) — and the sea and inland-waterway family — Free Alongside Ship (FAS), Free On Board (FOB), Cost and Freight (CFR) and Cost, Insurance and Freight (CIF). The family tells the learner which transport the rule was written for: the any-mode rules work for containerised door-to-door movements, and the sea rules are written for delivery onto a vessel.

Answer 2 swaps the families, which would put a ship's-rail rule on a door-to-door movement. Answer 3 dissolves a distinction the deck teaches deliberately — the family is the first guide to choosing a term. Answer 4 has no basis in the deck's presentation.

The takeaway: family first, then rule — the family tells you the transport the term assumes, and the rule's own wording gives the three allocations.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A declaration for goods bought on FOB terms omits the ocean freight and marine insurance from the declared value, and the agent asserts that the omission is correct "because the term is FOB". Which option best evaluates the agent's position?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The agent is right: on FOB terms the buyer's costs never enter the customs value.
2. The agent is right only if the freight was paid after the goods arrived.
3. The agent has identified the starting point but stopped too early: the FOB term shows the invoice excludes the freight and insurance, and the valuation rules' additions are exactly what brings the value to the required legal base — the term explains the invoice, and Module 8's rules build the value from it.
4. The agent is wrong because the delivery term never affects any customs procedure.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. The term is the first line of the valuation reading, not the last word: FOB means the invoice price excludes the freight and insurance the buyer bore — the deck's definition puts those costs on the buyer from the moment the goods are on board — and that is precisely why the valuation rules provide additions to bring the customs value to its legal base. The term tells the officer what the invoice figure includes and omits; it does not itself settle the value.

Answer 1 treats the term as a shield against the valuation rules — it is the opposite: the term is what tells the rules which adjustments apply. Answer 2 invents a payment-timing test that no rule contains. Answer 4 discards the connection this lesson built: the delivery term is read precisely because it allocates the costs the value is built from.

The takeaway: read the term to read the invoice; read the valuation rules to build the value. The term starts the analysis — it never finishes it.
