=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The six modes of transport
BODY:
The GRA's shipping and Incoterms training deck opens with the modes: goods move by ocean, air, rail, road, inland waterway and pipeline. Every consignment in this programme arrives by one of them — a reefer container by ocean at Tema, pharmaceuticals by air at Kotoka, cement by road from a neighbouring country, fuel by pipeline.

The mode matters to customs because the mode decides the craft, the craft decides the document, and the document decides what the officer checks. Ocean cargo travels under a bill of lading on a ship; air cargo travels under an air waybill on an aircraft; and the Act's definitions treat those craft differently, as the next block shows. Road and rail movements cross land boundaries, where the crossing itself — not a berth — is the customs event. Inland waterway and pipeline movements are the specialist tail: smaller volumes by water, and continuous flows by pipe that are measured rather than discharged.

The learner's first habit: for any consignment, name the mode before anything else. The mode predicts the craft, the place of control and the paperwork, and every later question in a clearance is easier once the mode is fixed.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The two craft the Act defines
BODY:
Act 891 defines the craft precisely, and the definitions are narrower than everyday usage.

"Ship" means a floating craft of every description — but the definition expressly does not include aircraft. The carve-out matters: a hovercraft or a floating derrick is a ship under the definition, while the newest cargo plane is not, whatever its tonnage. "Aircraft" is defined separately: an apparatus, whether or not mechanically propelled, used for the transport by air of human beings or goods — a definition wide enough to cover unpowered flight, because the transport function, not the engine, is what the law is addressing.

"Vehicle" reaches the land: a conveyance for the transport by land of goods or persons, with "motor vehicle" defined separately as a mechanically propelled vehicle for use on a road — a definition Module 13 returns to, because used vehicles carry their own tax, prohibition and valuation provisions (ss.55–61).

Three craft definitions, three transport layers. When a provision says a power applies to a ship, an aircraft or a vehicle, the question "which definition governs?" is answered by the mode the goods are moving in — and the answer changes the provisions that follow.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The vessel types a learner will actually meet
BODY:
The training deck's vessel catalogue matches what Tema's quays actually host, and each type carries a different cargo logic.

1. Container ships carry non-bulk cargo in standardised, truck-size intermodal containers — the workhorse of the FMCG trade, and the reason the Act's "container" definition (the next block) matters operationally.

2. Bulk carriers transport unpackaged bulk cargo: grains, coal, ore and cement — cargo measured by the hold, not by the box.

3. Tanker ships specialise in fluids: crude oil, petroleum products and liquefied gases — cargo whose control is about the pipe and the valve as much as the document.

4. RORO and multi-purpose vessels, and pure car carriers, roll vehicles on and off — the used-vehicle trade's craft.

5. Passenger ships, ferries and cruise liners exist primarily for people, which is why their baggage and stores follow their own provisions.

6. Offshore ships support the oil and gas industry — exploration, construction and energy production — outside the ordinary container flow.

The customs point behind the catalogue: the vessel type predicts the cargo's form, and the cargo's form predicts the examination approach, the documents and the risks. A bulk hold of cement and a boxship of mixed FMCG containers are different examinations, though the same Act governs both.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Cargo, stores and passenger baggage: the Act's split
BODY:
"Cargo" is defined in section 151 as goods other than stores or passenger or crew baggage — a definition that teaches by exclusion. The consignment of frozen mackerel, the container of tiles, the coil of steel: cargo. The food the crew eats, the fuel the vessel burns, and the passengers' suitcases: not cargo, however much they cross the border with the craft.

The carve-out has a provision behind it. Section 24 provides for aircraft and ship stores — the supplies the craft itself needs — and Module 13 returns to that regime for duty-free shops and controlled consumption. The reason the law separates them is control logic: cargo is the goods the declaration accounts for, item by item; stores are the craft's own provisions, controlled differently because their use is aboard the conveyance.

The practical consequence at a clearance: the manifest and the declaration speak in cargo; the stores list is a separate document with its own treatment. A learner who lumps them together will misread both. When the Act says cargo, it means exactly the goods other than stores and passenger or crew baggage — and the distinction decides which provisions follow the goods.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The container, defined by the law
BODY:
The steel box that dominates modern trade is defined in Act 891 as well. A container is an article of transport equipment with four qualities, stated in the definition: it is of a permanent character and strong enough to be suitable for repeated use; it is specially designed to facilitate the carriage of goods by one or more modes of transport without intermediate reloading; it is fitted with a device that permits ready handling, particularly its transfer from one mode of transport to another; and it is designed as to be easy to fill and empty.

Read the four qualities as the container's job description: durable, multimodal, liftable, fillable. They are why a box stuffed in Shenzhen can travel ship, road and rail to Kumasi without its cargo being rehandled — and why customs control is built around the box: seal it at stuffing, and the identification survives the whole journey.

The definition also draws the line against things that look like containers but are not: a weak crate built for one trip fails "permanent character and repeated use"; a box without handling fittings fails the transfer clause. The learner's skill is to match the equipment in front of them to the definition's four qualities, exactly as the next worked example does.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The master: the person the Act holds responsible
BODY:
Every provision about a vessel's arrival, report and cargo speaks of "the master" — and section 15 defines the word: for the purposes of that Part, the master means a person who is in charge of a conveyance. The definition is functional, not a job title: whoever is in charge, is the master, whether the shipping company's appointed captain or whoever has command in practice.

Why the law pins the duties there: the master is the one person who can be required to account for everything the craft carries. Section 17 requires the report the master makes on arrival; section 19 sets the master's duties; section 39 requires the master to deliver an account of cargo before departure; section 40 provides the transire the master delivers on arrival. The report-and-account chain in Module 3 hangs on this defined person.

The learner's takeaway is a habit of reading: when a provision says "the master shall...", the law is assigning a duty to whoever is in charge of the conveyance at that moment — and the assignment is enforceable against that person. Craft, cargo and master: the three defined anchors of every vessel movement this programme studies.

Reference: the modes of transport and vessel operations are introduced in the Logistics programme, module Transport and Shipping Operations.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A RoRo vessel works the Tema car park
BODY:
Step 1. The craft. A pure car carrier berths at Tema with 1,100 used vehicles on its decks. The craft is a ship under section 151 — a floating craft of every description — and it is not an aircraft, so the aircraft provisions never engage. The mode is ocean; the cargo logic is roll-on, roll-off.

Step 2. The cargo split. The vehicles are cargo — goods other than stores or passenger or crew baggage. The vessel's own provisions, from bunker fuel to the crew's provisions, are stores under the same definition's carve-out, and section 24's regime governs them separately. The distinction is live at this berth: a RoRo carries a lot of both.

Step 3. The person. The master — whoever is in charge of the conveyance — delivers the arrival report under section 17 and accounts for the cargo. The importer's agent lodges declarations for the vehicles; each vehicle is separately classified and valued, because the used-vehicle provisions of sections 55–61 apply to the trade.

Step 4. The control decision. Risk-based control under section 3 selects some vehicles for physical verification: chassis numbers against documents. The rest proceed on documents.

Step 5. The release. Cleared vehicles roll off under their own power to the bonded car park; the master's account of cargo reconciles with what was discharged.

What does this example demonstrate? One vessel, every definition of this lesson working at once: ship, cargo against stores, the master's duties, and the vehicle provisions waiting in Module 13.

Key lesson: identify the craft, split the cargo from the stores, and find the person the Act holds — then the clearance reads itself.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Two vessels at two ports: a boxship and a tanker
BODY:
Step 1. The boxship at Tema. A container ship berths with 2,000 twenty-foot equivalents. Its cargo is packed in containers — equipment that meets all four qualities of the Act's definition: permanent, multimodal, fitted with corner fittings for ready handling, and designed to be easy to fill and empty. The examination question is simple: which boxes, if any, the risk system selects.

Step 2. The tanker at Rotterdam. In the same week, a tanker discharges petroleum products at a European port. No containers: the cargo is fluid, measured by flow meter, carried in the vessel's own tanks. The examination question is entirely different — quantity, quality and the pipe manifold — though the legal spine is the same Act-shaped architecture: report, declare, assess, release.

Step 3. The comparison. Both masters reported on arrival; both cargoes were declared; both releases required the conditions of section 6(1). What differed was the cargo's form, and form drove everything operational: unitised boxes versus metered fluid, seal numbers versus meter tickets.

Step 4. The transfer. A learner who has worked the boxship can read the tanker's clearance — because the definitions (ship, cargo, master) hold constant, and only the operational layer changes with the vessel type.

What does this example demonstrate? Vessel type changes the operational questions, not the legal ones; the definitions are the constant.

Key lesson: learn the definitions once, and every vessel type becomes a variation on a spine you already know.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: "Ship" does not include aircraft — read definitions exactly
BODY:
The definition of ship — a floating craft of every description — ends with an exclusion: but does not include aircraft. It would be easy to read "floating craft of every description" as covering everything that carries goods, and to assume the aircraft provisions are a subset of the ship provisions. They are not: the Act defines aircraft separately, in its own words, for transport by air. The lesson generalises: in customs law, definitions are read exactly, and their exclusions carry as much weight as their inclusions. When a provision applies to a ship, it does not apply to an aircraft unless another provision says so; when it applies to a vehicle, neither the ship nor the aircraft definition reaches it. The professional never assumes a word's everyday width — the section 151 definition is the width.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Stores travel with the craft, and have their own regime
BODY:
The cargo definition's carve-out — goods other than stores or passenger or crew baggage — is not a drafting flourish; it is the gateway to a separate regime. Section 24 provides for aircraft and ship stores, and section 90 for their delivery, because stores are consumed aboard the craft rather than entered for home use. The control question for stores is whether they leave the craft lawfully, not whether they are declared as cargo. Two practical consequences follow. First, a manifest that lists stores as cargo has misclassified them at the document level, and the discrepancy will surface at reconciliation. Second, the duty-free shop regime of Module 13 exists because controlled consumption needs its own rules — the same carve-out logic at retail scale. Keep the three categories separate in your head: cargo, stores, baggage — each with its own provisions.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A customs provision authorises the proper officer to inspect "a ship". A cargo plane is on the apron at Kotoka loaded with undeclared goods. Which option best states whether the ship-inspection power covers the aircraft?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Yes, because the definition of ship covers every craft that carries goods for trade.
2. Yes, because aircraft and ships are both conveyances and the words are interchangeable.
3. No: "ship" is defined as a floating craft of every description but does not include aircraft, and "aircraft" is defined separately — so a ship power does not reach the aircraft unless another provision applies.
4. No, because aircraft can only be inspected by the aviation authority, never by customs.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 151 of Act 891 defines a ship as a floating craft of every description, but the definition expressly does not include aircraft; aircraft are defined in their own right — an apparatus, whether or not mechanically propelled, used for the transport by air of human beings or goods. The exclusion is part of the definition, so a power written for a ship does not stretch to an aircraft by ordinary usage; it applies only if a provision addresses aircraft separately.

Answer 1 reads "every description" wider than the definition allows — the exclusion carves aircraft out of "every description". Answer 2 dissolves the distinction between two separately defined words. Answer 4 overcorrects into a false rule: customs examines air cargo routinely; the point is which provision authorises the act, not whether any can.

The takeaway: definitions are read exactly, and an exclusion in a definition is as binding as its inclusion — match the craft to the defined word before applying the power.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
On a bulk carrier arriving to discharge cement at Takoradi, which of the following items on the vessel is NOT cargo within the meaning of section 151?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The 25,000 bags of cement in the hold, consigned to a Ghanaian trader.
2. The diesel fuel the vessel burns on its next voyage, carried as the craft's own supply.
3. A consignment of mining equipment for a Tarkwa contractor, declared on the manifest.
4. Bagged rice for an importer, declared alongside the cement.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 151 defines cargo as goods other than stores or passenger or crew baggage. The vessel's own fuel is a store — a supply for the craft itself — and stores are excluded from cargo by the definition's own words; section 24 provides the separate regime for aircraft and ship stores that this carve-out opens.

Answer 1 and Answer 4 are the consigned goods the vessel trades, plainly cargo. Answer 3 is cargo too, whatever the goods' end use — mining equipment declared on the manifest is goods other than stores or baggage. The trap in the question is the assumption that everything physical aboard a vessel is cargo; the definition's whole purpose is to separate the goods the declaration accounts for from the goods the craft consumes.

The takeaway: cargo is a defined term with a built-in exclusion — stores and passenger or crew baggage follow their own provisions, and a manifest that blurs the line will disagree with the reconciliation.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A trader imports ceramic tiles packed in steel containers and asks his agent why customs is confident the goods that arrive are the goods that were stuffed abroad. Which option best explains the role of the container in that confidence?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Containers are irrelevant to identification; only the master's honesty matters.
2. The Act's container definition describes equipment of a permanent character, strong enough for repeated use, specially designed to carry goods across modes without intermediate reloading, fitted for ready handling, and easy to fill and empty — so a seal applied at stuffing identifies the goods through the whole journey.
3. Containers are defined as single-trip packaging, which is why they are destroyed after one use.
4. The container definition applies only to ships' own equipment, never to cargo containers.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. Section 151 of Act 891 defines a container as an article of transport equipment that is of a permanent character and strong enough to be suitable for repeated use; specially designed to facilitate the carriage of goods by one or more modes of transport without intermediate reloading; fitted with a device that permits ready handling, particularly its transfer from one mode of transport to another; and designed as to be easy to fill and empty. Those four qualities are what make seal-based control possible: the box survives the journey, carries the goods across modes without rehandling, and can be secured at stuffing.

Answer 1 discards the entire control design that multimodal containers make possible. Answer 3 reverses the definition's first quality — permanence and repeated use are its opening words. Answer 4 invents a restriction; the definition describes transport equipment generally, and cargo containers are its paradigm case.

The takeaway: the container's legal qualities are the reason a seal applied abroad is evidence at Tema — control is designed around the box.
