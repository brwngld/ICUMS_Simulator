=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The documents that travel with the declaration
BODY:
Section 14 of Act 891 requires documents to be submitted with the goods' entry, and the declaration is only ever as good as the file behind it. The core supporting documents of an ordinary import are the commercial invoice, the packing list, and the transport document — the bill of lading or air waybill — with certificates and permits joining wherever the goods demand them.

Each document proves something different. The commercial invoice proves the commercial facts: who sold what to whom, at what price, on which delivery term. The packing list proves the physical facts: how the goods are packed, how many packages, what each weighs. The transport document proves the movement: which craft carried the goods, from where, to where, and in whose custody.

The officer reads all three against the declaration, because the declaration's particulars are assertions until the documents corroborate them. A quantity on the declaration that the packing list contradicts is a discrepancy before it is an offence — and the discrepancy machinery of section 51 (missing or incomplete documents) exists precisely to catch the file that does not hold together.

The learner's skill is assembling a file that proves itself: every particular traceable to a document, every document consistent with the others.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The commercial invoice and the packing list
BODY:
The commercial invoice is the seller's bill to the buyer, and it is the single most consequential document in a valuation. It names the parties, describes the goods, states quantities and unit prices, declares the currency and the total, and carries the delivery term — the Incoterm that says who bore the freight and insurance, which Module 8's valuation work consumes line by line.

The packing list is the shipment's inventory of itself: package by package, it states what is inside, how many pieces, and the weights and measurements. Where the invoice speaks in money, the packing list speaks in boxes and kilograms.

The two documents answer different questions and must agree with each other: the packing list's total packages and weights must reconcile with the invoice's quantities, and both must match the transport document's counts. A packing list describing forty pallets against an invoice for thirty-nine is exactly the kind of discrepancy section 51's missing-or-incomplete-documents provision exists for.

Both documents are introduced in the Logistics programme; this lesson's concern is what customs does with them — the officer's reading of the commercial facts against the physical facts, and the declaration against both.

Reference: the commercial invoice and the packing list are introduced in the Logistics programme, module Logistics Documentation.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The power to demand and inspect
BODY:
Section 101 of Act 891 gives customs the documentary power the whole supporting-file system rests on: the power to demand invoices and inspect packages. The provision empowers officers to require production of invoices and related documents, and to inspect the goods and their packaging — the legal basis for the officer who asks to see the commercial invoice, and for the officer who opens the carton to compare its contents with the paperwork.

The power's shape teaches the learner what to expect. It is not a fishing expedition: it is a demand for the documents that ordinarily exist for a commercial shipment, and an inspection of the goods against them. A trader who cannot produce an invoice has failed at the documentary layer before any question of valuation arises — and the inspection power means the goods themselves will be compared with whatever paperwork is produced.

The power also explains the professional discipline of document keeping: the file the agent assembles — invoices, packing lists, transport documents, certificates — is not clutter; it is the evidence base the Act's officers are entitled to call for. A clearance assembled without the file is a clearance awaiting a demand it cannot satisfy.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Records: the duty that outlives the clearance
BODY:
"Record" is defined in section 151 as any information kept in the ordinary course of business that pertains to any customs activity, in the nature of a statement, declaration, document, data, book, paper, correspondence, accounts, and financial accounting data. The definition's width is deliberate: the record is not one document type but the whole trail a customs activity leaves — the declaration, its supporting documents, the correspondence, the accounts behind the invoice.

Section 9 then imposes the duty: an owner, importer, consignee, exporter, entry filer or any other person required to keep records under the Act shall maintain them — and where the Act states the retention period, it is a statutory deadline under section 9, stated with its provision. The duty reaches electronic form naturally, because section 151 also defines an "electronic record": a record created, generated, sent, communicated, received, or stored by electronic means. A digitally kept file is a record like a paper one.

Why the duty matters to a document lesson: the commercial invoice and packing list a clearance consumes are the beginning of the record, not the end of it. The audit provisions of section 7 exist because the trail must survive the release — and the next lesson's post-clearance perspective builds on exactly this block.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: False statements: when a document is more than paper
BODY:
The supporting documents are legal statements, and the Act arms them accordingly. A "false statement" is defined in section 151 as a statement made with respect to goods subject to customs control that is false or misleading in a material respect, or a material omission from a required statement or document with respect to goods subject to customs control.

Two features of the definition do the work. Materiality: a typo in a reference number is not the offence; a false description, quantity or value — the particulars the declaration depends on — is. Omission: the definition reaches what is left out of a required statement, not only what is wrongly said, so an incomplete file is not a neutral state.

The consequence chain matters to the learner's professional posture: a document that misstates the goods exposes the declarant to the offence machinery of Module 14 — and the agent who lodged it is implicated, because the agent's own signature is a statement. The discipline that follows is simple and absolute: every particular on every document is verified against the goods and the source papers before it is lodged, because a wrong declaration is not a clerical error to be shrugged off — it is a false statement waiting to be material.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The file as a system: consistency is the proof
BODY:
The document lesson closes with the idea that organises it: the supporting file works as a system, and its proof is consistency. The invoice states the commercial facts; the packing list states the physical facts; the transport document states the movement facts; the declaration states all of them back to customs. When the four agree — quantities, weights, packages, descriptions, values — the file corroborates itself, and the officer's check is fast.

When they disagree, the file impeaches itself: a weight mismatch suggests undeclared goods; a description mismatch suggests misclassification; a value mismatch invites the valuation questions of Module 8. This is why officers read documents against each other before they read them against the goods — the internal consistency check is the fastest discrepancy detector in the clearance.

The learner's deliverable is therefore not a pile of papers but a reconciled file: every particular traceable to a source document, every source document consistent with the next, and every figure in the declaration traceable to the file. That is what "the documents travel with the declaration" means in practice — a system of proof, assembled before it is needed.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Assembling the file for a rice consignment at Tema
BODY:
Step 1. The goods. An importer brings in 2,000 bags of rice in forty containers. The agent opens the file with the commercial invoice: seller abroad, buyer in Accra, description "long-grain white rice", 2,000 bags at a unit price, total value in dollars, delivery term stated.

Step 2. The physical facts. The packing list states the packages: forty containers, fifty bags each, gross and net weights per container. The agent reconciles: two thousand bags, forty containers — the packing list agrees with the invoice's quantity.

Step 3. The movement facts. The bill of lading names the vessel, the loading port, and forty containers. The agent checks container numbers against the packing list's allocation — every box on the list is on the bill.

Step 4. The declaration. The agent lodges the declaration under section 49, and the particulars mirror the file: description, quantity, packages, value, delivery term. Section 14's document requirement is satisfied by the assembled file.

Step 5. The demand. The officer, exercising section 101's power, requires the invoice and inspects two containers. Bags and weights match the packing list; the file corroborates itself.

What does this example demonstrate? A clearance file is a self-corroborating system — invoice, packing list, transport document and declaration in agreement — and a section 101 inspection is answered by consistency.

Key lesson: assemble the file so that every particular proves itself from two directions, and the inspection becomes a formality.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A mismatch found at a foreign port: the lesson from Antwerp
BODY:
Step 1. The shipment. A European trader ships machinery to Antwerp with a packing list stating twelve crates. At inspection, the officers find eleven crates and one pallet of spare parts not on the list.

Step 2. The detection. The mismatch is found by the consistency check, not by contraband hunting: the transport document said twelve crates, the physical count said eleven plus one, and the difference is a document failure before it is anything else. The consignment is held until the file is corrected and the undeclared items are declared.

Step 3. The cost of the gap. The trader loses days, pays storage, and now has an examination history on the lane — all from a packing list that did not match what was loaded.

Step 4. The transfer. The scenario is not Ghanaian, and it does not need to be: the consistency check is the international grammar of document control. Ghana's section 51 (missing or incomplete documents) and section 101 (demand and inspection) produce exactly this outcome on exactly this kind of mismatch.

What does this example demonstrate? A file that disagrees with itself converts a routine clearance into a detained one — and the correction costs more than the care that would have prevented it.

Key lesson: reconcile the file before the officer does; the discrepancy the trader finds is a correction, the discrepancy the officer finds is a case.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: The declaration is only as good as its file
BODY:
A declaration asserts particulars; it does not prove them. The proof is the supporting file — invoice, packing list, transport documents, certificates — and the assertion and the proof must agree. The practical corollary is about sequence: assemble the file first, then lodge the declaration from it, never the reverse. A declaration written from memory and backfilled with documents is a declaration whose particulars were never checked against their sources, and section 151's false-statement definition reaches material omissions as well as wrong statements — so an incomplete file is not merely weak, it is a risk in itself. The professional standard this lesson teaches is a discipline of order: documents verified, then particulars lodged, then the file retained for the audit window.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Keep the file past release
BODY:
The record-keeping duty of section 9 does not end when the goods are released; that is precisely when it begins to matter. Section 7 provides the post-clearance audit — the mechanism by which customs revisits a clearance after the goods have gone — and the audit reads the record the trader kept: the invoice, the packing list, the correspondence, the accounts. Section 151's record definition reaches all of it, including records stored electronically. Where the Act states the retention period in section 9, it is a statutory deadline — keep records for it, stated with its provision. The professional habit is to treat release as the start of the archive, not the end of the matter: the file that cleared the goods is the file that will answer for them.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
An officer exercising section 101's power asks a clearing agent to produce the commercial invoice for a consignment, and inspects two cartons against the packing list. The agent objects that the declaration was lodged correctly and no offence is alleged. Which option best assesses the objection?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The objection is correct: an inspection requires an alleged offence first.
2. The objection is correct, because section 101 applies only to exporters.
3. The objection fails: section 101 empowers officers to demand invoices and related documents and to inspect packages and their contents — the power does not depend on a prior allegation, and the inspection checks the goods against the documents.
4. The objection fails because the officer may also seize the goods permanently at will.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 101 of Act 891 provides the power to demand invoices and inspect packages — the documentary backbone of the supporting-file system this lesson teaches. The power's exercise does not require a pending allegation: demanding the commercial invoice and comparing cartons against the packing list is exactly the consistency check the system is designed for, performed as a routine control or on selection.

Answer 1 invents an allegation precondition that the provision does not carry; the consistency check exists precisely to find discrepancies whether or not an offence is suspected in advance. Answer 2 limits the power with no textual basis — importers, exporters and goods in customs control generally fall within its reach. Answer 4 overstates into an absurdity: seizure is a separate machinery with its own provisions and procedure, not an incident of every inspection.

The takeaway: the section 101 power is the file's reason for existing — produce the documents, let the goods be compared, and the consistency of the file does the talking.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
Which of the following best describes what counts as a "record" under section 151 of Act 891?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Only the paper documents physically filed at the customs office.
2. Only accounting books and financial statements relating to duty payments.
3. Any information kept in the ordinary course of business that pertains to any customs activity — statements, declarations, documents, data, books, papers, correspondence, accounts and financial accounting data — including records created and stored electronically.
4. Only records that the trader voluntarily chooses to keep, with no retention duty attached.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 151 of Act 891 defines a record as any information kept in the ordinary course of business that pertains to any customs activity, in the nature of a statement, declaration, document, data, book, paper, correspondence, accounts, and financial accounting data — and the same section defines an electronic record as a record created, generated, sent, communicated, received, or stored by electronic means, so digital files are records like paper ones. Section 9 then imposes the duty to maintain them on the owner, importer, consignee, exporter, entry filer and others required to keep records.

Answer 1 narrows the definition to paper and to documents lodged at an office — the definition reaches correspondence, data and accounts wherever kept. Answer 2 narrows it to finance; the definition's list runs from statements to correspondence. Answer 4 mistakes a defined, duty-backed category for a voluntary one — section 9's duty is exactly what makes the retention an obligation.

The takeaway: the record is the whole trail, in any medium, kept because the Act requires it — and it must survive release for the audit window.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A declaration describes goods as "agricultural machinery", while the commercial invoice in the file describes the same items as "used combine harvesters", and the packing list's crate markings read "harvesters — used". The difference matters to duty treatment. Which option best states the position?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The declaration controls, because the declaration is the statutory form and the supporting documents are merely informal.
2. The mismatch is immaterial, because the goods are identifiable from either description.
3. The mismatch is a false-statement risk exactly as section 151 defines it — a statement false or misleading in a material respect with respect to goods subject to customs control — because the description difference is material to duty treatment; the file must be reconciled before lodgement.
4. The mismatch is cured automatically once the goods are released.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. Section 151 of Act 891 defines a false statement as a statement made with respect to goods subject to customs control that is false or misleading in a material respect, or a material omission from a required statement or document. The scenario's mismatch is material on its own facts: the difference between "agricultural machinery" and "used combine harvesters" affects classification, condition and potentially duty treatment — the particulars the declaration depends on. A declaration that contradicts its own supporting file asserts something the file disproves.

Answer 1 elevates the declaration above its evidence base; the lesson's whole system runs the other way — the declaration is proved by the file, and a statutory form does not sanitize a contradictory invoice. Answer 2 confuses identifiability with materiality: the goods may be findable, but the description's effect on treatment is the material point. Answer 4 is false — release does not cure a misstatement; the record-keeping duty keeps the file available for post-clearance audit precisely after release.

The takeaway: reconcile description, quantity and value across the file before lodgement — a material mismatch lodged is a false statement waiting for its provision.
