=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The transport document: who carries the goods
BODY:
Every consignment crosses the border on the strength of a transport document — and the mode picks it. Ocean cargo travels on a bill of lading; air cargo travels on an air waybill. Both documents do the same three jobs: they evidence the contract of carriage, they receipt the goods as taken on board, and they identify the party entitled to take delivery at the destination.

The bill of lading names the shipper, the consignee, the vessel, the ports, and the goods with their marks and counts. The air waybill does the same work for the air leg, in the airline's format. Both are the movement facts of the clearance: the craft, the route, the custody — and Module 1's definitions sit behind them, because the ship and the aircraft are defined terms, and the master who signs for the cargo is a defined person too.

Customs reads the transport document for three checks: does the document match the arrival report; do the goods' marks and counts match the packing list; and does the party claiming delivery match the consignee named on the document. The transport document is where the movement facts live — and the clearance cannot begin to prove itself without them.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The master's paper duties
BODY:
The transport documents are not the master's only paper. Act 891 assigns the master a sequence of document duties that make the craft's paper trail a customs instrument.

Section 17 requires the report the master makes on arrival — the craft's own declaration of what it has brought. Section 19 sets the master's duties in relation to the cargo and the conveyance. Section 39 requires the master to deliver an account of cargo before departure: the outbound craft must state what it is carrying out of the country. Section 40 provides the transire — the document the master delivers on arrival for coastwise movements.

The pattern is accountability by paper: every stage of a vessel's contact with the border produces a document the master is responsible for, and each document reconciles with the cargo's own papers. The bill of lading's counts, the arrival report's statement, the account of cargo — three documents that must agree, because they are three views of the same goods at three moments.

The learner reads them together: a transport document that disagrees with the arrival report is a discrepancy at the border's edge, before the goods are even discharged.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The certificate of origin: what it evidences
BODY:
The certificate of origin is the preference document, and its function is evidentiary: it states where the goods come from, in the form the claiming regime requires. Act 891 fixes the legal hook it feeds — section 70 provides for country of origin — and the certificate is the document that carries the origin claim into the clearance.

What the certificate shows, in the form the sources present it: the exporter, the consignee, a description of the goods, the origin criterion or rule the goods satisfy, the reference to the commercial invoice, and the issuing or attesting body's authentication. Each field exists because a customs officer must be able to connect the certificate to the consignment in front of them: the description matches the goods, the invoice reference matches the commercial invoice, the authentication matches the issuing authority's records.

The certificate does not create the origin; it evidences it. The origin rules themselves — preferential and non-preferential — are Module 9's subject. What this lesson requires is the document's place in the file: an origin claim without a certificate is an assertion without evidence, and the officer's check will find that quickly.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The file travels together
BODY:
The transport document, the commercial invoice, the packing list and the certificate of origin move as one chain, and the chain's links are cross-references. The bill of lading's container numbers match the packing list's allocation; the packing list's package counts match the invoice's quantities; the certificate of origin references the commercial invoice's number; the declaration cites them all.

Section 14 requires the documents to be submitted with the entry, and the officer's first read is the chain check: does each document's reference find its target, do the counts agree, does the description run consistently from the transport document through the invoice to the declaration. A chain with a broken link — an invoice number the certificate does not actually reference, a container on the bill but not on the list — is found by the link check before any deeper question arises.

The learner's discipline is to build the chain deliberately: issue and check documents in their real order (transport, invoice, packing list, certificate), cross-reference each to its predecessor, and lodge a declaration that cites the chain it rests on. The file that travels together proves together.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Origin claims and the officer's check
BODY:
When a consignment carries a certificate of origin, the officer's check has a specific shape: the claim is that the goods satisfy an origin rule, and the certificate is the evidence. The check runs: does the certificate's description match the goods; does its quantity match the invoice and the packing list; does its authentication hold; and does the claimed origin regime actually give the benefit claimed.

The distinction this lesson needs: the certificate evidences the claim; the claim's content — which rule the goods satisfy, which regime applies — is Module 9's subject. A learner who treats the certificate as a magic pass misses the point of the check: the certificate is checked because claims must be evidenced, and evidenced claims can still be wrong, which is why Module 9's verification and denial machinery exists.

What the check means for the file: the certificate is not an ornament to the chain — it is the document that turns an origin assertion into a claim that can be tested. Its data points are the test's handles, which is why the issuing body fills them accurately and the agent verifies them before lodgement.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Document duties as the system's reliability
BODY:
This lesson's provisions — the master's paper duties, the section 14 submission requirement, the certificate's evidentiary role — are the reliability layer of the whole clearance system. Every later module assumes the file exists and agrees: classification (Module 7) reads the description the invoice and declaration state; valuation (Module 8) reads the invoice's prices and terms; origin (Module 9) reads the certificate; audit (Module 14) reads the whole retained trail.

That dependence is why the master's account must reconcile with the manifest, why section 14's documents must accompany the entry, and why the certificate's fields must match the consignment. The system's reliability is not enforced by goodwill — it is enforced by provisions that make each document a legal statement, each statement accountable, and each discrepancy discoverable.

The learner closes the lesson with a single integrated picture: mode and craft (lesson 2.1), the commercial and physical facts (lesson 2.2), the movement and origin documents (this lesson) — one file, one chain, one system of proof.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Cocoa to Antwerp: assembling the export file
BODY:
Step 1. The movement. A Ghanaian cocoa processor ships 600 bags of cocoa beans to a buyer in Antwerp. The mode is ocean, so the transport document is a bill of lading naming the vessel, the loading port of Tema, the discharge port of Antwerp, and the consignee.

Step 2. The commercial file. The commercial invoice states the parties, the description "cocoa beans, grade A", 600 bags at the unit price, the total in euros, and the delivery term. The packing list states 600 bags, the weight per bag, and the container allocation. The agent reconciles: invoice quantity equals packing list bags equals bill of lading count.

Step 3. The origin document. The buyer's preference claim needs a certificate of origin. The issuing body attests a certificate describing the goods identically, referencing the commercial invoice's number, and certifying the origin. The agent checks every field against the file: description, quantity, invoice reference — each one matching.

Step 4. The export entry. The export declaration is lodged with the account of cargo due before departure under section 39, and the master's papers and the agent's file agree: one consignment, one count, one description, throughout.

Step 5. The arrival. At Antwerp, the buyer's broker lodges the import declaration with the same certificate — and the origin claim the Ghanaian file built is the claim the Belgian officer tests.

What does this example demonstrate? The export file and the import file are two halves of one chain; the certificate of origin is the link that carries the claim across.

Key lesson: build the chain at the origin, because the destination can only test what the origin assembled.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: An air waybill file lands at Rotterdam
BODY:
Step 1. The movement. A Shenzhen electronics exporter ships prototype devices to a buyer in Rotterdam by air. The transport document is an air waybill — the air leg's document — naming the shipper, the consignee, the flight routing, and the pieces and weights.

Step 2. The mode's consequences. The craft is an aircraft, defined in Act-shaped laws as the apparatus used for transport by air; the bill of lading is irrelevant to this movement, and the air waybill carries the movement facts instead. The goods are high-value, low-volume — the air waybill's piece count and weight are the checks.

Step 3. The consistency read. The Rotterdam officer compares the air waybill's pieces and weights with the commercial invoice's stated quantity and the packing list's allocation. All three agree; the description matches; the clearance proceeds on documents.

Step 4. The counterfactual. Had the air waybill said eight pieces and the packing list nine, the same consistency check would have found the gap — no contraband intelligence required, just the chain disagreeing with itself.

What does this example demonstrate? The transport document changes with the mode — bill of lading for the sea, air waybill for the air — but the system of checks does not: movement facts, commercial facts, physical facts, in agreement.

Key lesson: the mode picks the document; the discipline of consistency is the same in both.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Transport documents carry facts, not conclusions
BODY:
A bill of lading or air waybill is evidence of the movement — the craft, the route, the custody, the counts. It is not a customs decision: it does not classify the goods, value them, or release them. The learner's discipline is to keep the document's role straight when reading a clearance: the transport document proves the movement facts, and the movement facts are then checked against the commercial and physical facts. The same discipline runs the other way — a transport document's description of the goods is the carrier's receipt, not the trader's classification, so an officer who needs the goods' tariff description reads the declaration and the invoice, not the carrier's shorthand. Documents have roles; conclusions come from the provisions, applied to the roles' facts.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: An origin claim without a certificate is a hope
BODY:
An origin claim without a certificate is a hope, not a claim — and the hope is exactly what the officer's check will expose. A preferential origin claim has two parts: the goods satisfy a rule, and the claim is evidenced by the certificate the regime requires. Take the certificate away and the second part fails — and the first part cannot be tested, because the evidence is what makes the claim checkable. The practical rule for the agent: no certificate, no claim — advise the client to obtain the document before lodgement, not after the officer asks. The reason is the check's shape: the officer verifies the certificate against the goods and the file, and an origin asserted in the declaration alone gives the officer nothing to verify. The certificate is the claim's body; the declaration merely carries it.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A consignment of shea butter arrives by sea. The officer wants to verify the goods' movement facts. Which document carries them, and why?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The commercial invoice, because it is the most detailed document in the file.
2. The bill of lading, because the mode is ocean and the transport document evidences the craft, route, custody and counts of the goods.
3. The certificate of origin, because it names the exporter and is the most authoritative document.
4. The declaration alone, because it is the statutory form.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. The transport document is the document that evidences the movement: for ocean cargo, the bill of lading names the vessel, the loading and discharge ports, the shipper and consignee, and the goods with their marks and counts. The movement facts — which craft carried the goods, from where, in whose custody — live there, which is why the officer's first read compares the bill of lading with the arrival report and the packing list.

Answer 1 confuses the commercial facts with the movement facts: the invoice is the most detailed document about price and parties, but it does not evidence carriage. Answer 3 mistakes the certificate's role — origin evidence, not movement evidence. Answer 4 elevates the declaration while forgetting it asserts the movement facts the transport document proves.

The takeaway: mode picks the transport document, and the transport document is where the movement facts are proven — the file's other documents are checked against it.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
A trader lodges an import declaration claiming preferential duty for a consignment of processed goods, but the file contains no certificate of origin. The officer asks for it. Which option best describes the position?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The declaration alone suffices, because the declarant's statement is the claim and needs no document.
2. The officer should grant the preference and request the certificate afterwards, since origin is checked only after release.
3. The certificate is the evidence that makes the origin claim checkable; without it the claim is an unevidenced assertion, and the trader should obtain the document before lodgement rather than after the officer asks.
4. The absence of a certificate converts the goods into prohibited goods.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3. A preferential claim has two parts: the goods satisfy an origin rule, and the claim is evidenced by the certificate the regime requires. Section 70 of Act 891 provides the country-of-origin hook the claim feeds, and section 14's document requirement is where the certificate enters the file. Without the certificate there is nothing to verify — no authentication, no invoice reference, no origin criterion stated — so the claim cannot be tested and cannot be granted as claimed.

Answer 1 treats an assertion as proof; the whole documentary system this module teaches runs the other way. Answer 2 invents a check-now-verify-later rule that reverses the burden in practice — the claim is evidenced before it is granted, and post-clearance machinery exists for other failures, not for a claim that was never evidenced. Answer 4 confuses an evidentiary gap with a goods prohibition; the goods are not prohibited, the claim is unevidenced.

The takeaway: the certificate is the claim's body — obtain it before lodgement, because an unevidenced origin claim is a hope, not a claim.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A vessel's agent notices that the bill of lading states 400 packages while the master's account of cargo due before departure under section 39 states 410. Which option best describes the professional response?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The difference is immaterial, because the master's account is an internal document.
2. The discrepancy must be resolved before departure: the bill of lading, the arrival report and the account of cargo are three views of the same goods, and documents that disagree with each other are discrepancies at the border's edge.
3. The bill of lading should be amended to match the account, without checking which is correct.
4. The account of cargo is optional for departing vessels.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2. The lesson's document duties run as one reconciling system: section 17 provides the master's report on arrival, section 39 the account of cargo before departure, and the bill of lading evidences what was shipped. Three views of the same goods at three moments — they must agree, because a disagreement is a discrepancy in the craft's own papers before it is anything else. Resolving it means establishing the true count from the source documents, then correcting whichever document is wrong — not papering over the gap.

Answer 1 dismisses the discrepancy by labelling a statutory document internal; the account of cargo is the departure-side statement the border works from. Answer 3 amends documents to match instead of establishing the facts first — an amendment in the wrong direction turns a discrepancy into a false statement. Answer 4 invents an optionality the section does not carry.

The takeaway: the craft's paper trail is a reconciliation exercise — report, bill, account — and the professional resolves disagreements by finding the truth, not by aligning the paper to convenience.
