=== ASSESSMENT Q1 | tests lesson 3.1 ===
QUESTION:
A consignment of machine spare parts travels from a supplier in Turkey to a buyer in Kumasi. The buyer's file lists five milestones from the journey, but the pages have fallen out of order: release of the goods by the importing country's customs; collection of the goods from the supplier; export formalities in the exporting country; verification of the goods and proof of delivery at the buyer's warehouse; the main international carriage.

Which option lists the milestones in the order the journey actually follows?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Collection of the goods from the supplier; the main international carriage; export formalities in the exporting country; release of the goods by the importing country's customs; verification of the goods and proof of delivery at the buyer's warehouse.
2. Collection of the goods from the supplier; export formalities in the exporting country; release of the goods by the importing country's customs; the main international carriage; verification of the goods and proof of delivery at the buyer's warehouse.
3. Collection of the goods from the supplier; export formalities in the exporting country; the main international carriage; release of the goods by the importing country's customs; verification of the goods and proof of delivery at the buyer's warehouse.
4. Collection of the goods from the supplier; export formalities in the exporting country; the main international carriage; verification of the goods and proof of delivery at the buyer's warehouse; release of the goods by the importing country's customs.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The stages of the journey run in a fixed order, and the three formal milestones hold the key positions. Goods are collected from the supplier and moved inland to the export point; the exporting country's formalities must then be completed, because the goods may not lawfully leave until the export declaration is in order. The main international carriage follows, and only on arrival do the importing country's formalities begin. Release is the gate between the arrival stages and everything that comes after: until customs grants it, containers cannot leave the terminal and consignments cannot leave a customs facility. Verification and the proof of delivery come last, closing the journey at the buyer's own premises.

Each flawed option fails on exactly one transposition. Answer 1 places export formalities after the main carriage, as if goods could sail first and be cleared to leave afterwards. Answer 2 places import release before the main carriage, although release can only follow arrival in the importing country. Answer 4 places verification before release, which would deliver goods that are still under customs control.

The takeaway: collection and clearance at origin, carriage, release at destination, then verification. Learn the gates, not just the list.

=== ASSESSMENT Q2 | tests lesson 3.1 ===
QUESTION:
A soap manufacturer in Kumasi has 9 pallets of cartoned soap for a buyer in Europe, too small to fill a container. Its freight forwarder books the pallets into a consolidated container together with other shippers' goods. At the destination port the container is trucked to a container freight station, where the combined load is separated so that each consignee can receive its own goods.

Which option best describes what this arrangement involves?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The pallets travel with less handling than a full container load, so the manufacturer can use lighter packaging and fewer marks.
2. The container travels as the manufacturer's own sealed unit, because a consolidated container is reserved for one shipper until it is full.
3. Because the forwarder completes the export formalities for the combined container at origin, the goods can be delivered to the consignee abroad without import formalities or customs release.
4. The manufacturer shares the container with other shippers and pays only for the space its pallets occupy, accepting an extra handling point at each end and the need for accurate marking so that its consignment can be identified and separated at the destination.
CORRECT: 4
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 4.

Consolidation combines the goods of several shippers into one container or vehicle so that each pays only for the space its goods occupy; it is the standard service for consignments too small to fill a container. The saving has a price the lesson states plainly: consolidation adds an extra handling point at each end, because the combined load must be built at origin and separated again at destination, and it depends on accurate marking, because every consignment in the combined load must be identifiable so that it can be recovered when the load is separated at the container freight station. This is the handoff idea at work: at the deconsolidation handoff, goods, marks and documents must agree, or consignments end up in the wrong pile.

Answer 1 reverses the handling truth: consolidated cargo is handled more, not less, which is why packaging and marks matter more, not less. Answer 2 misdescribes the service as the manufacturer's own sealed container, which is the full container load this small consignment is trying to avoid. Answer 3 repeats a stubborn misconception: export formalities completed at origin never replace import formalities and release in the destination country.

The takeaway: consolidation trades a freight saving for extra handling and a hard dependence on accurate marking at every handoff.

=== ASSESSMENT Q3 | tests lesson 3.1 ===
QUESTION:
An importer in Accra checks its tracking system every morning. Two weeks ago the status of its container changed to arrived at the port. Confident that the shipment is now under control, the manager waits for the goods to be delivered. No import declaration has been lodged, no collection truck has been booked, and time-based charges at the terminal have begun to accumulate.

Which statement best explains the situation?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The tracking system must be faulty, because a container that shows as arrived should reach the buyer's premises automatically.
2. Tracking shows the location and status of goods, but it does not lodge declarations, obtain release, or arrange collection; control comes from acting on the information, so the importer's side must now complete the formalities and book the truck.
3. The shipping line is responsible for clearing and delivering imported goods, because goods in the carrier's custody remain the carrier's responsibility until they are delivered.
4. The goods must be waiting for a customs inspection that no one can influence, so the importer was right to wait.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Shipment tracking monitors the location and progress of goods; it does not itself perform any stage of the journey. The lesson's warning is exact: tracking shows location and status, but it does not clear customs, correct a wrong document, or load a truck. Control over a journey comes from what participants do with tracking information. The arrived event here is real, but arrival only opens the destination stages: the import declaration must be lodged, any duties and taxes settled, release obtained, and collection transport arranged. Because nobody acted, the goods sit after arrival, and once the allowed free period ends, storage and related charges begin to accumulate day by day.

Answer 1 blames the system, but the event is genuine; what failed is the response to it. Answer 3 confuses custody with responsibility: the carrier carries the goods, yet the import formalities belong to the importer's side, and responsibility follows the trade terms agreed in the contract rather than the physical location of the goods. Answer 4 treats delay as untouchable, but the importer's own action, a prompt declaration and a truck booked against the expected release, was exactly what the situation demanded.

The takeaway: a party who watches a tracking screen without acting on it has observation, not control.

=== ASSESSMENT Q4 | tests lesson 3.2 ===
QUESTION:
A retailer in Hamburg buys a season's stock of consumer electronics from a manufacturer in Shenzhen. The order has three parts: the launch quantity, needed at a fixed launch date; replenishment stock during the season, too time-sensitive to spend weeks at sea but not urgent enough to justify the cost of flying; and the bulk of the volume, planned months in advance and not urgent. Overland rail services connect the two regions.

Which option assigns each part of the order to the most appropriate mode?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Launch quantity by air, replenishment stock by rail, bulk volume by sea.
2. Launch quantity by sea, replenishment stock by air, bulk volume by rail.
3. Launch quantity by rail, replenishment stock by sea, bulk volume by air.
4. All three parts by sea, because a single order should travel by a single mode at the lowest cost per unit.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

Mode selection matches each part of the demand to the mode whose profile fits it. The launch quantity must hit a fixed date, and a missed launch forfeits sales, so it justifies air freight, the fastest and most expensive mode, used for the smallest part of the volume. The season's replenishment is the middle case the lesson describes precisely: too time-sensitive to spend weeks at sea, not urgent enough to justify flying, which is exactly where rail sits, faster than sea and cheaper than air, where the network connects the regions. The bulk of the volume is planned months in advance and not urgent, so it takes sea freight, the slowest mode but the lowest cost per unit over long distance, and that cheapness matters most at the largest quantity.

Answer 2 puts the bulk on the most expensive mode and the launch on the slowest, inverting every profile. Answer 3 is the same inversion in the other direction. Answer 4 fails because one order may sensibly travel by several modes; the lesson's comparison is a ladder on which each part of the demand finds its rung, and cost per unit is only one factor among urgency, quantity, and reliability.

The takeaway: mode selection is a portfolio decision; place each part of the order on the speed-and-cost ladder.

=== ASSESSMENT Q5 | tests lesson 3.2 ===
QUESTION:
A container is packed with goods at a factory, sealed, and carried by truck to a port. It is lifted onto a vessel, sailed to another continent, lifted onto a train at the destination port, and hauled to an inland terminal, where a truck delivers it to the warehouse. The goods are never taken out of the container. The journey was booked as separate contracts, one for each leg, with no single operator responsible for the whole movement.

Which statement best describes this arrangement?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. It is multimodal transport, because more than one mode of transport is used.
2. It is sea freight with incidental road and rail legs, because the vessel performs the longest part of the journey.
3. It is intermodal transport, because the goods travel in the same loading unit across the changes of mode without the goods themselves being handled; the separate contracts mean it is not multimodal, which would require a single contract and one responsible operator.
4. It is multimodal and intermodal at the same time, because packing the goods in one container automatically creates a single contract with one responsible operator.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The two terms answer different questions. Intermodal transport describes how the goods physically travel: in one and the same loading unit, typically a container, across two or more modes without the goods themselves being handled at the changes of mode. Multimodal transport describes how the journey is contracted: carriage by at least two modes under a single multimodal transport contract, with one operator responsible for the goods from origin to destination. The movement described keeps the goods in one sealed container from factory to warehouse, which is intermodal in physical form, but the contracts were arranged separately for each leg, so no single operator answers for the whole journey and the multimodal definition is not met.

Answer 1 commits the most common confusion in this topic: the number of modes does not make a journey multimodal; the single contract and the single responsible operator do. Answer 2 dismisses the arrangement as one mode with extras, ignoring that a deliberate combination of modes is exactly what both terms describe. Answer 4 asserts the very equivalence the lesson denies: packing goods in a container says nothing about contracts, and the two features are independent even though both can appear in one shipment.

The takeaway: ask who is responsible and under how many contracts, and whether the goods are handled at the changes of mode; the answers, not the labels, name the arrangement.

=== ASSESSMENT Q6 | tests lesson 3.2 ===
QUESTION:
A shipping line has carried an importer's container across the ocean and discharged it at a seaport. The goods must now reach the buyer's warehouse at a specific street address in an inland city. A colleague argues that the sea freight should simply continue to the warehouse door, and that no other mode of transport is needed to complete the journey.

Which option best identifies the flaw in this reasoning?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. There is no flaw: a booked sea freight service runs from the supplier's door to the buyer's warehouse, and the port call is only a technical pause.
2. Every mode's network has edges: the sea network runs between ports, and a port is never the final destination, so the inland leg lies outside sea freight's reach; delivery to a specific address is road freight's work, which is why road performs the last mile of almost every journey.
3. The container must be unpacked at the port and the goods re-packed into railway wagons, because only rail freight is allowed to leave a port area.
4. Air freight should take over the inland leg, because it is the fastest mode and therefore the appropriate mode for every part of a journey.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Every mode runs on its own transport network, its system of routes, facilities and connections, and every network has edges. The sea network runs between ports, and the lesson states plainly that a port is never the final destination. A warehouse at a specific street address in an inland city lies outside the sea network, however good the ocean service is, so the journey cannot end with the vessel. Road freight's network is the only one that reaches ordinary addresses, which is why road carries the first and last legs of nearly every international journey and performs the last-mile delivery almost always.

Answer 1 describes a door-to-door sea service that does not exist; the quality of the booking does not extend a network. Answer 3 invents a rule about port areas: containers are routinely trucked or railed inland as whole sealed units, and no mode is confined to the port by permission. Answer 4 assigns the inland leg to air freight, whose network is the most limited of all, joining cargo airports only, and whose cost profile fits no ordinary inland delivery.

The takeaway: map the networks before costing the options; the modes that do not physically connect origin to destination are not cheaper alternatives, they are not alternatives at all.

=== ASSESSMENT Q7 | tests lesson 3.3 ===
QUESTION:
A company exports shea butter to Europe. This season it ships the butter in sealed steel drums, each drum handled, marked and counted as a unit. Next season a larger contract moves the same butter pumped directly into the heated tank of a tanker vessel, with no packaging at all.

Which statement best describes how the cargo classification changes between the two shipments?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The drummed butter is general cargo and the tankered butter is liquid bulk, because classification follows the form in which the goods travel, not the identity of the product.
2. The classification does not change, because cargo type is a permanent property of the product.
3. Both shipments are containerised cargo, because drums and tanks are both types of containers.
4. The drummed butter is liquid bulk and the tankered butter is general cargo, because the size of the contract decides the classification.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

Classification follows the form in which the goods travel, not the identity or the value of the product. In sealed steel drums, the butter moves as countable, packaged units, each drum handled, marked and checked individually, which is general cargo. Pumped loose into the heated tank of a tanker, the butter passes directly into the carrying compartment with no packaging at all, and nothing separates the goods from the tank; that is the definition of liquid bulk. The product has not changed, only the form in which it travels, and the classification changes with it, taking its packaging, handling equipment, storage arrangements and documents along.

Answer 2 is the beginner error the lesson's important note dismantles: cargo type is not a permanent property stamped on the product. Answer 3 misreads both forms: drums are transport packaging, not standardised freight containers, and cargo pumped into a vessel's tank is the textbook case of bulk rather than containerised cargo. Answer 4 swaps the two classes and then justifies the swap by contract size, which plays no part in classification; a larger contract changes the quantity, not the form of travel.

The takeaway: ask how the goods will travel, in packages or loose, in a hold or in a tank, and classify from that answer.

=== ASSESSMENT Q8 | tests lesson 3.3 ===
QUESTION:
A carton design has served a company well on short domestic deliveries by air within one region: dry handling, few lifts, and little stacking. The company now exports the same product in the same cartons on an ocean voyage of several weeks.

Which option best explains why the packaging may need to change?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Packaging is standard across all journeys, so the same cartons will perform identically at sea; only the transport documents change.
2. Only dangerous goods require packaging chosen for the journey; ordinary products may travel in any sound carton.
3. Packaging must match the whole journey: an ocean voyage brings weeks of humidity and salt-laden air, stacking beneath other cargo, and repeated lifting between truck, warehouse and ship, so the export cartons need sturdier construction and protection against moisture.
4. The change is needed only because sea freight is slower than air freight, so the cartons simply need to be made heavier to survive the longer time.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

Packaging serves protection and handling across the entire journey, so it must be matched to every stress the chain will apply, not to the name of the mode alone. The lesson's own contrast is exactly this one: a carton adequate for a short, dry air trip may fail on an ocean voyage of weeks through humid, salt-laden air, stacked beneath other cargo and lifted repeatedly between truck, warehouse and ship. Export packaging is therefore normally sturdier than packaging for a short local delivery, and protection against moisture becomes part of the design rather than an optional extra.

Answer 1 is the assumption the lesson corrects: packaging is not standard across journeys, because journeys differ in what they do to the box. Answer 2 narrows journey-matched packaging to dangerous goods; regulation does impose certified packaging on dangerous goods, but every consignment's packaging must still suit its own chain, as perishables, fragile goods and ordinary exports all show. Answer 4 reduces the problem to time, yet weeks in a dry, unstacked store would harm nothing; the enemies are moisture, compression and repeated handling, and the design must answer those specific stresses.

The takeaway: matching packaging to the mode and to every handling step along the chain is a core logistics decision, not an afterthought.

=== ASSESSMENT Q9 | tests lesson 3.3 ===
QUESTION:
A consolidated container carrying several exporters' consignments arrives at a container freight station abroad, where staff unpack and separate the contents. One exporter's cartons carry no identification marks at all: no consignee code, no destination, no package numbers, only plain brown cartons. The other consignments are separated within hours; the unmarked cartons cannot be attributed to any consignment.

Which statement best identifies what went wrong and why marking matters?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The freight station staff were at fault, because deconsolidation should proceed without checking packages against documents.
2. The exporter's cartons lacked the identification marks that tie each package to its consignment and to the shipping documents, so at the separation point nobody could read which consignment the cartons belonged to; marks are the package's side of the conversation with the paperwork.
3. The cartons should have carried dangerous goods labels instead, because only hazard labels identify whose goods a package contains.
4. Nothing was wrong with the cartons, because the container number and seal number on the outside of the container identify every package inside it.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Cargo marking turns an anonymous box into an addressed, instructable unit. Identification marks, the consignee's code, the destination, the package number within the consignment, tie each package to its consignment and to the shipping documents, which record the same marks, counts and weights. At deconsolidation the receiver separates the combined load precisely by reading the marks against the documents: carton 137 of 1,000 is expected, counted and signed for because the documents say it exists. Where the marks are absent, the check has nothing to read, the consignment cannot be attributed, and the result is delay, misdelivery or a disputed claim. This is also why the journey lesson calls consolidation a service that depends on accurate marking.

Answer 1 blames the station's staff, but the failure was created at packing, and the staff did exactly what the process requires by checking the cargo against the documents. Answer 3 confuses the layers of marking: hazard labels carry safety information for handlers and emergency services, not consignment identity. Answer 4 fails because the container and seal numbers identify the box, not the several shippers' consignments mixed inside a consolidated load.

The takeaway: mark the packages to match the documents; a package that cannot be identified from its marks will sooner or later be handled wrongly.

=== ASSESSMENT Q10 | tests lesson 3.4 ===
QUESTION:
A receiving warehouse in Kumasi is expecting an imported consignment of 480 cartons of bottled fruit juice. Before the goods arrive, the team needs to know how the consignment is packed: how many cartons there are, how many bottles each carton contains, the gross and net weights, and how the cartons are arranged on pallets. The commercial invoice is already in the file.

Which statement best describes what the team needs?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Nothing further: the commercial invoice already provides all of this, because it describes the goods in detail and states the total value of the consignment.
2. The packing list, which describes the physical consignment, the packages, the quantities per package, the weights and the dimensions, and which carries no prices, because its purpose is physical rather than financial.
3. The pro forma invoice, which is issued after delivery and lists the physical contents of each carton in detail.
4. The bill of lading, which itemises the contents of every carton the carrier received.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The two commercial-family documents describe the same consignment from two angles, and the lesson's division is sharp: the commercial invoice describes the sale, while the packing list describes the boxes. The invoice records the parties, the description, the quantities, the unit prices and the total value, and the currency; it leads payment and supports customs valuation. The packing list is deliberately physical: how many cartons, how many units each carton contains, gross and net weights, dimensions and the arrangement on pallets, and it carries no prices, because its purpose is not financial. Warehouse staff pick, count and receive against it, carriers plan loading and stowage from it, and customs compare what is physically present against it.

Answer 1 assumes the invoice covers the physical detail, but its figures serve the transaction, and nothing on it tells the team how the cartons are packed. Answer 3 misstates the pro forma invoice twice: it is issued before dispatch, as a quotation in invoice form, to confirm terms or arrange payment, not after delivery. Answer 4 gives the bill of lading a function it does not have; it acknowledges receipt of the goods and evidences the contract of carriage, and it does not itemise the contents of each carton.

The takeaway: the invoice is for the sale, the packing list is for the boxes, and the two must agree completely.

=== ASSESSMENT Q11 | tests lesson 3.4 ===
QUESTION:
A Ghanaian importer has two consignments arriving this week from the same European seller: a container of ceramic tiles by sea, travelling under a bill of lading, and a pallet of urgent machine parts by air, travelling under an air waybill. The importer's agent collects both consignments at the destination.

Which statement correctly describes how the two consignments can be claimed?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Both consignments are claimed by presenting the original transport document, because every transport document is a document of title.
2. Neither consignment requires any document, because goods are delivered to whoever comes to collect them.
3. The air consignment is claimed by presenting the original air waybill, which transfers title to the goods, while the sea container is delivered to the named consignee without any document.
4. The sea container is claimed with the properly issued original bill of lading, because certain forms of that document control who may claim delivery of the goods, while the air cargo is delivered to the consignee named on the air waybill without an original being presented, because the air waybill is non-negotiable and not a document of title.
CORRECT: 4
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 4.

The bill of lading is the classic document serving three functions: receipt for the goods, evidence of the contract of carriage, and, for certain forms, document of title. The title function is what makes the original valuable: possession of the properly issued original controls who may claim delivery at destination, which is why goods travelling under it can even be sold while still at sea. The air waybill shares the first two functions only. It is always non-negotiable and never a document of title, so there is no original to present at the destination airport: the cargo is delivered to the consignee named on the document, and the agent's work is to complete the import formalities, not to surrender a title paper.

Answer 1 generalises the title function to every transport document, which is precisely the trap; only certain ocean bills carry it, and neither the air waybill nor the road consignment note does. Answer 2 imagines delivery without records, but every handover is made against documents. Answer 3 swaps the characters of the two documents entirely.

The takeaway: receipt and evidence of contract belong to every transport document; the document-of-title function belongs to the ocean bill of lading alone.

=== ASSESSMENT Q12 | tests lesson 3.4 ===
QUESTION:
A supermarket in Tema orders 300 cartons of rice from a distributor. Its receiving staff count 296 cartons, record the shortage on the delivery documents before signing, and the driver countersigns. A week later the distributor's commercial invoice arrives, claiming payment for 300 cartons.

Which statement best describes correct practice for the supermarket?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Pay the invoice in full, because the commercial invoice is the seller's formal statement of the sale and its figures govern the payment.
2. Adjust the delivery record to show 300 cartons, so that the records agree with the invoice and payment can proceed smoothly.
3. Before paying, match the order, the delivery record and the invoice; the three-way match fails for the 4 missing cartons, so payment for 300 should be held and the shortage resolved against the delivery record that both parties signed.
4. Pay for 296 cartons and consider the matter closed without informing the distributor, because a payment below the invoice amount requires no explanation.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

Before paying, a buyer matches three records: the order, the delivery record and the invoice. This three-way match exists precisely for the situation described. The order says 300, the invoice says 300, but the delivery record, honestly made at the handover and countersigned by the driver, says 296 received and 4 short. The match therefore fails for the missing cartons, and payment for 300 should be held until the shortage is resolved under the agreed terms, against the evidence both parties signed. The proof of delivery can do this job only because the discrepancy was recorded at the moment of delivery, while the driver was still present.

Answer 1 treats the invoice as governing payment, but the invoice records the sale, not the receipt; payment follows evidence of what was actually delivered. Answer 2 proposes the one thing the lesson forbids: altering the record so that the documents agree. A record changed to match the invoice is not a record but a guess with a signature on it, and the buyer loses the evidence needed to resolve the claim. Answer 4 quietly underpays and closes the matter, yet discrepancies must be reported and resolved openly, because an unexplained difference leaves both parties' records wrong.

The takeaway: sign for what you counted, match documents against each other, and let payment follow the verified record.
