=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Why goods movement needs documents
BODY:
Think about collecting a parcel from a delivery office. You do not simply reach behind the counter and take the box. You identify yourself, and you sign a record showing that you received the parcel. That small everyday exchange contains the whole idea of this lesson: goods cannot move far without records moving with them.

Logistics works the same way, on a much larger scale. A shipping line will not load a container on trust. A warehouse will not release goods because a driver says they are expected. Customs will not release an import because a trader promises that the goods are correctly described. In every case, a document does the talking.

Logistics documentation is the documents and records used to support, control, and verify logistics activities. Behind it lies a simple principle: goods move physically, but information must move ahead of the goods and with them. Before a refrigerated container of frozen fish leaves a cold store in Tema, information about it must already have reached the shipping line, the port and the buyer, and while the goods travel, their documents travel too.

The main purposes of logistics documentation are the following.

1. Instructions and contracts
Documents tell every participant what to do and record what was agreed. A dispatch instruction tells a warehouse what to load and where to send it, and a transport document records the agreement under which a carrier moves the goods.

2. Receipts
Documents confirm that goods were actually handed over or received, protecting both sides with a record of the handover.

3. Evidence of the right to claim the goods
On some shipments, holding the right document is what allows a person to claim the goods at destination. The document controls who may collect them.

4. Declarations to authorities
Documents declare to customs and other authorities what the goods are, what they are worth and where they come from, so that the correct rules can be applied.

5. Records supporting payment
Invoices and delivery confirmations allow the seller to bill the buyer, and allow the buyer to verify that what it is asked to pay for was actually delivered.

One shipment may involve many documents, but they belong to a small number of families, and the next block introduces them.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The families of logistics documents
BODY:
Although a single international shipment can generate a thick file of paperwork, nearly every document in the file belongs to one of five families, and each family answers one question about the shipment.

1. Commercial documents describe the sale that the goods carry out. The main examples are the commercial invoice, the pro forma invoice and the packing list.

2. Transport documents describe the carriage of the goods. The bill of lading covers sea transport, the air waybill covers air transport, and the road consignment note covers road delivery. Each is issued when the carrier receives the goods.

3. Certificates provide independent confirmation of particular facts about the goods, such as where they come from or their state of health. Common examples are the certificate of origin, phytosanitary and health certificates, and quality certificates.

4. Customs declarations declare the goods to the customs authorities of the countries involved, so that the correct customs rules and charges can be applied.

5. Delivery records close the journey. The delivery note travels with the goods to the receiver, and the proof of delivery confirms that the handover happened.

The families work together. The customs declaration is prepared from the details on the commercial invoice and the packing list, the transport document links the goods to the carrier, and the delivery records prove that the goods arrived. Notice also that the same core details, such as quantities, weights and descriptions, appear again and again across the families. That repetition is deliberate, because different participants read different documents, and it is also why errors spread so easily, a point this lesson returns to.

One placement note before we continue. This lesson teaches what each document is for and when it appears. The deep treatment of every document, including how they are examined, corrected and matched, belongs to Module 6, which is devoted entirely to logistics documentation. Everything here is the foundation that module will build on.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The details logistics documents record
BODY:
Although logistics documents serve different purposes, most record a similar core of information about the shipment. Learning this core now makes every later document easier to read, because you will know what to look for.

1. The parties
The names and addresses of the sender and the receiver. In international trade the sender is known as the shipper and the receiver as the consignee. Documents may also name the agents or carriers involved in the movement.

2. Description of the goods
What the goods are, described clearly enough to identify them: frozen shrimp, ceramic floor tiles, diesel engine spare parts. Vague descriptions such as general goods invite questions, because nobody can verify what is really being moved.

3. Quantities and packages
How many cartons, bags, drums or pallets, and how many units each package contains. Quantities are the most commonly checked details in logistics, so they must be identical everywhere they appear.

4. Weights and measurements
The gross weight including packaging, the net weight of the goods themselves, and where relevant the volume or dimensions of the packages. Carriers need weights to plan loads safely, and authorities use them to verify declarations.

5. Origin and destination
Where the goods start their journey, where they are going, and the specific points where they are loaded and discharged.

6. Dates
When a document was issued and when the goods were dispatched, picked up or delivered. Dates create the timeline every participant works to.

7. Marks, numbers and references
Packages carry markings that identify them, and each shipment normally carries a shipment reference number, a unique identifier assigned to a shipment or logistics transaction for tracking and recordkeeping. The reference is how everyone involved points to the same shipment without confusion, in conversations, emails and computer systems.

8. Values and charges
The value of the goods and, where applicable, the freight charges, including whether the transport cost has been paid or remains payable at destination.

9. Handling instructions
Directions such as keep refrigerated or fragile, which tell everyone who touches the cargo how to treat it.

Because the same details appear on many documents, an error in one is easily copied into the others. Logistics professionals therefore check documents against each other, not against memory.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Commercial invoices
BODY:
The commercial documents describe the sale that the movement of goods carries out. The most important is the commercial invoice, a document issued by a seller that provides details of goods sold, their value, and the commercial transaction.

A commercial invoice typically shows the seller and the buyer, a description of the goods, the quantities, the unit prices and the total value, the currency, and the references linking the invoice to the order and the shipment. It is much more than a request for money. It performs three jobs at once.

1. It records the commercial transaction
The invoice is the seller's formal statement of what was sold, to whom, at what price and under what conditions. If a disagreement arises about what was agreed, the invoice is central evidence.

2. It supports payment
Buyers use the invoice to arrange payment, and where banks are involved in international payment arrangements, the banks examine the invoice together with the other documents. Payment flows through documents, and the invoice leads them.

3. It supports customs valuation
Customs authorities decide duties and taxes on the basis of the declared value of the goods, and the commercial invoice is the document that declares that value. An invoice that states a value which is not true misleads the authority and puts the shipment at risk.

A related document is the pro forma invoice, an advance copy of an invoice issued before the goods are dispatched. The seller uses it to set out the proposed details of the sale, and the buyer uses it to confirm the terms, arrange payment or apply for permits. The pro forma invoice is a quotation in invoice form, not a demand for payment. The commercial invoice, by contrast, is issued for the actual shipment and demands payment for goods actually sold.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The packing list
BODY:
The packing list, a document describing the contents, quantities, packaging, weights, or dimensions of goods in a shipment, is the document that describes the physical consignment. Where the commercial invoice describes the sale, the packing list describes the boxes.

A packing list for a consignment of 500 cartons shows how the goods are packed: how many cartons there are, how many units each carton contains, the gross and net weights, and where relevant the dimensions of each carton and the way the cartons are arranged on pallets. It carries no prices. Its purpose is physical, not financial.

Three groups of people rely on it.

1. Warehouse staff
They pick, count and pack against the packing list, and the receiving warehouse at the other end counts incoming cartons against it.

2. Carriers and terminal staff
They use the package counts, weights and dimensions to plan loading, stowage and handling, especially where goods must be kept refrigerated or stacked within limits.

3. Customs authorities
When they examine a consignment, they compare what is physically there against the packing list and the declaration to confirm that the goods match the paperwork.

Because the packing list and the commercial invoice describe the same goods from two angles, the two must agree completely. If the invoice says 500 cartons and the packing list says 550, one of them is wrong, and until the difference is resolved the shipment carries a question mark. How such disagreements arise, and what they cost, is treated later in this lesson.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The bill of lading: the key document of sea transport
BODY:
Sea transport has its own key document. A bill of lading is a maritime transport document issued by or on behalf of a carrier that acknowledges receipt of goods and provides evidence of the contract of carriage; certain forms also function as documents of title. The abbreviation B/L is standard in daily use.

The bill of lading is the classic example of one document serving all three of the roles a transport document can play.

1. Receipt for the goods
When the carrier receives the container or cargo and, in the usual case, loads it on board the vessel, the bill of lading records what was received and its apparent condition. It is the shipper's proof that the goods were handed over.

2. Evidence of the contract of carriage
The document sets out the agreement between the shipper and the carrier: the parties, the vessel and the ports, and the basis on which the goods are carried. If a dispute arises about the carriage, the bill of lading is the evidence of what was agreed.

3. Document of title
This is the function that makes the bill of lading famous. For certain forms of the document, possession of the properly issued original controls who may claim delivery of the goods at destination. That is why the document can be used to support international payment arrangements, and why goods travelling under it can even be sold while still at sea.

No other common transport document combines all three functions. One further point completes the sea picture: sea transport also uses a simpler, non-negotiable document called a sea waybill, which is a receipt and evidence of the contract but not a document of title. When and why traders choose one or the other is a Module 6 topic. For now, hold on to the three functions.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Air waybills and road consignment notes
BODY:
Air and road transport use documents that are simpler in one important respect: they are never documents of title.

The air waybill, abbreviated AWB, is a non-negotiable air transport document that provides evidence of the contract of carriage and acknowledges receipt of cargo for air transportation. It is the air cargo counterpart of the bill of lading in its first two functions: it proves the airline received the cargo, and it evidences the agreement under which the cargo is carried. It also serves as the freight bill and supports customs processing. What it never does is control who may claim the goods. The cargo is delivered to the consignee named on the document, and no original document has to be presented to collect it. Where a freight forwarder consolidates several shippers' goods into one air consignment, the airline issues a master air waybill for the consolidated load and the forwarder issues a house air waybill to each shipper for its own part of it; that structure is a Module 6 detail.

Road transport uses the consignment note, a transport document containing information about goods entrusted to a carrier for delivery. Like the air waybill, it is a receipt for the goods and evidence of the contract of carriage, and it is non-negotiable: it is not a document of title. The note normally accompanies the goods on the journey, so that the driver can show what the vehicle is carrying. In Europe, international road movements use a standardised form of consignment note under the CMR convention. Ghana is not part of that convention, and road movements in Ghana and the wider region travel under consignment notes issued under national and regional practice. The function, however, is the same everywhere: a receipt, a record of the carriage agreement, and a description of the goods travelling with the vehicle.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Certificates that travel with goods
BODY:
Some facts about goods cannot simply be declared by the trader; someone independent must confirm them. Certificates are documents in which an authority or an independent inspector states that a particular fact about the goods is true. Which certificates a shipment needs depends on the goods and on the countries involved. This lesson covers the names and purposes only.

1. Certificate of origin
This certificate states where the goods come from, that is, the country in which they were produced or manufactured. It matters because customs treatment often depends on origin. A non-preferential certificate supports ordinary customs treatment. A preferential certificate evidences that the goods qualify under a specific trade agreement, such as the ECOWAS trade liberalisation scheme or the African Continental Free Trade Area, and supports a claim to reduced or zero duty. The distinction is commercial: a preferential certificate can be worth money to the importer.

2. Phytosanitary certificate
For plants and plant products, the plant-protection authority of the exporting country may certify the phytosanitary condition of the goods, meaning their condition with respect to plant pests and diseases.

3. Health or sanitary certificate
For products of animal origin, such as meat or dairy, a corresponding health certificate may be required to confirm that the goods meet health requirements.

4. Quality or inspection certificates
An independent inspector may certify the quality, quantity or condition of the goods, for example at loading. These are common where buyers purchase large commodity parcels they cannot inspect personally.

Notice the common thread. Each certificate is issued by a party other than the buyer or the seller, which is precisely what makes it credible. A trader asserting that its goods are disease-free proves nothing; the exporting country's authority certifying it does.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Customs declarations
BODY:
The fourth family is the document that speaks to government: the customs declaration. When goods cross a border, the customs authority of the country must be told what the goods are, what they are worth, where they come from and where they are going, so that the authority can apply the correct rules, controls and charges. The declaration is the trader's formal statement of those facts, and it is prepared from the other documents in the file: the description and quantities come from the invoice and the packing list, the departure and arrival details come from the transport document, and any certificates are presented alongside it. It is the clearest example of the document families working together.

In Ghana, the customs declaration for an imported consignment is lodged electronically in ICUMS, the Integrated Customs Management System, which operates as Ghana's single-window platform for trade declarations. In practice the lodging is done by a licensed clearing agent acting for the importer, and every consignment receives a Unique Consignment Reference, abbreviated UCR, which is used to track it through the process. The UCR is worth noticing. It is a real, national-scale example of the shipment reference number introduced earlier in this lesson, doing exactly what shipment references always do: giving every participant one name for one shipment.

Customs declarations are only introduced here. What a declaration contains, how customs examines and clears it, and what happens when something goes wrong are treated in the later modules of this course. The point to carry forward is simpler. The declaration is where all the other documents meet the authorities, and its accuracy depends entirely on theirs.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Delivery notes and proof of delivery
BODY:
The journey is not complete when the goods arrive; it is complete when the arrival is recorded. Two documents do this recording.

The delivery note is a document accompanying goods that identifies the items and quantities being delivered. It travels with the consignment, and it is the first document the receiver reads. The receiving process follows the same pattern everywhere. The receiver counts the packages, checks them against the delivery note, inspects their condition, and then signs. The signature says: what this document describes is what I received. Anything unusual, whether a shortage, damage or a wrong item, is noted on the document at the moment of delivery, while both parties are still present.

The proof of delivery, abbreviated POD, is a document or electronic confirmation showing that a shipment has been delivered or received. In its simplest form it is the signed delivery note itself. In modern operations it may be an electronic record, such as a captured signature, a photograph of the delivered goods or a confirmation in a tracking system. Its role is the same in every form: it is the evidence that the journey ended correctly, and it is what closes the shipment's file.

Proof of delivery also completes the payment loop. Before a buyer pays an invoice, the buyer's people match three records: the order, the delivery record and the invoice. This check is known as the three-way match, and it works only if the delivery records are accurate, which is why the moment of signing matters so much. A signed delivery note and proof of delivery for goods that were never counted is not a record. It is a guess with a signature on it.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Following one export: frozen shrimp from Tema to Amsterdam
BODY:
A seafood processor in Tema sells 500 cartons of frozen shrimp to a distributor in Amsterdam, travelling by sea in one refrigerated container. All figures are illustrative for teaching purposes. Follow the documents, stage by stage.

Step 1: The sale is agreed
The buyer receives a pro forma invoice setting out the proposed sale details and uses it to confirm the terms. The processor then issues the commercial invoice for the actual shipment: the parties, the goods, the quantity, the value and the currency.

Step 2: The goods are prepared
The shrimp are packed into 500 cartons of 10 kilograms net each and loaded into the refrigerated container. The packing list records the carton count, units per carton, weights and dimensions, and warehouse staff pick and count against it.

Step 3: The certificates are obtained
Because shrimp are products of animal origin, a health certificate confirming they meet health requirements is obtained from the competent authority, and a certificate of origin is prepared for the buyer's import formalities.

Step 4: Declaration and handover to the carrier
The processor declares the goods to customs in the required export formalities before loading. The container is delivered to the shipping line at Tema, and the carrier issues the bill of lading on receiving it: the processor's receipt, evidence of the contract of carriage, and the document of title controlling who may claim the container in Amsterdam.

Step 5: The information travels ahead
Copies of the invoice, packing list, certificates and transport document reach the buyer and its agent long before the vessel does. The documents have moved ahead of the goods.

Step 6: Arrival and clearance in Amsterdam
The buyer's agent claims the container with the bill of lading and lodges the import declaration with the Dutch customs authority, presenting the invoice, packing list and certificates. Customs checks that the documents tell one consistent story before release.

Step 7: Final delivery and record
When the distributor receives the shrimp, a delivery note accompanies them and the distributor signs for the 500 cartons, creating the proof of delivery that closes the file.

What does this example demonstrate?
Every stage produced a document, and each proved something specific: the invoice the sale, the packing list the physical contents, the certificates the origin and health status, the bill of lading the handover and the carriage contract, and the delivery records the final receipt. Together they tell one consistent story.

Key lesson
Goods cannot complete an international journey on their own. They travel inside a set of documents assembled in order and kept consistent, each existing because someone, whether carrier, buyer, bank or authority, must be able to trust a fact without seeing the goods.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: An air shipment: spare parts from Singapore to Rotterdam
BODY:
Documents adapt to the mode of transport. A machine manufacturer near Rotterdam has a production line stopped, waiting for a replacement control unit. The unit is in stock at the supplier's warehouse in Singapore. It is small, valuable and urgently needed, so it will travel by air. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: Order and invoice
The manufacturer confirms the order, and the Singapore supplier issues a commercial invoice describing the control unit, its quantity and its value, with the references linking it to the order.

Step 2: Packing
The unit is packed into one wooden crate. The packing list records one crate, the gross weight of 85 kilograms and the dimensions. These details matter particularly in air transport, where weight and space determine the freight.

Step 3: Booking and the air waybill
The supplier's freight forwarder, the intermediary that arranges shipments on behalf of shippers, books space with an airline. When the airline receives the crate, an air waybill is issued naming the manufacturer as consignee. The air waybill acknowledges receipt of the cargo and evidences the contract of carriage, and it is non-negotiable: it is not a document of title.

Step 4: Release on arrival
At the destination airport there is no original document to present and no title to transfer. The airline's agent contacts the named consignee, the manufacturer's clearing agent completes the import formalities, and the cargo is released. Compare this with the ocean shipment in the previous example, where the bill of lading controlled who could claim the container. Air cargo moves to the named consignee because the air waybill says so, and nothing more is needed.

Step 5: Delivery and record
The crate is delivered to the manufacturer's factory, the delivery note is checked and signed, and the proof of delivery completes the file.

What does this example demonstrate?
The document families are the same by every mode, meaning an invoice, a packing list, a transport document and delivery records, but the transport document changes character with the mode. Sea transport can use a document of title; air transport cannot.

Key lesson
When the mode changes, ask what the transport document can and cannot do. The air waybill is fast and simple precisely because it is not a document of title, and a seller consigning air cargo has no paper to control who collects it, which is why such shipments suit trading relationships built on trust.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A domestic delivery and a five-carton shortage
BODY:
Documentation discipline matters just as much on a domestic delivery inside Ghana. Consider a distributor that supplies cooking oil from its Tema warehouse to supermarkets in Accra. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: Dispatch
A supermarket branch orders 240 cartons of cooking oil. The distributor's warehouse picks and loads 240 cartons onto a truck and prepares a delivery note identifying the items and the quantity of 240 cartons. The documents travel with the driver.

Step 2: Arrival and check
At the branch, the receiving staff do not sign on sight. They count the cartons as they are unloaded, and they count 235. They inspect the condition of the cartons as well. The delivery note says 240; the truck is holding 235.

Step 3: Recording the discrepancy
The staff record the actual count of 235 and note the shortage of 5 cartons on the delivery documents before signing. The driver countersigns, so both parties leave with the same honest record: 235 received, 5 short. The proof of delivery therefore shows what was really received, not what was planned.

Step 4: Investigation
The distributor now investigates with evidence in hand. Was a carton left at the warehouse? Did the loading count slip? Was a carton damaged and removed along the way? The warehouse dispatch records, the truck's load and the delivery note can be compared precisely because the delivery note records the truth.

Step 5: The payment follows the record
The commercial invoice will eventually claim payment for 240 cartons, but the supermarket's records will not agree with it: the order says 240, the delivery record says 235 and the invoice says 240. This three-way match is exactly the check that prevents the supermarket from paying for cartons it never received until the difference is resolved.

What does this example demonstrate?
The documents did their job because they were allowed to record reality. A shortage discovered and recorded at delivery is a small, claimable and investigable event. The same shortage discovered weeks later, or covered by an inaccurate signature, becomes an argument with no evidence.

Key lesson
Sign for what you counted, not for what the paper says. The delivery note and the proof of delivery protect both sides only when they record the truth at the moment of handover.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: One shipment, one story: documents must agree
BODY:
The single most important quality principle in logistics documentation is consistency. Every document in a shipment's file is a retelling of the same story, and the details must agree from document to document: the quantities on the invoice, the packing list and the declaration; the weights on the packing list and the transport document; the descriptions everywhere; the parties and their names and addresses; and the references linking order to invoice to shipment.

A document discrepancy is an inconsistency, omission, or error identified within a document or between related documents. Discrepancies are common, and they are expensive. A quantity that disagrees stops a customs officer from verifying a declaration. A misspelled consignee name can leave cargo standing at a destination because nobody can match the paperwork. A missing date or signature can invalidate a document that a bank or an authority is being asked to rely on. In international payment arrangements, where banks pay against documents rather than goods, a single discrepancy can pause payment until it is corrected.

The basic errors to watch for are these: quantities that differ between documents; weights that do not match the packages; vague or incomplete descriptions of the goods; the wrong consignee or delivery address; missing dates, signatures or stamps; and references that do not link the documents to each other.

None of these errors is difficult to prevent, which is exactly the point. They are prevention problems, not accidents, and careful checking of one document against another before dispatch catches most of them.

Module 6 treats this subject properly: how documents are examined, how discrepancies are managed and corrected, and which consistency checks professionals run. For now, adopt the habit this lesson recommends: check documents against each other, never against memory.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Never issue or sign a document with unverified details
BODY:
Every document in this lesson makes claims about the real world: that 500 cartons exist, that goods came from a particular country, that 240 cartons were delivered. The entire system runs on those claims being true, which is why the professional rule is absolute. Issue a document only when its details have been verified, and sign only for what you have personally counted or checked.

Your signature on a document is a statement of fact. When you sign a delivery note for 240 cartons without counting, you are certifying a receipt you did not verify. When a document is issued describing goods, values or origins that nobody checked, every downstream participant, whether carrier, bank or customs authority, builds its decisions on an unverified claim.

Integrity goes further than accuracy. Deliberately misstating a value, an origin, a quantity or a description on a document is misdeclaration. It can be done to reduce charges, to evade a restriction or to disguise a shortage, and wherever it is done it puts the trader at serious risk: goods can be held or seized, penalties can follow, and the trust that took years to build disappears. A document with false details is worse than no document at all, because it becomes evidence against the very people who created it.

The practical habits are simple. Check documents against the physical goods, not against assumption. Correct errors openly, on the record, so both parties see the correction. Report discrepancies promptly instead of hoping they will be forgotten. Accuracy and integrity are what make documents trustworthy, and trustworthy documents are what trade runs on.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A shipping line at Tema will not accept a 20-foot container of goods for loading until the exporter's booking is confirmed and the required documents are in order, even though the container is already standing at the terminal.

Which option best explains why logistics operations depend on documents in this way?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Because documents exist mainly to satisfy banks, and a shipment without them can only proceed when payment has already been made in advance.
2. Because documents carry the information ahead of and with the goods, serving as instructions, receipts, evidence for claiming goods, declarations to authorities and records supporting payment, so no participant can act correctly without them.
3. Because documents are needed only when goods cross a border, and domestic movements can legally proceed without any records at all.
4. Because documents allow the carrier to inspect the physical contents of every sealed container before it is loaded onto the vessel.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Documents carry the information that logistics runs on. The lesson opened with the principle that goods move physically, but information must move ahead of and with them, and it identified five purposes that documents serve: instructions and contracts, receipts, evidence of the right to claim goods, declarations to authorities, and records supporting payment. A container standing at a terminal without its documents cannot be loaded, because the carrier has no verified instruction, no record of what it is receiving, and nothing to show the authorities.

Answer 1 narrows documents to banking, but payment is only one of the five purposes, and shipments proceed every day without any bank involvement. Answer 3 is wrong because documentation is just as essential to domestic movement: the delivery note, the transport document and the proof of delivery in this lesson served a purely domestic journey from Tema to Accra. Answer 4 confuses documents with inspection: a document describes the goods, but the carrier does not open a sealed container to read what is inside.

The takeaway: goods never travel alone. Every participant in a logistics operation acts on documents, so the documents must exist, must travel with or ahead of the goods, and must be accurate.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
During a training session, a new employee says that an air waybill works exactly like an ocean bill of lading, because whoever holds the original document can claim the cargo at the destination airport.

Which statement best corrects this?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. An air waybill is a negotiable document of title, but airlines keep the original instead of issuing it to the shipper.
2. An air waybill is only a quotation for the freight charges, and the real transport document is issued when the cargo arrives.
3. An air waybill acknowledges receipt and evidences the contract of carriage, but it is always non-negotiable and is not a document of title, so the cargo is delivered to the consignee named on it.
4. An air waybill transfers ownership of the goods to the airline until the freight charges have been paid in full.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

Both documents share their first two functions: each acknowledges receipt of the cargo and provides evidence of the contract of carriage. The difference lies in the third function. Certain forms of the ocean bill of lading function as documents of title, so possession of the properly issued original controls who may claim the goods at destination. The air waybill is always non-negotiable: it is never a document of title, and the cargo is delivered to the consignee named on it without any original being presented. That is exactly the point the new employee has misunderstood.

Answer 1 inverts the truth: negotiability is the bill of lading's feature, not the air waybill's. Answer 2 mistakes the air waybill for a quotation; the quotation-like member of the document families is the pro forma invoice, while the air waybill is a full transport document issued on receipt of the cargo. Answer 4 invents a transfer of ownership to the airline, which no transport document performs.

The takeaway: receipt and evidence of contract belong to every transport document, but the document-of-title function belongs to the ocean bill of lading alone.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A supermarket in Accra receives a delivery of cooking oil. The delivery note states 240 cartons, but the staff count 235 cartons before the driver leaves the premises.

Which action best describes correct professional practice?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Sign for the 240 cartons on the delivery note, since the document reflects what left the warehouse and the driver's paperwork must remain unchanged.
2. Record the actual count of 235 cartons and the shortage on the delivery documents before signing, so the proof of delivery shows what was really received and the discrepancy can be investigated.
3. Refuse the entire delivery and send all 240 cartons back, because a shortage makes the whole consignment unacceptable.
4. Sign for 235 cartons and quietly write 235 on the driver's copy after the driver has left, so the paperwork matches without any disagreement.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The delivery note and the proof of delivery exist to record what was actually delivered, so a shortage must be recorded on the document before signing, and the discrepancy must be reported so that both parties can investigate against evidence. Signing for 240 cartons when 235 were counted would make the receiver's own signature certify a receipt that never happened, and the parties would lose the very record the distributor needs to investigate and the supermarket may need to claim.

Answer 1 treats the document as more authoritative than the reality it is supposed to record, which defeats its purpose. Answer 3 overreacts: a shortage of five cartons, once recorded, is a claimable and investigable event, not a reason to reject the whole delivery. Answer 4 describes altering paperwork quietly after the driver has gone, which destroys the shared evidence both parties need and undermines the integrity of the record, even when the corrected figure happens to be right.

The takeaway: documents must reflect verified reality. Count, record honestly, sign, and report the discrepancy, because the proof of delivery is the evidence both sides will rely on.

- The bill of lading's three classic functions: receipt for the goods, evidence of the contract of carriage, and document of title; possession of a duly endorsed bill controls the right to delivery, making it valuable for payment arrangements and for selling goods in transit. — source: research sheet trade-transport-standards.md, section 4 (ocean bill of lading).
- The sea waybill is the non-negotiable alternative to the bill of lading: a receipt and evidence of contract but not a document of title. — source: research sheet trade-transport-standards.md, section 4.
- The air waybill is always non-negotiable: receipt, evidence of the contract of carriage, freight bill and customs document for air cargo, but not a document of title. — source: research sheet trade-transport-standards.md, section 4.
- The master air waybill is issued by the airline and the house air waybill by the freight forwarder for its part of a consolidated load. — source: research sheet trade-transport-standards.md, section 4 (master/house air waybills).
- The road consignment note is non-negotiable (a receipt and evidence of the contract of carriage, not a document of title) and accompanies the goods; Europe standardises international road carriage and its consignment note under the CMR convention, Ghana is not a CMR contracting party, and West African road movements use consignment notes under national and regional practice. — source: research sheet trade-transport-standards.md, section 4 (road and rail) and DO NOT CLAIM item 4.
- Certificate of origin: non-preferential certificates support ordinary (MFN) customs treatment; preferential certificates evidence qualification under a trade agreement and support claims to reduced or zero duty, for example under the ECOWAS Trade Liberalisation Scheme (ETLS) and AfCFTA certificates of origin. — source: research sheet trade-transport-standards.md, sections 5 and 12 (rules of origin).
- A phytosanitary certificate covers plants and plant products and is issued by the plant-protection authority of the exporting country; a sanitary or health certificate is the counterpart for products of animal origin; quality or inspection certificates are issued by independent inspectors attesting quality, quantity or condition at loading, with contractual (not standardised) contents. — source: research sheet trade-transport-standards.md, section 5 (trade certificates).
- Customs authorities assess duties and taxes on the basis of the declared value of the goods, declared on the commercial invoice. — source: research sheet trade-transport-standards.md, section 12 (WTO valuation framework, transaction value), taught generically without rates.
- In Ghana, the customs declaration for an imported consignment is lodged electronically in ICUMS (the Integrated Customs Management System), Ghana's single-window platform for trade declarations, normally by a licensed clearing agent acting for the importer, and every consignment receives a Unique Consignment Reference (UCR) for tracking. — source: research sheet ghana-trade-customs.md, sections 1 and 6 (ICUMS; clearing agents) and its SAFE PHRASINGS; no export-specific ICUMS procedure is claimed, and the worked example keeps export formalities generic.
- The three-way match: the purchase order, the goods receipt/delivery record and the supplier invoice are matched before payment is released. — source: research sheet logistics-scm-frameworks.md, section 3 (procurement and the order-to-receipt flow).
- A freight forwarder is an intermediary arranging international shipment for shippers, booking carriage, consolidating cargo and preparing documentation. — source: research sheet logistics-scm-frameworks.md, section 1 (freight forwarder).
- Refrigerated (reefer) containers are insulated temperature-controlled equipment used for perishables, suitable for frozen shrimp. — source: research sheet trade-transport-standards.md, section 6 (containers, special types).
- Receiving practice: receivers count and inspect goods against the delivery note before signing, record shortages or damage on the document at the time of receipt, and the proof of delivery (paper or electronic) evidences delivery; in modern operations electronic POD may capture signatures, photographs or system confirmations. — source: general business knowledge (operational practice), consistent with the lesson brief's definitions of delivery note and proof of delivery.
- The pro forma invoice is an advance, quotation-form invoice issued before dispatch, used to confirm terms, arrange payment or apply for permits, and is not a demand for payment. — source: general business knowledge.
- Shipper and consignee as the standard terms for the sender and receiver in international trade documents. — source: general business knowledge (standard trade usage).
- All quantities and weights in the worked examples (500 cartons of 10 kilograms, 85 kilograms, 240 and 235 cartons) are illustrative for teaching purposes. — source: drafting instruction, WRITING_SPEC.md rule 5.1.
