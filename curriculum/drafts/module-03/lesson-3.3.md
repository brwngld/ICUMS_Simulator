=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: What cargo is and how it is classified
BODY:
Walk through any market, warehouse, or construction site in Ghana and you are surrounded by cargo waiting to move: bags of cement at a distributor, drums of shea butter ready for export, sacks of cocoa at a buying centre. In logistics, all of these are cargo. Cargo means goods transported by ship, aircraft, train, truck, or another means of transportation. The previous lesson introduced the transport modes that move goods; this lesson looks at the goods themselves, because the nature of the cargo decides almost everything about how it must be packaged, handled, stored, and carried.

Cargo is classified so that everyone in the chain can make sensible operational decisions. The classification is practical, not academic: it determines what equipment lifts the goods, what packaging protects them, what documents describe them, and what the movement costs. The main classification questions used in this lesson are the following.

1. How does the cargo travel? Goods that move as individual packages or units are general cargo; goods that move unpackaged in very large quantities are bulk cargo.

2. Is the cargo packed into a container? Goods packed inside standardised freight containers are containerised cargo, whatever the goods themselves may be.

3. Does the cargo have special characteristics? Some goods deteriorate quickly (perishable cargo), some break easily (fragile cargo), and some present hazards (dangerous goods).

4. Is the cargo unusually large or heavy? Exceptional items belong to project or oversized cargo.

The same goods can fall into different classifications depending on how they are packed and presented for transport, a point this lesson returns to more than once. What never changes is the principle: identify the cargo type first, and the packaging, handling, storage, and transport decisions follow from it.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: General and breakbulk cargo
BODY:
General cargo means goods transported as individual packages, units, or consignments rather than as unpackaged bulk commodities. It is the category most people picture first, because most goods in everyday trade travel this way: cartons, sacks, drums, crates, bales, and pieces of equipment handled one unit at a time.

General cargo has three practical features.

1. It moves in countable units. A consignment of 500 cartons can be counted, marked, and checked piece by piece, making receipt and delivery verification straightforward.

2. Each unit carries its own protection and identification. The carton, sack, drum, or crate is itself the packaging that protects the goods, and each package can carry marks and labels, subjects this lesson takes up shortly.

3. It needs more handling than bulk. Every unit must be lifted, moved, stacked, and unstacked individually or on pallets, so handling effort is greater than for loose commodities.

Cement illustrates the familiar Ghanaian case: it leaves the factory in bags and moves to distributors and construction sites as bagged general cargo, counted sack by sack, even though the product inside is a powder that could in principle be moved loose.

The term breakbulk cargo is closely related. Breakbulk means cargo transported as separate packages or units rather than in containers or as unpackaged bulk. Historically, breakbulk described goods loaded piece by piece into the general holds of conventional ships; the term is still used for separate-piece cargo where the pieces are too large or awkward for containers, or where the ports involved are not fully containerised. Breakbulk is therefore part of the general cargo family: all breakbulk is general cargo in separate units, though modern general cargo is very often palletised or containerised as well.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Bulk cargo: dry and liquid
BODY:
Bulk cargo means large quantities of unpackaged commodities transported directly in a vessel's hold, tank, or other suitable transport compartment. Nothing separates the goods from the carrying compartment: the cargo is poured, pumped, or dropped straight in. Bulk cargo divides into two families.

1. Dry bulk. Free-flowing solid commodities poured into holds or silos: grains, ores, sand, gravel, fertiliser, sugar, and clinker, the raw material ground into cement. Specialised vessels with large open holds carry dry bulk, and silos and sheds store it ashore. Handling uses grabs, conveyor belts, and loaders rather than forklifts, because there are no packages to pick up.

2. Liquid bulk. Unpackaged liquids pumped directly into tanks: crude oil, refined fuels, chemicals, and vegetable oils. Tankers carry liquid bulk by sea, pipelines and hoses move it ashore, and storage tanks hold it until it is needed. There is no packaging in the ordinary sense; the tank is the compartment.

Ghana's trade includes both families, and one national example deserves attention: cocoa beans, one of Ghana's best-known agricultural exports, move toward the ports in enormous volumes. When the beans travel loose in large quantities they are handled as a dry agricultural bulk commodity; when the same beans are packed in jute sacks, each sack becomes a unit of general cargo. The product has not changed, only the form in which it travels, and the classification changes with it.

Bulk handling is efficient because it removes packaging: vast quantities move quickly through the right terminals, but the goods are exposed, so quality depends on clean compartments, dry storage, and careful handling at every transfer point.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Containerised cargo and the standard container
BODY:
Containerised cargo means goods packed and transported inside standardised freight containers. The freight container is a standard steel box, strong enough to be lifted and stacked, sized the same way in every country, and transferable between ship, truck, and rail without handling the goods inside; it is what makes the multimodal movement introduced in the previous lesson practical.

Containers are built to international standards that fix their dimensions and strength. The commonly quoted figures for the two most common dry containers follow.

The twenty-foot container measures approximately 5.9 metres long, 2.35 metres wide, and 2.39 metres high inside, giving roughly 33 cubic metres of space. It may be loaded to a maximum gross mass of 30,480 kilograms, the combined weight of the empty box and its contents, of which approximately 28,250 kilograms is available as payload for cargo.

The forty-foot container measures approximately 12 metres long with the same width and internal height, giving roughly 67.5 cubic metres, yet its payload, approximately 26,700 kilograms, is actually lower than a twenty-foot box. A useful rule follows: heavy, dense cargo goes in twenty-foot containers, while light, bulky cargo suits forty-foot boxes. The high-cube forty-foot container adds internal height, approximately 2.7 metres, for cargo that stacks high but weighs little.

Three weight terms describe any container. Tare is the weight of the empty container. Payload is the maximum weight of cargo it may carry. Maximum gross is the permitted total of the two.

Capacity is counted in standard units: the TEU, twenty-foot equivalent unit, counts one twenty-foot container, and the FEU, forty-foot equivalent unit, counts one forty-foot container. One FEU equals two TEU, and a high cube still counts as one FEU.

Beyond the dry box, standard container types answer special needs: the refrigerated container, or reefer, carries perishables under temperature control; the open top, with a removable tarpaulin roof, is loaded from above for tall or long cargo; the flat rack, with no sides or roof, carries machinery and other out-of-gauge items; and the tank container, a tank built into a twenty-foot frame, carries liquids, gases, and powders, often dangerous goods.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Cargo that needs special care: perishable and fragile goods
BODY:
Some cargo survives an ordinary journey without complaint; some does not. Two categories in particular impose conditions on packaging, handling, and storage.

Perishable cargo means goods that may deteriorate or spoil unless transported and stored under suitable conditions. Frozen tuna landed for processing and export, fresh fruit and vegetables, dairy products, cut flowers, and many medicines are perishable. What they need is the cold chain: an unbroken sequence of temperature-controlled storage and transport, from the chilled store at origin, through the refrigerated container or temperature-controlled truck, to the cold room at destination, with the temperature monitored throughout. The chain is only as strong as its weakest point; a few hours at the wrong temperature can spoil a whole consignment. Perishables therefore demand refrigerated equipment, prompt handling, and temperature records as part of the shipment information.

Fragile cargo means goods susceptible to damage from impact, vibration, pressure, or improper handling. Glassware, ceramics, mirrors, electronic devices, precision instruments, and tiles are typical examples. Fragile goods rarely need special transport equipment; they need protection engineered into the packaging and care built into the handling. Inner cushioning absorbs shocks, outer cartons resist compression, packages are marked so every handler knows to treat them gently, and stacks are kept to heights the bottom packages can bear.

Both categories teach the same lesson in different ways: the cargo's characteristics, not the carrier's convenience, set the conditions of carriage. A forwarder who accepts frozen tuna must be able to guarantee refrigeration from pickup to delivery; a warehouse storing ceramics must control how high the pallets are stacked. Storage conditions, including temperature, humidity, ventilation, and stacking limits, belong in the planning conversation too. Identifying special-handling cargo early, while the shipment is still being planned, prevents damage no paperwork can repair afterwards.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Dangerous goods and the nine hazard classes
BODY:
Dangerous goods are substances or articles that present hazards during transportation and are subject to applicable safety regulations. The category is broader than beginners expect, covering ordinary products: aerosol cans, lighter fuel, paints and thinners, bleach, fireworks, pool chemicals, and the lithium batteries inside phones and power banks. What makes an item a dangerous good is not that it is unusual, but that it presents a hazard in transport and is therefore regulated.

Internationally, dangerous goods are divided into nine classes, worth learning in outline.

1. Explosives.

2. Gases, including flammable, non-flammable non-toxic, and toxic gases. Aerosols travel in this class.

3. Flammable liquids, such as petrol, paints, and many solvents.

4. Flammable solids, which also covers materials liable to spontaneous combustion and materials that emit flammable gas in contact with water.

5. Oxidising substances and organic peroxides, which feed fires rather than burn alone.

6. Toxic and infectious substances.

7. Radioactive material.

8. Corrosive substances, which destroy skin, metal, and other materials.

9. Miscellaneous dangerous substances and articles, a class that includes lithium batteries, dry ice, and environmentally hazardous substances.

Each regulated substance or article is identified by a UN number, a four-digit code used worldwide, together with its proper shipping name; aerosols, for example, travel under UN 1950, and lithium-ion batteries under UN 3480. For packing purposes, most classes are additionally graded into packing groups by degree of danger: packing group I for great danger, II for medium danger, and III for minor danger. Classes 1, 2, and 7 have no packing groups.

Two cautions frame everything else. First, dangerous goods are accepted for transport only under strict conditions of declaration, packaging, marking, and segregation, which the important note near the end of this lesson explains. Second, the full treatment, including the modal rulebooks and their documentation, belongs to Module 11 of this programme; here the aim is to recognise the classes and respect them.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Project and oversized cargo
BODY:
Not everything fits in a box. Project cargo is the collective name for items that are individually so large, heavy, or awkward that they cannot travel as ordinary units or in standard containers: electrical transformers, industrial generators, mining and road-building plant, prefabricated structures, boats, and factory machinery. A piece that exceeds standard container dimensions is called out-of-gauge cargo, and each such movement is planned almost as an individual engineering operation.

Standard container types give some help. Flat rack containers, with no sides or roof, accept machinery and vehicles that would never pass through a dry box's doors; open top containers, with a removable tarpaulin roof, take tall or long cargo that must be lowered in from above by crane. Beyond container limits, cargo travels on specialised trailers, cradles, and platforms, and ports lift it with heavy cranes.

What distinguishes project cargo from ordinary freight is the depth of planning. A single oversized movement may require checking the lifting capacity at each transfer point, confirming that the route can carry the weight and dimensions, obtaining any special permissions, and agreeing schedules with everyone involved, because a transformer that cannot pass under a bridge or be lifted by the available crane stops the project. The essential points are these: oversized cargo is identified early, planned individually, moved with specialised equipment, and marked so that everyone along the route knows its weight, balance, and lifting points. Ghana sees such movements regularly, when heavy construction, mining, and energy equipment arrives through the ports for projects inland.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Packaging: levels, functions, and suiting the journey
BODY:
Packaging means the materials and methods used to contain, protect, preserve, and prepare goods for handling, storage, and transportation. Packaging works in levels, easiest to see on an everyday product.

1. Unit or primary packaging. The bottle, jar, wrapper, or sack that holds the product itself: the jar of shea butter the customer buys. It is designed for the shopper, not mainly for the warehouse.

2. Inner packaging. The material inside the outer box that cushions and separates: cardboard dividers between jars, polythene liners that keep moisture out, foam inserts for electronics. Inner packaging protects items from one another and from the shocks of the journey.

3. Outer or transport packaging. The carton, sack, drum, crate, or pallet that handlers actually lift and stack. Transport packaging is designed for the logistics chain, and it carries the marks and labels the chain reads.

Whatever the level, packaging performs four functions.

1. Protect. Against mechanical shocks and vibration, crushing and stacking loads, moisture and humidity, contamination, and, where relevant, temperature extremes.

2. Unitise. Packaging gathers many small items into one handleable unit: twelve jars into one carton, twenty cartons onto one pallet, a thousand cartons into one container. Unitisation is what makes modern handling speeds possible.

3. Identify. The package surface carries the marks, numbers, and labels that tell the chain what the package is, where it goes, and how to treat it.

4. Comply. Some packaging is required by regulation rather than choice: dangerous goods need packaging certified to performance standards, and food products need hygienic packing.

Finally, packaging must suit the journey: a carton adequate for a short, dry air trip may fail on an ocean voyage of weeks through humid, salt-laden air, stacked beneath other cargo and lifted repeatedly between truck, warehouse, and ship. Matching packaging to the mode and to every handling step along the chain is a core logistics decision, not an afterthought.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Common packaging forms and palletisation
BODY:
Across warehouses and ports the same packaging forms appear again and again, each suited to particular goods.

1. Cartons. Corrugated boxes, the standard transport packaging for consumer goods, processed foods, and dried products. Cartons are light, printable, and stackable, and they are almost always unitised on pallets for movement in quantity.

2. Sacks. Bags of woven plastic, paper, or jute for free-flowing goods: cement, rice, sugar, fertiliser, and cocoa beans. Sacks are cheap and space-efficient but vulnerable to snagging, moisture, and overstacking, which is why their stacking height and covers matter so much.

3. Crates. Wooden or plastic framed boxes for heavy, valuable, or fragile items: machinery, tiles, glassware, and equipment. A crate can be engineered for the exact piece it carries and marked with lifting points and orientation arrows where needed.

4. Drums. Cylindrical containers of steel or plastic, commonly holding liquids: shea butter, oils, chemicals, and food ingredients. Drums seal tightly, stack reliably, and can be palletised for mechanical handling.

5. Big bags. Large woven sacks, often with lifting loops, each holding several hundred kilograms up to roughly a tonne of free-flowing goods such as granules, nuts, or powders. Big bags combine bulk economy with unit handling: a forklift lifts one pair of loops and moves what would otherwise be dozens of small sacks.

Unitising cartons, sacks, and drums leads to palletisation, the arrangement and securing of goods on pallets to facilitate handling, storage, and transportation. A pallet is a flat portable platform, usually wooden or plastic, that a forklift can slide beneath. Goods are stacked on it in a planned pattern, then secured with stretch wrap or strapping so the whole load behaves as one stable unit. The benefits are considerable: a forklift moves hundreds of cartons in seconds, packages are handled less often and therefore damaged less, stacks are more stable, and counting is quicker. The cost is the pallet itself and the space it occupies, a trade-off shippers accept worldwide.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Cargo handling: principles and equipment
BODY:
Cargo handling means the activities involved in loading, unloading, lifting, moving, stacking, and securing goods. Every package is handled many times between sender and receiver, and each handling is a chance to do the job well or badly. Good handling follows a few principles that apply from a village loading bay to a modern container terminal.

1. Respect the package's limits. Stacking limits, whether printed on the packaging or agreed for the product, exist because bottom layers fail when loads are excessive.

2. Observe orientation. Packages marked this side up must stay this side up; an inverted drum of liquid or carton of instruments can leak or break without a single knock.

3. Handle fragile cargo as fragile. No dropping, throwing, or dragging; cushioning stays in place; and fragile units are never used as a base for other cargo.

4. Keep loads balanced. When lifting, loading a truck, or stuffing a container, the centre of gravity, the point where the load's weight is considered to act, should stay low and near the middle. Unbalanced loads topple, stress equipment, and make transport dangerous.

5. Segregate incompatible goods. Some cargoes must never travel or sit together: dangerous goods away from foodstuffs, oxidising materials away from flammables, wet goods away from dry. Segregation is a planned decision made before loading, not an accident of the loading order.

6. Protect from the elements. Covers, dry floors, and sheltered storage keep rain, humidity, and sun from doing quiet damage between the handling steps.

Handling equipment matches the packaging: forklifts and pallet trucks need pallets or firm bases, port cranes lift containers and heavy pieces, conveyors move loose parcels, and container handlers stack boxes in yards. Where none of these reach, manual handling does, which is why safe lifting technique and clear floor discipline remain part of the basics. The planner's task is to match equipment to cargo: a drum needs a clamp, a big bag needs loop hooks, and a fragile crate needs steady hands.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Cargo marking: the information on the package
BODY:
Cargo marking means the application of identification details, handling instructions, or other relevant information to cargo packages. Marking turns an anonymous box into an addressed, instructable unit that anyone in the chain can handle correctly without opening it or reading the sales contract. Marks fall into three layers.

1. Identification marks. Who the package belongs to and where it goes: the consignee's mark or code, the destination port or city, the country of origin, the package number within the consignment, such as carton 137 of 1,000, and often the gross weight. On palletised and containerised cargo these details also appear on the shipping documents, a connection developed below and in the next lesson.

2. Handling instructions. Standard pictorial symbols and words that tell every handler how to treat the package: this way up, fragile, keep dry, do not stack, centre of gravity, stacking limit. Pictograms matter because a package may be handled by people who share no common language along its route; the picture of a wine glass or an umbrella needs no translation.

3. Regulatory labels. Labels required by regulation, above all the hazard labels and UN number markings for dangerous goods, which tell carriers, terminals, and emergency services exactly what hazard a package presents.

Good marking practice passes three tests. It is accurate, agreeing with the documents and the actual contents. It is durable, able to survive rain, abrasion, and handling without becoming unreadable. And it is honest: marks that mislead, whether by accident or design, put handlers and cargo at risk and create disputes months later.

Marking is best understood as the information layer of packaging: packaging protects the goods physically, marking protects them operationally, because a package that cannot be identified, oriented, or risk-assessed from its marks will sooner or later be handled wrongly.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Packing a containerised export of shea butter products
BODY:
A cosmetics producer in Accra has concluded a sale of shea butter products to a buyer in Rotterdam and must pack 1,000 cartons for ocean shipment through Tema. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: The product and its packaging levels. Each carton holds 12 jars. The jar is the primary packaging, designed for the customer; a cardboard divider set inside each carton is the inner packaging, keeping the jars from touching; the carton itself is the transport packaging. A packed carton measures roughly 0.4 by 0.3 by 0.25 metres, about 0.03 cubic metres, and weighs about 12 kilograms.

Step 2: Palletisation. Staff stack 20 cartons on each wooden pallet in a block pattern and secure the load with stretch wrap. The 1,000 cartons fill 50 pallets, each now a single stable unit a forklift can move in seconds.

Step 3: Choosing the container. The planner compares the cargo against container capacity. Volume: 1,000 cartons at about 0.03 cubic metres come to about 30 cubic metres before the pallets beneath them are counted, which already fills the roughly 33 cubic metres inside a twenty-foot container, and fifty pallets cannot be fitted into one. Weight: 1,000 cartons at 12 kilograms give 12,000 kilograms, comfortably below a forty-foot container's payload of approximately 26,700 kilograms. The cargo is light for its volume, so a forty-foot box is right; the pallets travel in it double-stacked with room to spare, while a twenty-foot container would accept only part of the load.

Step 4: The marking scheme. Before wrapping and loading, staff mark every carton alike: the consignee's code and destination, RTM-256, ROTTERDAM; the origin, PRODUCT OF GHANA; the package number, from carton 1 of 1,000 through carton 1,000 of 1,000; and the handling pictograms, this way up and keep dry, because both contents and cardboard suffer from moisture and inversion.

Step 5: Loading and handover. The 50 pallets are stuffed into the container in a planned pattern, the load is secured, and the container is sealed. The container and seal numbers, the package count, and the marks are recorded for the shipping documents that will accompany the cargo.

What does this example demonstrate? Packaging worked in levels, from jar to divider to carton to pallet to container, each level adding protection and unitisation. The container choice followed from arithmetic against the container's known capacity. The marking scheme prepared the cargo to travel: any handler between Accra and Rotterdam can identify, orient, and deliver each carton without further explanation.

Key lesson: pack from the product outward, unitise for handling, match the container to the cargo's volume and weight, and mark the packages so that any handler, anywhere, can handle them correctly.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A handling failure: bagged cocoa damaged on the road to Tema
BODY:
Not every movement goes well, and failures teach more than successes. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

An exporter in Kumasi has sold 1,200 jute sacks of cocoa beans to a European buyer, for shipment through Tema Port. Each sack holds roughly 60 kilograms, so the consignment weighs about 72 tonnes and needs three truckloads for the journey south.

Step 1: The loading. To save time, the loading crew piles the sacks directly onto three open truck beds without pallets or dunnage, stacks them high to fit each load on one deck, and sets off without waterproof covers.

Step 2: What went wrong. Three failures compound one another. First, the stacks are too high: on the rough road the truck's flexing drives the top sacks' weight into the bottom rows, and by Tema the lowest sacks have burst, spilling beans that are swept up and rebagged in whatever material is at hand. Second, the sacks sit directly on the bare bed, so road spray and a midday rainstorm soak the lower layers; wet jute stains, and damp cocoa is at risk of mould, which the buyer will reject. Third, nothing separates the sacks from the bed, the straps, or one another, so abrasion opens seams along the stack.

Step 3: The consequences. At the port, the receiver's inspection notes burst and stained sacks, wet layers, and rebagged loose beans. The buyer raises a claim: part of the quantity is lost outright, more is discounted for suspected quality damage, and the exporter's recovery against the carrier is weakened because the way the goods were packed and loaded contributed to the loss. A loading job that saved an hour has cost a margin and a customer's confidence.

Step 4: What should have happened. The sacks should have travelled on pallets or at least on clean dunnage, stacked to a height the bottom sacks could bear, covered with waterproof sheeting for the whole journey, and repacked properly, not improvised on the dock, wherever damage appeared on the way.

What does this example demonstrate? Improper packaging and handling convert a sound product into a claim: overstacking crushed, moisture spoiled, and abrasion opened, each failure small alone but decisive together.

Lessons from the example: stacking limits, dry separation from the vehicle bed, and weather protection are not formalities; they are the difference between cargo that arrives saleable and cargo that arrives as a dispute.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Dangerous goods under control: a consolidated shipment from Singapore
BODY:
An international example shows how this lesson's rules come together. A freight forwarder in Singapore is consolidating a groupage consignment, meaning several customers' goods combined in one container, for ocean shipment to Rotterdam: 60 cartons of aerosol paints, 40 drums of a solvent-based cleaning fluid, and 200 ordinary cartons of plastic household goods. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: Classification. The forwarder starts from each product's safety data sheet, the standard document in which the manufacturer describes the product's hazards. The aerosols, which hold flammable propellant under pressure, are dangerous goods of Class 2, gases; the cleaning fluid is a flammable liquid, Class 3; the plastic goods are not dangerous goods at all. Each dangerous product is matched to its UN number and proper shipping name, the aerosols under UN 1950, and the flammable liquid is assigned a packing group according to its degree of danger; gases, being a class without packing groups, are not graded in this way.

Step 2: Declaration. The forwarder declares the dangerous goods to the ocean carrier on the dangerous goods declaration, stating the UN numbers, proper shipping names, classes, packing groups, quantities, and packing. The carrier's acceptance staff check the declaration before the goods are accepted; undeclared dangerous goods discovered later can see the shipment refused or offloaded.

Step 3: Packaging, marking, and labelling. The dangerous goods travel in packaging certified to the required performance standards. Each package is marked with its UN number and proper shipping name and bears the hazard label for its class; the container itself displays the corresponding placards so that everyone who sees it knows a flammable cargo is inside.

Step 4: Segregation. In the consolidation plan the forwarder keeps the two classes of dangerous goods apart as the sea rulebook, the IMDG Code, requires, stows both away from the plastic household goods, and confirms nothing incompatible shares the space. Segregation is decided on paper before a single package is loaded.

Step 5: Carriage. The accepted, declared, labelled, and segregated container sails to Rotterdam, where receiving checks find marks and documents in agreement.

What does this example demonstrate? Dangerous goods do not travel by accident. Classification from the safety data sheet, declaration to the carrier, compliant packaging and labels, and planned segregation together make a hazardous consignment safe to carry; remove any one step and the hazard travels with the goods.

Key lesson: the declaration, packaging, and labels of dangerous goods transport are not bureaucracy laid over the cargo; they are the safety system itself.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Why dangerous goods demand special acceptance
BODY:
A dangerous package cannot simply be handed to a truck driver or stacked into a hold like any other carton. The reason is that dangerous goods threaten three things at once: the people who handle them, the vehicle, vessel, or aircraft that carries them, and the cargo stowed nearby. A leaking drum, an overheating battery, or a hidden aerosol crushed in a stack can start a fire, poison a crew, or destroy cargo worth many times the offending package's value. No part of the chain therefore treats dangerous goods as ordinary freight.

Acceptance is the gatekeeper moment. A carrier accepts dangerous goods only after they are properly declared, classified, packaged, marked, and, where required, segregated; for air transport, acceptance is performed only by trained and authorised staff working through detailed checklists. Sea and air each have their own rulebooks, and the declaration states the UN number, proper shipping name, class, packing group, and quantities, so that every later handler knows what is inside.

Two warnings complete the picture. First, dangerous goods hide inside ordinary-looking cargo: lithium batteries sit in countless electronic devices, and dry ice, used to keep samples frozen, is itself a Class 9 dangerous substance. Shippers must identify such items honestly rather than assume careful packing makes them ordinary. Second, misdeclaration is not a paperwork shortcut but a genuine danger: an undeclared hazard travels with no precautions, endangering people and cargo, and when discovered it brings refusal, penalties, and liability for any harm caused.

Module 11 treats dangerous goods in depth. At this stage, remember the principle: dangerous goods earn their special handling because the consequences of treating them casually fall on everyone in the chain.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: The same commodity can travel as different cargo
BODY:
A common beginner error is to treat cargo type as a permanent property of the product, as if cocoa were simply bulk and machinery simply general cargo. In reality, classification follows the form in which the goods actually travel, and the same commodity can legitimately appear in several categories.

Consider the examples this lesson has used. Cement leaves the factory as bagged general cargo, while its raw material, clinker, crosses the oceans as dry bulk. Cocoa beans packed in jute sacks are general cargo, counted sack by sack; the same beans flowing loose through a bulk handling chain are dry bulk. Shea butter in sealed drums is packaged general cargo, drum by drum; the same butter pumped into a heated tank is liquid bulk. Even a carton of tinned tomatoes changes character at the container's doors: as individual cartons the consignment is general cargo; stuffed into a container, it is handled as containerised cargo.

The classification matters because everything operational hangs from it. The form of travel decides the packaging, the handling equipment, the storage arrangement, the documents, the rates, and the risks. Bagged cargo can be counted and split into small deliveries but costs handling at every move; bulk cargo moves cheaply in vast quantities but needs specialised terminals and resists subdivision.

So when planning or describing a shipment, ask not what the product is but how it will travel: in packages or loose, on pallets, in a container, in a hold, in a tank. Answer that, and the cargo type, with all its requirements, follows.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Marks and labels connect cargo to its documents
BODY:
Throughout this lesson, packages have carried marks: consignee codes, package numbers, pictograms, hazard labels. It is tempting to see these as decoration, but that understates their role. Marks and labels are the physical cargo's side of a conversation with the paperwork, and the conversation must agree.

When a consignment is booked and shipped, its identification marks, package counts, weights, container numbers, and seal numbers are recorded on transport documents. At every handover, the receiver checks the cargo against those documents by reading the marks: carton 137 of 1,000 is expected, counted, and signed for because the documents say it exists. Where marks and documents disagree, the result is delay while staff reconcile pieces, misdelivery when a package cannot be attributed to a consignment, or a disputed claim when the receiver cannot prove what was received. In customs processing too, the description and marks of packages must line up with the declaration that describes them.

Handling pictograms and hazard labels carry the same principle further: they put the handling instructions and the safety information where the cargo will be, not merely in an office file. A package whose fragility is recorded only in a document nobody at the loading bay has read will be handled as if it were unbreakable.

The practical discipline is consistency: mark the packages to match the documents, and the documents to match the packages, every time. The next lesson introduces logistics documents properly, and as each one appears, notice how the marks, numbers, and labels this lesson placed on the packages reappear on the paper. Cargo and documents tell one story, or the story falls apart.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A vessel berths at Tema Port carrying three consignments: maize poured loose into the ship's hold, cartons of tinned tomatoes stacked on pallets, and frozen fish packed inside refrigerated containers.

Which statement best describes how these cargoes are classified?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. All three are general cargo because each consignment consists of food carried by sea.
2. All three are bulk cargo because each is carried in large quantities on the same vessel.
3. The loose maize is dry bulk cargo, the palletised cartons are general cargo, and the frozen fish is containerised cargo requiring temperature-controlled handling.
4. The classification depends on the value of each product: the most valuable consignment is general cargo and the rest is bulk.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

Classification follows the form in which each consignment travels, not the product's food use and not the fact that all three share a vessel. The maize is poured loose into the hold with no packaging, which is the definition of dry bulk cargo. The tinned tomatoes travel as individual cartons stacked on pallets, countable units with their own packaging and marks, which makes them general cargo. The frozen fish moves inside refrigerated containers under temperature control, so it is containerised cargo with a perishable, special-handling requirement.

Answer 1 fails because belonging to the same product family does not make cargoes alike; the packaging and form of travel decide the classification. Answer 2 fails because bulk describes unpackaged cargo in direct contact with the hold or tank, which applies only to the maize. Answer 4 fails because price plays no part in classification; a cheap commodity in sacks and a costly one in a hold are still classified by their form of travel.

Takeaway: to classify cargo, look at how it travels, not at what it is.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
An exporter in Accra wants to ship 200 cartons of aerosol air fresheners by sea. A colleague suggests declaring them as ordinary cartons of household goods because they are packed carefully and stacked securely.

Which option best explains why this suggestion is wrong?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Declaring the aerosols as ordinary goods would be acceptable cost management, since the classification of a consignment can be chosen to reduce freight charges.
2. Dangerous goods may never be transported by sea, so the consignment of aerosols cannot be shipped at all.
3. Aerosols are dangerous goods of Class 2, gases, so they must be declared with their UN number and proper shipping name, packed and labelled to the applicable requirements, and accepted by the carrier; careful packing does not remove the hazard.
4. Classification follows the buyer's preference, so the exporter may describe the goods however the customer requests.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

Aerosols contain propellant under pressure and are classified as dangerous goods of Class 2, gases, so they fall under the dangerous goods requirements however carefully they are packed. Transporting them legally requires declaring them to the carrier with their UN number and proper shipping name, packing them in compliant packaging, applying the hazard labels, and obtaining the carrier's acceptance. Careful stacking is not among the requirements, because it does nothing to control the hazard itself.

Answer 1 fails because the classification of dangerous goods is set by regulation for safety reasons, not chosen for cost. Answer 2 fails because dangerous goods are carried by sea every day; the rules govern how they travel, they do not forbid them. Answer 4 fails because classification follows the hazard of the contents, not the parties' preference; misdeclaring the goods would endanger the vessel and crew and expose the exporter to refusal and penalties.

Takeaway: if a product presents a transport hazard, it must enter the dangerous goods system; packing quality never substitutes for declaration.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A company in Accra must send 300 cartons of glassware to a customer in Tamale. The journey involves loading at the warehouse, a long road journey, unloading, and storage in a warehouse where other goods will be stacked nearby.

Which option describes the most suitable basic packaging and handling approach?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Loose cartons loaded directly onto the truck bed to save space, since glassware does not need any special treatment once packed.
2. Cartons with inner cushioning, stacked on a wrapped pallet, marked this way up and fragile, lifted by forklift, and stacked only to a safe height in the warehouse.
3. Each glass wrapped in paper and the cartons then piled as high as the warehouse allows, because stacking height does not affect packaged goods.
4. Plain cartons without marks or pictograms, because handling instructions are useful only for dangerous goods.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Glassware is fragile cargo, so the packaging must absorb the shocks of the road and the handling chain, and the handling must respect the marks. Inner cushioning protects each piece, the palletised and wrapped cartons move as stable units, the this-way-up and fragile marks instruct every handler along the route, and a safe stacking height in the warehouse keeps the bottom cartons within the compression they can bear.

Answer 1 fails because loose cartons on a truck bed take the full vibration of the journey and the full weight of any stacking, exactly the pressures fragile goods cannot resist. Answer 3 fails because packaging does not remove stacking limits; compression crushes the bottom layers of even well-packed cartons when stacks are too high. Answer 4 fails because handling marks exist for fragile, perishable, and orientation-sensitive goods generally, not only for dangerous goods; a glassware carton without its pictograms will be handled like any anonymous box.

Takeaway: suitable packaging, correct marks, and disciplined stacking form the basic formula for moving fragile cargo safely.
