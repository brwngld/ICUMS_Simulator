=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The major modes of transportation
BODY:
The previous lesson followed goods from a supplier to a buyer and showed that a shipment passes through preparation, collection, transportation, handling, storage, and delivery. This lesson examines the transportation stage itself and asks a practical question: in how many ways can goods physically travel?

Logistics operations use five broad arrangements, called modes of transport.

1. Sea freight. The transportation of goods by vessels across seas, oceans, or other navigable waterways.

2. Air freight. The transportation of goods by aircraft.

3. Road freight. The transportation of goods using road vehicles such as trucks and vans.

4. Rail freight. The transportation of goods using trains operating on railway networks.

5. Multimodal transport. Journeys that combine two or more of these modes within one coordinated movement.

Each mode exists because it solves a different problem. A vessel can carry enormous quantities across an ocean, but it cannot deliver to a shop's door. A van can deliver to any address that a road reaches, but it cannot cross an ocean. An aircraft crosses continents in hours, at a cost that only some goods can justify. A train can haul a very heavy load over land efficiently, but only between points that a railway connects.

No single mode is at the same time the fastest, the cheapest, and the most flexible. Every transport decision is therefore a trade-off, and the practical skill is matching the mode to the goods, the distance, and the delivery requirement.

This lesson describes each mode in turn, explains how modes are combined in multimodal and intermodal movements, and closes with a framework for comparing the modes and selecting an appropriate one for a given shipment.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Transit time, freight capacity, and the transport network
BODY:
To compare transport modes objectively, three terms are needed. They appear throughout this lesson and in later modules, so each is defined carefully here.

1. Transit time. The time required for goods to travel between specified points in a transportation journey. Transit time is commonly quoted between the points where the carrying mode takes over and gives up the goods: airport to airport in air freight, port to port in sea freight. The total time a consignment is on the move is longer than the carrying time alone, because it includes loading, handling at terminals, waiting for scheduled departures, and, across borders, customs formalities.

2. Freight capacity. The quantity or weight of cargo that a transport vehicle or service can accommodate. Capacity differs enormously between modes. A delivery van may carry a few tonnes at most. An articulated truck carries many times more. A single large container vessel can carry thousands of containers; an earlier lesson noted that vessel capacity is commonly counted in twenty-foot equivalent units, called TEU. Capacity matters because it decides how much cargo can move in one journey, and moving more cargo in one movement usually lowers the cost per unit.

3. Transport network. A system of routes, facilities, and connections used to move goods or passengers between locations. Each mode has its own network. The road network consists of roads, depots, and delivery points. The rail network consists of railway lines, terminals, and wagons. The sea network consists of ports and shipping routes. The air network consists of airports and flight routes. A mode can serve only the places its network reaches, which is why the shape of the available networks strongly influences transport decisions in every country.

With these three terms in place, the modes can now be described and compared on a common basis.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Sea freight: the largest carrier of international trade
BODY:
Sea freight is the transportation of goods by vessels across seas, oceans, or other navigable waterways. It is the foundation of international trade: the greater part of the goods moving between continents travels by sea, because no other mode can match what a vessel carries in one voyage.

The defining characteristics of sea freight follow from the sheer scale of vessels.

1. Very large freight capacity. A single large container vessel carries thousands of containers, and bulk carriers and tankers move grain, ores, clinker, crude oil, and refined products in quantities measured in many thousands of tonnes.

2. The lowest cost per unit over long distance. Because a vessel spreads its operating cost across enormous cargo quantities, sea freight is normally the cheapest way to move goods between continents, unit by unit.

3. The slowest transit times. Vessels travel far more slowly than aircraft, and a voyage between continents is measured in weeks rather than days. Port handling at each end adds further time.

Sea cargo moves in two broad forms, and the distinction matters when choosing equipment.

1. Containerised cargo. Manufactured goods, packaged foods, and general merchandise are packed into standard steel containers, which container ships carry. The container protects the goods and allows easy transfer between vessel, truck, and train, a point developed later in this lesson.

2. Bulk cargo. Unpackaged homogeneous goods such as grain, ores, and clinker are poured or scooped directly into a vessel's holds and carried by dry bulk carriers. Liquids travel in tankers. Vehicles and wheeled machinery sail on specialised roll-on roll-off vessels.

An earlier lesson introduced shipping lines and their liner services, which run published routes and schedules; that is how most container cargo is booked. The next lesson examines cargo types and packaging in detail.

The main limitations of sea freight are its slow speed, its dependence on ports, and its exposure to congestion and weather along the route. A port is also never the final destination: after discharge, goods still need a road or rail journey inland. Sea freight therefore suits cargo that is heavy, bulky, or plentiful, and that is not urgent.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Air freight: the fastest and most expensive mode
BODY:
Air freight is the transportation of goods by aircraft. It is the fastest mode over long distances: where a vessel needs weeks to cross between continents, an aircraft crosses in hours, and a consignment that flies is typically delivered within a day or two of departure, airport to airport. Speed is air freight's defining advantage and the reason it exists as a freight mode.

Cargo travels in the holds of passenger aircraft and in dedicated freighter aircraft, moving through airport cargo terminals built for quick acceptance, security screening, and release. An earlier lesson named the air waybill, the transport document an airline issues for goods it carries; the airline takes the goods into its care at the origin airport and releases them at the destination airport.

The advantages of air freight follow directly from speed.

1. Shortest transit time. The decisive benefit for cargo where every day matters.

2. Less stock tied up in transit. Goods that arrive quickly start earning or selling sooner, which partly offsets the higher cost for high-value cargo.

3. Predictable schedules. Cargo is booked onto frequent scheduled flights, so the next departure is rarely far away.

The limitations are equally clear.

1. The highest cost of any mode. Air freight is charged on chargeable weight, a billing weight that reflects the space cargo occupies as well as what it weighs. Light but bulky cargo can therefore be charged as though it weighed much more. For most goods, flying costs several times more than sea freight.

2. The smallest freight capacity. An aircraft carries only a fraction of what a vessel carries, and size, weight, and safety restrictions limit what may fly at all.

3. A limited network. Air freight runs between airports equipped to handle cargo, and delivery beyond the airport needs road transport, just as sea freight does beyond the port.

Air freight therefore suits cargo that is urgent, perishable, high in value, or light. Pharmaceuticals, urgent spare parts, electronics, documents, fresh produce, and flowers are typical examples.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Road freight: door-to-door flexibility
BODY:
Road freight is the transportation of goods using road vehicles such as trucks and vans. It is the mode most people see every day, and the only mode that, in normal practice, completes a delivery to an ordinary address. It dominates regional and local distribution and carries the first and last legs of most international journeys, whatever mode crosses the ocean or continent in between.

The defining characteristics of road freight are flexibility and reach.

1. Door-to-door delivery. A truck can collect from a farm, factory, or warehouse and deliver to a shop, a site, or a customer's gate without transhipment. No other mode offers this.

2. A vehicle for every load. Road freight uses everything from small vans to large articulated trucks, so the vehicle can be matched to the size of the consignment.

3. Flexibility in timing and routing. Road transport can depart when it is needed and adjust its route, which fixed networks and published schedules do not allow in the same way.

Road freight also owns the final stage of nearly every supply chain. Last-mile delivery is the final stage of transportation in which goods are delivered from a distribution point to the intended recipient. It is the stage customers actually see, and it is almost always performed by road.

The advantages of road freight are door-to-door service, speed over short and medium distances, and wide availability, since roads reach places that ports, airports, and railway lines do not.

The limitations are equally practical.

1. Limited freight capacity per vehicle. A truck carries a tiny fraction of what a train or vessel carries, so cost per unit rises when large volumes move long distances by road alone.

2. Higher cost per unit over long hauls. Where rail or sea can take over the long part of a journey, they usually do it more cheaply.

3. Exposure to road conditions. Traffic congestion, road quality, weather, and border formalities all affect road transit times.

Road freight therefore suits regional distribution, collection and delivery legs, and any movement that must end at a specific address.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Rail freight: heavy loads over land
BODY:
Rail freight is the transportation of goods using trains operating on railway networks. Its defining strength is efficiency over land: one train, hauled by one or a few locomotives, can move a load that would otherwise require a very large number of trucks. Where a suitable network exists, rail is therefore an economical way to carry heavy or bulky cargo long distances between cities and industrial areas.

The characteristics of rail freight follow from the nature of the network.

1. Large freight capacity per movement. Trains carry bulk commodities in specialised wagons and containers on flat wagons, combining many wagonloads into one movement.

2. Fixed routes and terminals. A train runs only where track exists, between terminals equipped to load and unload it. This makes rail services steady and repeatable where they operate.

3. Efficiency for heavy, dense cargo. The cost per tonne over land is low for cargo that fills the train's capacity, which is why mining and agricultural regions use rail heavily.

The advantages of rail freight are its economy for heavy bulk over long land distances, its large capacity per movement, and its independence from road congestion.

The limitations decide where rail is used.

1. The network limits reach. Rail can serve only points joined by track. Where lines do not exist, or do not connect the right places, rail is simply not an option, whatever its advantages on paper. How much railway infrastructure exists differs from one country and region to another.

2. Rail is rarely door-to-door. Goods almost always travel the first and last parts of the journey by road, moving between the origin, the rail terminal, and the final destination. Rail performs the middle, long-haul leg.

3. Dependence on schedules and terminals. Cargo must fit the service's timetable and be handed over at a terminal, which adds handling compared with a single truck journey.

Rail freight therefore suits heavy, non-urgent bulk cargo such as minerals, grain, timber, and clinker, and containerised goods moving long distances over land between connected terminals.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Multimodal and intermodal transport
BODY:
Sea, air, road, and rail each cover a different part of a journey, so long movements normally use more than one of them. Two terms describe how modes are combined, and they are related but not identical.

Multimodal transport is the transportation of goods using at least two different modes of transport under a single multimodal transport contract. One operator, called the multimodal transport operator, takes responsibility for the whole journey from origin to destination, even where different carriers, such as a shipping line and a trucking company, each perform one leg. The shipper deals with one contract, one responsible partner, and one point of contact.

Intermodal transport is the movement of goods using two or more transportation modes, typically in the same loading unit, without handling the goods themselves when changing modes. The loading unit is usually a container: goods are packed once at origin, and at every change of mode the container itself is lifted from ship to train to truck, while the goods inside are never touched.

The container is what makes such movements practical, because it is built to a standard that ships, trains, and trucks can all carry. Special types extend the principle: refrigerated containers carry perishables, open-top and flat-rack containers carry tall or heavy items, and tank containers carry liquids.

Packing goods once and lifting the sealed unit at each change of mode brings real benefits: less handling means less damage and theft, quicker transfers between modes, and simpler security because the container stays sealed.

The difference between the two terms is a common source of confusion, so state it precisely. Intermodal transport describes how the goods physically travel: in one and the same loading unit, untouched at each change of mode. Multimodal transport describes how the journey is contracted and who answers for it: one contract and one operator responsible end to end. The two are not competing choices. A single door-to-door container shipment can be intermodal in its physical form and multimodal in its contract at the same time.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: How modes combine in one journey
BODY:
The modes studied so far rarely appear alone in international trade. A typical import or export is a sequence of legs, each performed by the mode that suits it best; the previous lesson's journey from supplier to buyer usually crosses modes along the way.

A containerised import shows the pattern.

1. Road leg at origin. A truck collects the sealed container from the overseas supplier's premises and delivers it to the port of loading.

2. Sea leg. The container ship carries the container across the ocean to the destination port, such as Tema Port for a Ghanaian import.

3. Road leg at destination. After arrival formalities, a truck carries the container inland to a warehouse in Kumasi or another city.

The same logic appears in exports by air: a farm's produce travels by road to Kotoka International Airport, flies to a European airport, and travels by road again to the buyer's distribution centre. The aircraft does the long, fast leg; trucks collect and deliver at each end.

Notice what each mode contributes. The vessel or aircraft covers the long international leg, where its capacity or speed is worth the cost. Road transport covers the short legs, where door-to-door flexibility matters more than capacity. Where railways connect the right places, a train can take over an inland leg instead of trucks.

Who coordinates these legs depends on the contract. Under a multimodal transport contract, as defined earlier, one operator is responsible for arranging the whole chain, and the shipper holds that operator accountable from door to door. The same physical journey can also be assembled from separate contracts, one per leg, which the shipper or its freight forwarder then coordinates.

This is why mode selection is rarely a choice of one mode only. The real question is usually which combination of modes, contracted how, moves the goods best.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Factors that influence the choice of mode
BODY:
Choosing a transport mode is a balancing exercise. The main factors, and the questions they raise, are the following.

1. Nature of the cargo. Perishable goods need short transit times and continuous temperature control. High-value goods justify spending more on speed and security. Fragile goods favour fewer handlings. Heavy machinery and bulk commodities favour modes with large capacity. Dangerous goods face additional restrictions that differ by mode.

2. Urgency and required transit time. If the goods must arrive by a date, the shortlisted modes must be able to achieve it. Non-urgent goods can travel more slowly and more cheaply.

3. Cost of transport. Freight cost is compared against the value of the goods and the budget for the order. The differences between modes are large: over long distances, sea freight costs a fraction of air freight for the same weight. Paying for speed the goods do not need wastes money.

4. Quantity to be moved. The freight capacity each mode offers must match the consignment. A full container or truckload can be booked directly, while smaller consignments can share space through consolidation, a service an earlier lesson described under freight forwarders.

5. Accessibility and the transport network. The chosen mode must actually run between the relevant places; inland origins and destinations depend on road or rail connections for the first and last legs.

6. Reliability. A mode and route are judged not only on average speed but on how predictably they meet schedules, because downstream plans, from production runs to shop shelves, depend on promised arrival dates.

7. Total cost, not freight cost alone. Faster modes cost more to move goods but reduce the time stock is tied up in transit and the risk of running out; slower, cheaper modes do the reverse. A considered choice weighs the freight bill against these wider effects.

No single factor decides the answer. The appropriate mode best balances these factors for the specific shipment, as the worked examples later in this lesson demonstrate.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: A framework for comparing the modes
BODY:
With each mode described and the selection factors listed, the modes can now be compared directly. Five lenses cover the comparison: speed, cost, capacity, accessibility, and cargo suitability. The statements below are directional comparisons for building a shortlist; exact figures vary by route, season, and service.

1. Speed. Air freight is the fastest over long distances. Sea freight is the slowest, with voyages measured in weeks. Road freight is fast and direct over short and medium distances. Rail moves steadily over land where track exists. A multimodal journey's total transit time is the sum of its legs plus the handling at each change of mode.

2. Cost. Per unit of cargo over long distance, sea freight is normally the cheapest. Rail is economical for heavy loads over land. Road is efficient for short distances but comparatively expensive over long hauls. Air freight is the most expensive by a wide margin.

3. Capacity. Sea freight offers the greatest freight capacity, a single vessel carrying what would take thousands of trucks. Rail offers large capacity per train on its network. Road is limited per vehicle. Air offers the smallest capacity of all.

4. Accessibility. Road reaches nearly every address, making it the most accessible mode and the natural carrier of the last mile. Sea serves only ports, air only cargo airports, and rail only its own network. Multimodal combinations extend reach by connecting these networks.

5. Cargo suitability. Urgent, perishable, high-value, and light cargo suits air freight. Heavy, bulky, or plentiful non-urgent cargo suits sea freight. Heavy bulk over land between connected points suits rail. Regional distribution and every delivery to a specific address suit road.

Read together, the five lenses show why the modes coexist: each is the best answer to a different question. The worked examples that follow apply the framework to three Ghana-linked consignments and one international route.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Flying fresh green beans from Accra to Europe
BODY:
A commercial farm near Accra grows vegetables for export. A supermarket buyer in Europe places an order for fresh green beans, to arrive within days of harvest so the beans can reach the shelves while still fresh. This week the order is 150 cartons, and all quantities in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: The requirement is examined. Green beans have a shelf life of only a few days, and the buyer pays a premium for freshness. Sea freight from Tema Port would take weeks, longer than the beans can stay fresh, so sea freight is ruled out however cheap it is. Air freight is selected: the cargo's perishability demands speed, and its high value per unit justifies the higher freight cost.

Step 2: Harvest and the cold chain. The beans are harvested, sorted, and packed into cartons, then moved into a cold store to remove field heat. Temperature control continues at every stage that follows, because a break in the cold chain would shorten shelf life just as effectively as a slow mode would.

Step 3: Road leg to the airport. A refrigerated truck collects the 150 cartons from the farm and delivers them to the air cargo terminal at Kotoka International Airport. The road leg is short but essential: no aircraft will collect cartons from a farm.

Step 4: Acceptance and documentation. At the terminal, the shipment is checked in, screened, and documented. The airline or its agent issues an air waybill, recording the goods received for carriage to the destination airport.

Step 5: The air leg. The cartons fly to the destination airport in Europe within hours.

Step 6: Arrival and delivery. The buyer's agent completes the arrival formalities, and a truck delivers the beans to the buyer's distribution centre a day or two after packing.

What does this example demonstrate? The mode was chosen from the cargo's requirements outward: perishability demanded speed, and value justified cost. Road freight performed the first and last legs, so the shipment was already a combination of modes in practice.

Key lesson: air freight is not chosen despite its cost. It is chosen when the goods' shelf life and value make speed worth more than the saving a slower mode would offer.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Importing rice by sea through Tema Port
BODY:
An importing and distribution company in Accra buys packaged rice from a supplier in Asia for the Ghanaian market. The order is for 400 tonnes of rice packed into twenty containers. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: The requirement is examined. Rice is non-perishable with a long shelf life, the volume is large, and the company plans its purchases months in advance. Speed adds nothing the goods do not need, while cost per tonne matters greatly at this quantity. Sea freight is selected: for a heavy, non-urgent consignment between continents it offers the lowest cost per unit, and air freight would cost many times more for no benefit the goods can use.

Step 2: Booking and loading. The company works through a freight forwarder, which books space for the containers with a shipping line, as an earlier lesson described. At the port of loading, the rice is packed into the containers, which are sealed and delivered to the terminal in time for the sailing.

Step 3: The sea leg. The containers travel on a container ship to Tema Port. The voyage is measured in weeks, the longest transit time of any mode, and the company's purchasing plan was built around it.

Step 4: Arrival and the inland leg. After the arrival formalities at the port, trucks collect the containers and carry them to the company's warehouse in Accra, where the cartons are unloaded. The same sealed container did the sea leg and the road leg, and the goods themselves were not handled between the two.

Step 5: The comparison made explicit. Sea freight took the longest time of any mode but delivered 400 tonnes at the lowest cost per tonne. The rice's long shelf life made the weeks at sea harmless, and the saving helped the imported staple reach the market at a competitive price.

What does this example demonstrate? When cargo is heavy, bulky, or plentiful and not urgent, sea freight's capacity and low cost per unit outweigh its slow transit time.

Key lesson: the slowest and cheapest mode is often the correct choice, because transit time is only a defect when the goods need speed.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Distributing cement by road in Ghana
BODY:
A building-materials distributor in Takoradi supplies bagged cement to hardware shops and construction sites in Takoradi, Cape Coast, and the surrounding towns. Cement arrives at the distributor's depot in bulk loads and leaves it in bags. This example follows the outbound distribution.

Step 1: Inbound supply. Trucks deliver cement from the production plant to the distributor's depot, where it is stored and recorded.

Step 2: Orders arrive. Hardware shops order anything from a few dozen bags to a part-load; construction sites order scheduled quantities tied to their building programmes.

Step 3: Matching vehicles to loads. The distributor loads each order onto a suitable road vehicle: large deliveries onto an articulated truck that may serve several customers on one route, smaller orders onto a light truck or van.

Step 4: Door-to-door delivery. Each delivery ends at a specific address: a shopfront in a trading street or a site gate. Drivers offload, the receiving party checks and signs for the bags, and the paperwork confirms delivery.

Step 5: The last mile. Every one of these journeys ends with the final stage of transportation in which goods are delivered from a distribution point to the intended recipient. The distributor's vehicles perform this stage many times a day.

Why not another mode? Sea freight cannot reach any of these addresses; it serves ports. Rail freight would help only if track connected the depot to each customer, which address-by-address delivery rules out by its nature. Air freight has no role at these distances. Road freight is the only mode that can complete these movements, and its flexibility in vehicle size, timing, and routing suits orders that differ every day.

What does this example demonstrate? Regional distribution is road freight's natural territory. The mode's door-to-door reach, its range of vehicle sizes, and its freedom from fixed schedules match a daily pattern of many small, different deliveries.

Key lesson: the question is rarely which mode is strongest in the abstract. It is which mode can complete the movement as the goods actually require, and for delivery to an ordinary address, only one mode can.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: One order from China to Europe, three possible modes
BODY:
A manufacturer in Shenzhen, in China, supplies a retailer in Hamburg, in Germany. The retailer has confirmed a large order of consumer electronics and needs a delivery plan for the season. Three transport options are considered, and more than one may be used. The comparison is qualitative and quotes no figures.

Step 1: Sea freight, the default for volume. Containers leave a Chinese port by sea, pass along maritime shipping routes to a European port, and continue inland by road or rail. Sea freight offers the lowest cost per unit by a wide margin and the greatest capacity, but the longest transit times, measured in weeks. It suits the main volume of the order, which is forecast well in advance.

Step 2: Rail freight, the middle option over land. Overland rail services connect China with cities in Europe, carrying containers across the continent. Rail's position sits between the other two: faster than sea, slower than air, and priced between them. It suits goods too time-sensitive to spend weeks at sea but not urgent enough to justify the cost of flying, such as replenishment stock during the season.

Step 3: Air freight, for what cannot wait. The launch quantity and any urgently needed items fly from a Chinese airport to a European airport within days, at the highest cost per unit. The retailer accepts this cost on a small part of the order because launch dates carry sales it does not want to miss.

Step 4: Combining the options. The retailer and the manufacturer split the order: launch stock by air, mid-season replenishment by rail, and the bulk by sea. Each part travels by the mode whose profile fits its timing, and every journey still uses road legs at each end, in the combination patterns described earlier in this lesson.

What does this example demonstrate? The same goods, on the same route, for the same buyer can sensibly travel three different ways, because each mode answers a different need: cost for volume, balance for replenishment, and speed for urgency.

Lessons from the example: mode selection is a portfolio decision as much as a per-shipment decision, and the speed and cost profiles of sea, rail, and air give a trader a ladder on which to place each part of its demand.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: There is no single best mode of transport
BODY:
Beginners often ask which mode of transport is best, expecting a single answer. There is none. The comparison framework in this lesson has one purpose: to replace the search for a best mode with the search for the appropriate mode for this cargo, this route, and this delivery requirement.

Two errors show why. Calling air freight the best mode ignores that its cost is wasted on cargo that is not urgent: flying 400 tonnes of rice would multiply the freight bill without helping the goods. Calling sea freight the best mode ignores that its weeks of transit destroy value for anything perishable: the cheapest quote is worthless if the goods arrive spoiled. The same test applies to road and rail; each is the worst choice for someone else's cargo.

A related error is judging a decision on the freight bill alone. The cheapest transport can be the most expensive choice overall if it delivers late, forces the business to hold extra stock to cover its slowness, or exposes fragile goods to unnecessary handling. Total cost, including these wider effects, is what a business actually pays.

Three questions sort most decisions. What are the goods, and what do they tolerate? When must they arrive, and how certain must that date be? What does each available option cost, in money and in consequences? A mode chosen from the answers will rarely be glamorous, but it will be defensible.

Remember: in transport, best is not a property of the mode. It is a fit between the mode and the shipment.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Multimodal is not another word for intermodal
BODY:
The two terms for combined transport are often used interchangeably, and the confusion runs in one particular direction: people say intermodal when they mean the single-contract arrangement. The distinction is worth fixing, because the two words answer different questions.

Multimodal transport is about the contract and responsibility. It means carriage by at least two modes under a single multimodal transport contract, with one operator responsible for the goods from origin to destination. If you know whom to turn to when something goes wrong anywhere along the journey, the arrangement is multimodal in character.

Intermodal transport is about the physical unit. It means the goods travel in the same loading unit, typically a container, across two or more modes without the goods themselves being handled at the changes of mode. Under an intermodal movement the contracts are commonly arranged separately per leg, so there may be several responsible carriers rather than one.

Both features can appear in one shipment. A container packed at a factory, lifted unchanged from truck to ship to train, and delivered to a warehouse under one contract with one responsible operator is intermodal in its physical form and multimodal in its contract at the same time. Neither feature implies the other: goods can change mode in the same container under separate contracts, and a single contract can cover cargo that is handled and re-packed between legs.

When you meet the terms, ask two questions in order. Who is responsible, and under how many contracts? And are the goods themselves handled when the mode changes? The answers, not the labels, tell you which arrangement you have.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: A mode must exist on the route before it can be chosen
BODY:
The comparison framework ranks modes as though all of them were always available. In practice, availability decides before preference does. A mode can only be selected where its network actually connects the origin and the destination, and every mode's network has edges.

Sea freight requires a port at the loading end and a port at the receiving end. Air freight requires airports equipped to handle cargo, which not every airport is. Rail freight requires track between the right places; where a country's or region's railway network is thin, rail drops out of the shortlist regardless of its advantages on paper. Road freight needs a usable road, and even road has limits where terrain or road condition makes routes unreliable.

Inland geography matters as well. A business located far from any port or cargo airport depends on road or rail connections to reach the long-distance modes at all, and the final stage of almost every journey, the last-mile delivery, is completed by road. This is why road freight appears in nearly every international movement even when it crosses no border itself.

The practical habit this teaches is to map before costing. Before comparing quotes, establish how the origin connects to the destination: which ports, which airports, which railway lines, which roads. The options that do not physically connect the two points are not cheaper alternatives; they are not alternatives at all.

Because infrastructure differs from country to country and changes over time as networks grow, the shortlist of available modes should be re-checked for each route rather than assumed from experience elsewhere.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A fruit and vegetable exporter near Accra receives an order from a supermarket buyer in Europe for fresh green beans. The beans remain fresh for only a few days after harvest, and the buyer pays a premium price for fresh produce.

Which option best explains why air freight is the appropriate mode for this consignment?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Air freight is the cheapest mode of transport for goods moving between continents.
2. The beans' short shelf life requires the fastest available transit, and their high value per unit justifies paying the higher cost of air freight.
3. Air freight carries goods in sealed containers that are not opened until they reach the buyer.
4. Air freight removes the need for temperature-controlled handling before and after the flight.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Air freight's defining advantage is the shortest transit time of any mode, and its defining limitation is the highest cost. For fresh green beans, both facts point the same way: a shelf life of a few days rules out the weeks that sea freight requires, and the produce's premium value per unit makes the air freight bill worth paying. This is exactly the profile of cargo air freight exists to carry: urgent, perishable, high-value, and light.

Answer 1 fails because it reverses the cost relationship: sea freight, not air freight, is the cheapest mode over long distances. Answer 3 confuses air freight with containerised sea transport; air cargo travels on aircraft pallets and in airline unit load devices, not in sealed shipping containers. Answer 4 fails because perishable goods need an unbroken cold chain regardless of mode, including pre-cooling and refrigerated transport before the flight.

The takeaway: a mode is appropriate when its profile fits the cargo's requirements, and for perishable high-value produce, speed is worth more than the cost saving of a slower mode.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
An importer in Kumasi buys goods from a supplier in Asia. The goods travel by sea to Tema Port and then by road to Kumasi. The importer appointed one company under a single contract that covers the whole journey, and that company is responsible for the goods from the supplier's door to the warehouse door in Kumasi, even though a shipping line performs the sea leg and a trucking company performs the road leg.

Which statement best describes this arrangement?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. It is intermodal transport, because a single contract covers carriage by more than one mode.
2. It is multimodal transport, because at least two modes are used under a single contract with one operator responsible for the entire journey.
3. It is sea freight only, because the vessel carries the goods for the greater part of the distance.
4. It is road freight only, because the delivery into Kumasi is performed by a truck.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Multimodal transport is the transportation of goods using at least two different modes of transport under a single multimodal transport contract, with one operator responsible for the goods from origin to destination. The shipment described uses sea and road, is covered by one contract, and names one responsible company, which satisfies every element of the definition.

Answer 1 fails because it swaps the definitions, which is the most common confusion in this topic: the single contract and the single responsible operator are the marks of multimodal transport, while intermodal transport describes goods travelling in the same loading unit across modes without being handled, often under a separate contract for each leg. Answers 3 and 4 fail because naming a single mode contradicts the arrangement: the journey deliberately combines two modes, and neither the longer leg nor the final leg alone describes it.

The takeaway: when two or more modes move under one contract with one responsible operator, the arrangement is multimodal, whatever the modes are and whichever carries the goods furthest.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A food company must move 200 tonnes of dried maize from a depot to its factory in another region. A railway line connects the two towns, and both have goods terminals. The maize is non-perishable, and delivery is planned several weeks in advance.

Which option best explains why rail freight is a suitable choice for this movement?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Rail freight delivers door-to-door from the depot to the factory without any road transport.
2. Rail freight is the fastest of all transport modes available for this cargo.
3. Rail freight can move heavy, non-urgent bulk cargo efficiently over land between points connected by the rail network, with road vehicles covering the short legs at each end.
4. Rail freight is available only for consignments that are too small for sea freight to accept.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

Rail freight's defining strength is efficiency over land for heavy, dense, non-urgent cargo, and the scenario supplies every condition it needs: a large tonnage, no urgency, and a network that connects the two points. Because rail runs between terminals rather than between doors, the first and last legs of the journey are normally completed by road, which is exactly the combination pattern this lesson describes.

Answer 1 fails because rail is rarely door-to-door; a train serves its own network and terminals, not a depot gate or a factory yard, so road legs at each end are almost always required. Answer 2 fails because air freight, not rail, is the fastest mode, and speed is not rail's advantage in any case; rail's advantage is capacity and cost efficiency for bulk. Answer 4 fails because rail is not a fallback for consignments other modes reject; it is a first-choice mode for heavy bulk where the network allows.

The takeaway: where a network connects the right places, rail is often the most economical way to move heavy, non-urgent bulk over land, supported by road at both ends.
