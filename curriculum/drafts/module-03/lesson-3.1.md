=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The journey of goods as a chain of stages
BODY:
When a buyer agrees to purchase goods from a supplier, the agreement is only the beginning. The sale is complete in practical terms only when the goods reach the buyer in the agreed quantity and condition. Everything that happens between the two premises is the journey of goods, the subject of this lesson.

Two terms locate the journey. The origin is the location where a shipment begins its transportation journey; the destination is the location where goods are intended to be delivered. A shipment is a quantity of goods transported together under a particular transport arrangement, whether a single pallet, a full truck, or a container holding thousands of cartons. The physical transportation and handling of goods between locations is called cargo movement.

An international shipment typically passes through nine stages.

1. Order and preparation. The buyer's order is confirmed, stock is verified, and the goods are selected, counted, and made ready.

2. Packing and stuffing. Goods are packed to protect them for the whole journey, then loaded into a container or vehicle.

3. Collection and inland movement to the export point. Goods are collected and moved by road or rail to a port, airport, or border crossing.

4. Export formalities. The exporting country's customs requirements are completed and the goods are cleared to leave.

5. The main international carriage. Goods travel between the two countries by sea, air, road, or rail.

6. Arrival and import formalities. Goods arrive in the importing country, where customs formalities are completed and release is granted.

7. Deconsolidation at the destination facility. Combined loads are separated so that each receiver's goods can be recovered.

8. Inland delivery. Goods move from the arrival point to the buyer's premises.

9. Receipt and verification. The buyer counts and inspects the goods and confirms delivery.

A domestic journey follows the same skeleton without the border stages.

One idea unites all nine stages: the handoff. At each stage boundary, responsibility for the goods passes from one party to another, from supplier to carrier, carrier to terminal, and finally carrier to buyer. Every handoff is a point where goods, information, and money must stay aligned, echoing the coordination of goods, information, and resources that this course identified as the heart of logistics. When a handoff fails, goods stop, records disagree, and payments are disputed. The rest of this lesson walks through the stages, and the handoffs between them.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The participants who carry out the journey
BODY:
No single organisation performs the whole journey. The previous module introduced the main participants in the logistics ecosystem; this lesson places them along the route. Two terms identify the sender and the receiver.

The consignor is the person or organisation that sends goods to another party. In a typical sale, the consignor is the seller or exporter: the farm cooperative dispatching pineapples, the factory dispatching cartons of tomato paste. The consignee is the person or organisation designated to receive the goods. In a typical sale, the consignee is the buyer or importer named in the transport documents. Usually the two names correspond to seller and buyer, but not always: a supplier may ship goods to a shop on the buyer's behalf, and the documents then name that receiving party as consignee. The names follow who sends and who is designated to receive, not who pays or who owns the vehicles.

Between them stand the supporting roles, each active at particular stages.

1. Carriers move the goods by sea, air, road, or rail.

2. Freight forwarders arrange and coordinate shipments on behalf of exporters and importers, booking carriage and organising handoffs.

3. Warehouse and terminal operators receive, store, consolidate, and hand over cargo at the facilities along the route.

4. Customs representatives prepare declarations and guide goods through the formalities of the exporting and importing countries.

5. Drivers, equipment operators, and terminal staff perform the physical cargo handling at each handoff.

Each participant was described in the previous module; what matters here is where each one acts. A single international journey may pass through a dozen organisations, and the consignor and consignee must be able to rely on all of them. That reliance holds only when every participant works from accurate, current information, which is why information flows matter in this lesson as much as the movement of the goods.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Preparing and packing goods at the origin
BODY:
The journey begins before anything moves. Stage one, order and preparation, starts when the buyer's order is received and confirmed. The supplier verifies availability in the ordered quantity, then warehouse staff pick, count, inspect, and stage the goods for dispatch. Errors caught here cost little; the same errors discovered at the destination cost money, time, and customer confidence. Preparation also includes checking that any certificates the goods require can be obtained before departure.

Packing decisions follow. Packaging serves two purposes across the entire journey: protection and handling. For protection, goods must survive stacking, mechanical handling, changes in humidity and temperature, and the vibration of long-distance transport, which is why packaging chosen for export journeys is usually sturdier than packaging for a short local delivery. For handling, the form of the packaging determines how goods can be lifted, stacked, and counted: cartons on pallets move by forklift in seconds, while loose cartons must be handled piece by piece.

Stage two is packing and stuffing. Stuffing is the industry term for loading goods into a container or vehicle. Goods are arranged and secured so that they cannot shift in transit, and a seal is applied to the container so that parties downstream can tell whether it was opened along the way. For perishable products, stuffing is where the cold chain begins: the unbroken path of temperature-controlled storage and transport, with continuous monitoring, that carries sensitive goods from origin to destination. Fresh produce is pre-cooled before it is loaded, and refrigerated equipment keeps it at the set temperature at every later stage. A consignment of fresh pineapples, for example, is packed into crates, stacked on pallets, and stuffed into a refrigerated container held at the required temperature; cartons of tomato paste are palletised and stuffed into a dry container. The packing choices made at the origin protect the goods through every handoff that follows.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Collection and the inland movement to the export point
BODY:
Stage three begins with collection. Collection is the process of picking up goods from a supplier, warehouse, or designated location for transportation. Collection is arranged in advance: the transport operator, or the freight forwarder acting for the exporter, is given the collection address, the agreed time, and the details of the goods. The driver must arrive with instructions that match the cargo, because a mismatch between information and goods strands the cargo and disturbs every later connection.

From the supplier's premises, the goods move inland to the export point: the port, airport, or border crossing where the international carriage will begin. Two patterns are common. A full container load is often stuffed at the supplier's own premises: an empty container is delivered there, loaded and sealed, then trucked to the port. Smaller quantities are usually carried to a warehouse or container freight station, where the goods of several exporters are consolidated, meaning combined into one container or vehicle to share the cost of the journey. Consolidation saves money for shippers with small loads, but it adds an extra handling point and depends on accurate marking, because every consignment in the combined load must be identifiable so that it can be separated again at the destination.

The inland leg ends at the export point, and it ends against a clock. Vessels, aircraft, and scheduled road services depart at fixed times, and cargo must be handed over before the carrier's cut-off, the latest time at which cargo can be accepted for a particular departure. A shipment that misses its cut-off waits for the next departure, which may be days away. Careful exporters therefore plan the inland leg backwards from the cut-off, allowing time for the drive, the terminal queues, and the formalities before handover.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Export formalities and the handover to the international carrier
BODY:
Stage four takes place before the goods may leave the country. The customs formalities of the exporting country must be completed: an export declaration describing the goods, their value, and their destination is lodged with customs, and customs releases the goods for export when the declaration is in order. The exact requirements differ from country to country and from goods to goods. Some goods additionally require permits or certificates; plants and plant products, for instance, are commonly covered by a phytosanitary certificate issued by the exporting country's plant-protection authority. Responsibility for export formalities follows the trade terms of the sales contract, commonly expressed using the Incoterms 2020 rules; in most international sales the seller's side clears the goods for export.

When the formalities are complete, the goods are handed to the international carrier at the port, airport, or terminal. The carrier checks and accepts the cargo, and a transport document, for sea cargo a bill of lading, records what was received and on what terms it will be carried; the documents themselves belong to a later lesson. For containers, one safety requirement deserves mention here: before loading, the shipper must declare the container's verified gross mass, its actual total weight including the cargo, and a container whose weight has not been verified may not be loaded. Carriers also set a documentation cut-off alongside the cargo cut-off, so shipping information must be submitted on time as well as the physical boxes; a shipment missing either usually waits for the next vessel or flight.

The stage ends with loading and a significant handoff: the container is lifted aboard the vessel or the cargo onto the aircraft, and responsibility for the export leg has passed from the exporter's side to the carrier. A tracking event such as loaded or departed is recorded, opening the record of the main carriage that the consignor and consignee will follow from afar.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The main international carriage
BODY:
Stage five is the main carriage, sometimes called the line-haul: the long international leg between the export point and the arrival point in the destination country. The mode chosen, sea, air, road, or rail, depends on the nature of the goods, the distance, the urgency, and the cost; the next lesson, Modes of Transport: Sea, Air, Road, Rail, and Multimodal, compares the modes in detail, so they are only named here.

During the carriage, goods are in the continuous custody of the carrier, but they are not necessarily left untouched. Some journeys are direct, while others involve transhipment, meaning the transfer of the goods, or of the container holding them, from one vessel or aircraft to another at an intermediate hub. Every transfer is another cargo handling operation, another point at which delay or damage can occur, and another event in the tracking record. Goods may also be stored in transit, waiting for a connecting departure, space, or paperwork; storage during a journey is a normal part of many international movements.

The carrier's duties are to carry the goods with care, to follow the agreed route and schedule as far as conditions allow, and to keep the goods safe, with refrigerated equipment kept powered for sensitive cargo. The main carriage also concentrates the physical risks of the journey: weather, handling damage, and delay. Many shippers therefore arrange insurance cover for goods in transit; insurance is treated later in this course, so it is only noted here that the main carriage is the stage most often insured against.

Throughout the carriage, the participants report position and status events, departed, transhipped, expected arrival, arrived, which feed the tracking and communication practices described near the end of this lesson.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Arrival, import formalities, and release
BODY:
Stage six begins when the vessel berths, the aircraft lands, or the truck reaches the border crossing. For an import journey, arrival is not relief but the start of the most demanding formal stage, because the goods are now under the control of the importing country's customs authorities. Until the import formalities are completed and release is granted, they cannot lawfully be delivered to anyone.

The import formalities centre on a declaration. A declaration describing the goods, what they are, what they are worth, and where they come from, is lodged with the customs administration, usually by the importer or by a customs representative acting for the importer. On the basis of the declaration, customs assesses the duties and taxes payable under the country's rules; rates and procedures differ from country to country and are a later subject of this course. Modern practice lodges much of this information before the goods arrive, known as pre-arrival declaration: when customs has the data in advance, it can analyse risk early and release goods much faster on arrival.

Customs does not inspect everything. Most administrations use risk-based selection: many, including Ghana's, sort declarations into channels, releasing some shipments on the documents alone, checking others' documents, and physically inspecting only shipments that present a risk. In Ghana, customs clearance is processed electronically through ICUMS, the national customs management system used at the country's ports of entry. When customs is satisfied, the declaration correct and any duties and taxes paid as applicable, the goods are released from customs control. Release is the gate between this stage and the rest of the journey: until it is granted, containers cannot leave the terminal and consignments cannot leave a customs facility. An error in the declaration discovered here leaves goods waiting exactly when they are closest to their destination, which is why accurate information at the arrival handoff matters as much as careful physical handling.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Deconsolidation, destination storage, and collection
BODY:
Stage seven is deconsolidation, the mirror image of the consolidation that may have happened at origin. Deconsolidation means separating a combined load into the individual shipments it contains, so that each consignee can receive its own goods. The work happens at a destination facility: a container freight station, a terminal, or a bonded warehouse. Where a container holds the goods of a single consignee, no separation is needed: the container is usually trucked intact to the consignee's premises and unpacked there. Where it holds the consolidated goods of several consignees, it is taken to a container freight station, unpacked, and its contents separated, checked against the documents, and held ready for each consignee. Each consignee's goods must be identified and recorded correctly, because a consignment placed in the wrong pile is one of the most confusing errors to unwind later.

Goods waiting at the destination, for release, for collection, or for onward transport, are stored at terminals, freight stations, or warehouses. Destination storage is a service with a clock attached. Terminals and carriers normally allow a limited period of free time after arrival or discharge, and once that period ends, storage and related charges begin to accumulate day by day; the lengths of these periods and the amounts charged are set by each carrier and terminal, not by any general rule. That clock is one of the practical reasons importers complete their formalities promptly and arrange collection early.

Stage seven closes with collection: once the goods are released, they are collected by road for the final inland delivery, usually by a truck arranged by the consignee or the consignee's representative. Importers who use tracking information to book the truck against the expected release date save days at this handoff; those who wait until release is granted begin the search for transport while the free-time clock is already running.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Final delivery, verification, and proof of delivery
BODY:
Stage eight is the inland delivery, the movement of goods from the arrival point to the buyer's premises. This is the stage most people picture when they think of logistics, yet it is only one of nine, and by the time it happens, most of the journey's decisions have been made.

Stage nine closes the journey with delivery and verification. Delivery is the transfer of goods to the intended recipient or designated destination. Receiving goods well is a discipline of its own: the receiving staff count the packages against the accompanying documents, inspect their condition, and compare the goods with the order itself. Any discrepancy, a shortage, a damaged carton, a wrong item, is recorded at the moment of receipt, while the carrier is still present and responsibility can still be traced to the correct party. A discrepancy recorded a day later is far harder to investigate, because nobody can say when the loss or damage occurred.

The delivery is confirmed by proof of delivery, usually abbreviated to POD. A proof of delivery is a document or electronic record confirming that goods have been delivered or received. It may take the form of a signed delivery note, a photograph, or an electronic confirmation carrying the time, place, and receiver's identity. The POD performs three jobs in one: it closes the final handoff, because responsibility for the goods passes to the buyer; it anchors the records, because the buyer's stock records increase and the supplier's records show the order fulfilled; and it supports what follows commercially, since invoicing and payment under the agreed terms generally depend on evidence of what was actually delivered.

The journey, then, is complete not when the truck arrives, but when the goods are verified, the proof of delivery is issued, and the records on both sides agree. The flow of money catches up with the flow of goods according to the payment terms of the contract, ending with goods, information, and money reconciled.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Shipment tracking, communication, and the causes of delay
BODY:
Shipment tracking is the process of monitoring the location and progress of goods during transportation. Tracking begins at booking and ends at proof of delivery: an event is recorded at every meaningful point of the journey, collected, export cleared, loaded, departed, transhipped, arrived, released, delivered. The events come from the systems of carriers and terminals, from drivers' updates, and from forwarders' messages, letting every participant see where the shipment stands without seeing the goods.

Tracking matters for four reasons.

1. Planning. Parties prepare each stage before it arrives: trucks are booked, warehouse labour is scheduled, and cold-store space is reserved against the expected arrival, not after it.

2. Early detection. A problem seen early is a problem with options: documents can be corrected before arrival, an alternative connection can be sought, and customers can be warned while adjustment is still possible.

3. Coordination. Each organisation in the chain plans on facts instead of guesses, which reduces the small mismatches that waste time at every handoff.

4. Customer service. Businesses that can answer credibly where an order is and when it will arrive keep the confidence of their own customers even when the journey is imperfect.

Tracking is only half of the practice; communication is the other half. A tracking event creates value only when it reaches the parties who must act, in time to act. Communication between logistics participants is therefore deliberate: the parties agree who is told what, when, and through which channel, from the collection notice at origin to the proof of delivery at destination.

Even with good tracking and communication, delays occur. The common factors affecting timely delivery include the following.

1. Incomplete or incorrect documents, which hold goods at export or import formalities.

2. Congestion at ports, airports, borders, or on inland roads.

3. Missed cut-offs and missed connections, which push a shipment to the next departure.

4. Weather and seasonal conditions on sea, in the air, and on land.

5. Shortages of equipment or capacity, when no container, truck, or vessel space is available.

6. Inspections and holds by authorities during formalities.

7. Poor communication itself, when information arrives late, incomplete, or not at all.

Most of these causes cannot be eliminated by any single party, but all of them can be anticipated, monitored, and communicated, which is why tracking and communication are not extras added to a journey but core operations of it.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: An export journey: fresh pineapples from Ghana to Europe
BODY:
Consider a farm cooperative in Ghana's Eastern Region that grows fresh pineapple for export. A supermarket distributor in Rotterdam orders a consignment for a fixed sailing date. The cooperative books the shipment through a freight forwarder; all quantities and times are illustrative.

Step 1: Order and preparation. The distributor's order specifies the variety, the quantity, 18 pallets, the quality standard, and the sailing date. The cooperative confirms availability and plans the harvest to match, so fruit is cut at the right maturity and moves to packing within hours.

Step 2: Packing. The fruit is graded, packed into crates, and the crates are stacked on pallets. Because pineapple is a plant product, the cooperative obtains a phytosanitary certificate from the national plant-protection authority in good time.

Step 3: Stuffing. A refrigerated container, a reefer, is delivered to the packing station and pre-cooled. The 18 pallets are stuffed, secured against movement, and the doors are sealed; temperature recording begins. The cold chain must now hold until Rotterdam.

Step 4: Inland movement. The reefer is trucked to Tema Port, timed to arrive before the shipping line's cargo cut-off for the booked vessel.

Step 5: Export formalities. The cooperative's agent lodges the export declaration, and the exporting country's customs completes the formalities, checking the declaration and accompanying certificates.

Step 6: Handover and loading. The terminal receives the container before the cut-off, the verified gross mass having been declared as required before loading, and the vessel loads the box, plugging it into its power supply.

Step 7: Main carriage. For this example, assume a sea voyage of about twelve days to Europe. The container is kept at temperature and powered throughout, and the forwarder shares tracking events, departed, transhipment, expected arrival, with the distributor.

Step 8: Arrival and import formalities. The distributor's agent lodges the import declaration, the destination country's customs formalities are completed, and the goods are released.

Step 9: Delivery and verification. The container is trucked to the distributor's cold store in Rotterdam, unpacked, and its temperature record is checked along with a count and inspection of the fruit. The proof of delivery is issued, stock records are updated, and payment follows the agreed terms.

What does this example demonstrate? Nine stages, one unbroken cold chain, and a handoff at every stage boundary. The goods passed from cooperative to transport operators to terminal to shipping line to the European agent to the distributor, and the information, from order to certificates to tracking events to proof of delivery, moved in step with them.

Key lesson: an export succeeds when physical handling and information are both right at every handoff. One missed cut-off or one broken temperature record can undo the whole journey.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: An import journey: tomato paste from Italy to Kumasi
BODY:
Consider a wholesale distributor in Kumasi that supplies catering-size cartons of tomato paste to retailers across the Ashanti Region. The distributor orders a full container load from a food producer in Italy; in this example, 20 pallets holding 1,000 cartons. The agreed trade terms make the seller responsible for the carriage to Tema, while the buyer is responsible for clearing the goods on import and taking delivery in Ghana; all quantities are illustrative.

Step 1: Order and preparation. The order is confirmed, the producer verifies stock, and the cartons are counted and stacked on pallets at the Italian factory.

Step 2: Stuffing. An empty container is delivered to the factory, the 20 pallets are stuffed and secured, and the doors are sealed. The loading details are sent to the carrier in time for the documentation cut-off.

Step 3: Inland movement. The sealed container is trucked from the factory to an Italian seaport, arriving before the cargo cut-off for the booked vessel.

Step 4: Export formalities. The seller completes the export formalities of the exporting country, and the container is cleared to leave.

Step 5: Main carriage. The container is loaded and sails for Tema. During the voyage, the carrier's tracking updates show the departure, any transhipment, and a firmer expected arrival date.

Step 6: Arrival and import formalities. Before the vessel arrives, the distributor's customs representative prepares the import declaration from the seller's documents. Once the vessel berths at Tema, the declaration is processed electronically through Ghana's customs system, and customs releases the goods once it is in order and the applicable duties and taxes are settled.

Step 7: Release and collection. The released container is collected from the terminal by a truck the distributor booked in advance against the expected release date, so that no time-based storage charges accumulate.

Step 8: Inland delivery. The truck carries the container from Tema to Kumasi, where it arrives at the distributor's warehouse with its seal intact.

Step 9: Receipt and verification. Warehouse staff confirm the seal, break it, count the 1,000 cartons against the documents, and inspect for damage. The proof of delivery is signed, stock records are updated, and the invoice is settled under the agreed terms.

What does this example demonstrate? In an import journey, the buyer's heaviest responsibilities begin when the goods arrive: the formalities, the release, the collection, the inland delivery, and the receipt are the consignee's side of the work, usually performed through representatives.

Key lesson: the same nine stages appear in every international journey. What changes between an export and an import is which party carries the load at each stage, and the trade terms agreed in the sales contract are what fix that division.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Managing a delay with tracking and communication: medicines from Mumbai to Accra
BODY:
Consider a pharmaceutical importer in Accra that orders 40 cartons of temperature-sensitive medicines from a supplier in Mumbai, India. The medicines are destined for hospital supply, so reliability matters as much as speed; air freight keeps the exposure time short. All details are illustrative.

Step 1: Order and preparation. The order is confirmed, the supplier picks and counts the 40 cartons, and the medicines are packed in insulated cartons prepared for temperature-sensitive air cargo.

Step 2: Handover and export formalities. The consignment is accepted by the air carrier at Mumbai against the airline's requirements, the export formalities are completed, and the shipment is booked on an air freight service to Kotoka International Airport routed through an intermediate hub.

Step 3: Carriage and a problem spotted early. The freight forwarder monitors the tracking events. Two days before the expected arrival, while reviewing the shipment documents against the destination requirements, the forwarder notices that one supporting certificate from the supplier is incomplete.

Step 4: Communication. The forwarder alerts the importer's customs representative immediately. The representative confirms that the gap would prevent release, and the supplier in Mumbai issues a corrected certificate electronically within a day. The correction is complete before the medicines land.

Step 5: Arrival and import formalities. The consignment arrives at Kotoka International Airport. The import declaration, prepared in advance, is already lodged; customs checks the documents, now complete, and releases the goods. Throughout the handling at the airport, the importer's arranged temperature-controlled storage keeps the medicines within their required range.

Step 6: Delivery and verification. The cartons are trucked to the importer's temperature-controlled warehouse in Accra, counted, and checked, including the temperature indicators on the cartons. The proof of delivery is signed, the stock records are updated, and the hospital orders are dispatched on schedule.

Now consider the same journey without communication. The certificate gap would have been discovered only after arrival, at the clearance check. The medicines would have waited at the airport while a corrected document was chased across two countries; storage charges would have accumulated; the cold chain would have faced avoidable risk; and hospital orders would have slipped.

What does this example demonstrate? Delay factors, incomplete documents, inspections, congestion, cannot always be prevented, but they can be detected early and managed. Tracking provided the visibility; communication turned that visibility into action by putting the problem in front of the one party who could fix it while there was still time.

Key lesson: the value of shipment tracking is realised through communication. Information must reach the party who can act, while action is still possible.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Following the handoffs: frozen fish from Tema to Tamale
BODY:
The handoff concept can be seen at its clearest in a simple domestic journey. A frozen-food company in Tema receives an order from a wholesaler in Tamale: 300 cartons of frozen fish, to be delivered within three days. There is no border crossing and no international carriage, yet the same structure of handoffs appears. All quantities are illustrative.

Step 1: Order and preparation. The order is confirmed, and 300 cartons are drawn from the cold store, counted, and stacked on pallets, remaining at freezer temperature throughout.

Step 2: Collection. A refrigerated truck arrives. The driver presents collection instructions listing 300 cartons; the cold store staff check that the trailer is at the correct temperature, load the pallets, and both parties sign the collection record. This is the first handoff: the goods pass, and a signed record of exactly what passed travels with them.

Step 3: Inland movement. The truck makes the long road journey north. The driver keeps the trailer at the set temperature, and the dispatcher follows progress through periodic updates.

Step 4: Delivery and verification. At the Tamale warehouse, the wholesaler's staff count the cartons: 298 are intact and 2 are crushed. The damage is noted on the delivery document before it is signed, while the driver is still present and the cause, loading or transit, can still be investigated.

Step 5: Records and payment. The signed proof of delivery, carrying the damage note, returns to the Tema company. Its records show the order fulfilled with two cartons damaged, the matter is resolved under the agreed terms, and the invoice is prepared to match the delivered quantity. Payment follows according to the agreed terms.

Now map the handoffs. At collection, the goods were 300 cartons and the information was the signed collection record, the very document the later invoice would rely on. At delivery, the goods were 298 intact cartons and 2 noted as damaged, and the information was the proof of delivery carrying that damage note, on which the invoice was built rather than on the original order.

What does this example demonstrate? Even the simplest journey is a chain of handoffs, and at each one the goods, the information, and the payment records must match. Where they match, disputes are rare and quickly settled. Where they do not, the parties argue about what happened instead of running their businesses.

Key lesson: treat every handoff as a small transaction in itself. Goods pass, a record proves what passed, and those records carry the money flows that follow.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Responsibility does not always sit where the goods are
BODY:
A common beginner assumption is that whoever physically holds the goods is responsible for them, and that responsibility passes only when the goods physically change hands. This is not how journeys work. Responsibility for the goods, for the documents, and for the charges passes at points agreed in advance, and those points may be far from the goods' physical location.

The division of responsibility follows the trade terms of the sales contract, commonly expressed using the Incoterms 2020 rules, which allocate tasks, costs, and risk-transfer points between seller and buyer. Two consequences surprise beginners. First, under some rules the seller arranges and pays for carriage all the way to the destination while the risk of loss or damage has already passed to the buyer much earlier, for example when the goods are handed to the first carrier at origin. A buyer can therefore carry the risk of a voyage whose freight the seller is paying for. Second, the rules allocate tasks, export formalities to one side, import formalities to the other, but they do not transfer ownership of the goods, and they are not themselves the contract of carriage.

The practical habit from this lesson: at every stage, ask whose responsibility the goods are here, who is preparing the information, and who pays the charges now arising. Journeys go wrong most often at handoffs where nobody can answer those three questions with certainty.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Arrival is not delivery, and tracking is not control
BODY:
Two misconceptions deserve a final warning.

The first is that arrival means the job is done. Arrival begins the most delicate part of an import journey: the import formalities, the release, the deconsolidation or collection, the inland delivery, and the verification. Goods can sit for days after a vessel berths while declarations are processed, inspections are conducted, and collection transport is arranged, and once the allowed free time ends, time-based charges begin to accumulate. Delivery happens only when the goods are verified and the proof of delivery is issued, not when the truck reaches the gate and certainly not when the ship docks.

The second is that because goods are tracked, they are under control. Tracking shows location and status; it does not clear customs, correct a wrong document, load a truck, or keep a refrigerated container at temperature. Control over a journey comes from what participants do with tracking information: correcting documents before arrival, booking trucks before release, warning customers before a delay becomes a missed promise. A party who watches a tracking screen without acting on it has observation, not control.

A successful journey is therefore measured against the whole chain, not against the arrival event: the goods arrive in the right quantity and condition, the formalities are completed in both countries, the records at every handoff are accurate, and the delivery is verified with proof of delivery and payment records aligned. The goods, the information, and the money arrive together, and that is the real end of the journey of goods from supplier to buyer.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A container of imported rice is discharged from a vessel at a Ghanaian seaport. The distributor in Kumasi is waiting for the goods.

Which option best describes what must happen before the distributor receives the rice?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The shipping line delivers the container directly to the distributor's warehouse as soon as the vessel berths.
2. The journey is complete, because arrival in the destination country ends the movement of goods.
3. Import formalities must be completed and the goods released by customs before the container can be collected and delivered inland to Kumasi.
4. The consignor in the exporting country must now arrange transport from the port to the distributor's premises.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

After a shipment arrives in the importing country, the goods remain under customs control until the import formalities are completed and the goods are released. Only then can the container be collected from the terminal, or the consignment collected from a destination facility, and moved inland to the buyer's premises. Arrival opens the destination stages of the journey; it does not end it.

Answer 1 fails because carriers do not deliver automatically at the gangway: release must be obtained and collection arranged. Answer 2 confuses arrival with completion, ignoring formalities, collection, and delivery. Answer 4 looks backwards along the chain: the consignor's responsibilities ended at the export handover, and responsibility has since passed to the importer's side.

The takeaway: between arrival at the port and delivery to the buyer stand the import formalities, the release, and the inland delivery, and each is a stage of the journey in its own right.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
A shea butter cooperative in Tamale sends 400 cartons to a cosmetics company in Hamburg, and a freight forwarder arranges the ocean shipment on behalf of the cooperative.

Which statement best describes the consignor and the consignee of this shipment?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The consignor is the shipping line and the consignee is the freight forwarder, because they physically handle the goods.
2. The cooperative is the consignor, because it sends the goods, and the cosmetics company is the consignee, because it is designated to receive them.
3. The cooperative is the consignee, because it prepares the goods, and the cosmetics company is the consignor, because it pays for them.
4. The freight forwarder is the consignor and the cooperative is the consignee, because the forwarder coordinates the shipment.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The consignor is the person or organisation that sends goods to another party, and the consignee is the person or organisation designated to receive them. In this shipment the cooperative dispatches the 400 cartons from Tamale, so it is the consignor, and the Hamburg company is named to receive them, so it is the consignee. The parties who physically handle or coordinate the goods do not take either name.

Answer 1 mistakes the carrier and the forwarder for the sender and receiver; they are service providers acting on the shipment, not its endpoints. Answer 3 reverses the terms by tying them to preparation and payment, neither of which decides the names. Answer 4 confuses coordination with sending.

The takeaway: consignor and consignee describe who sends and who is designated to receive a shipment, regardless of who transports it, coordinates it, or pays for it.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
An importer learns from its freight forwarder, three days before the vessel arrives, that one of the documents needed for customs release is incomplete.

Which option best explains why this early communication matters?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Good communication makes the incomplete document acceptable to customs without correction.
2. Early warning allows the importer to correct the document before arrival, so the goods can be released promptly instead of waiting after arrival while charges accumulate.
3. Tracking guarantees that the vessel will arrive on schedule, so the document problem will resolve itself.
4. Delays after arrival are always the carrier's responsibility, so the importer need take no action.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The value of shipment tracking is realised through communication: an event or a spotted problem must reach the party who can act while action is still possible. Here, the early warning gives the importer time to obtain a corrected document before the vessel berths, so the declaration can be processed and the goods released on arrival, instead of the goods sitting in port after arrival while storage and related charges begin once the free period ends.

Answer 1 is wrong because communication supports documents but never replaces them; customs releases goods against documents that are complete and correct. Answer 3 is wrong because tracking reports progress and expected arrival but guarantees nothing, and no schedule resolves a document defect. Answer 4 is wrong because the importer's side is responsible for completing its import formalities, and delay costs money whoever is at fault.

The takeaway: early detection through tracking, followed by prompt communication to the party who can act, turns a potential crisis into a short, managed delay.

- The nine-stage chain (order and preparation, packing and stuffing, collection and inland movement, export formalities, main carriage, arrival and import formalities, deconsolidation, inland delivery, receipt and verification) is a teaching framework built from standard international trade practice — source: general business knowledge; consistent with curriculum/research/trade-transport-standards.md sections 4, 5 and 7.
- Incoterms 2020 is the ICC's set of eleven rules allocating tasks, costs, and risk-transfer points between seller and buyer; the rules do not transfer ownership and are not themselves the contract of carriage — source: trade-transport-standards.md section 1.
- Export clearance is the seller's task under all Incoterms 2020 rules except EXW, and import clearance is the buyer's under all except DDP; under some rules (the C rules) the seller pays carriage to destination while risk passes at shipment — source: trade-transport-standards.md section 1.
- Since 1 July 2016 (SOLAS regulation VI/2) the shipper must obtain and communicate the verified gross mass of every packed container, and a container without verified gross mass may not be loaded — source: trade-transport-standards.md section 7.
- Carriers set a cargo cut-off and a documentation cut-off before each sailing, and a shipment missing a cut-off usually rolls to the next departure — source: trade-transport-standards.md section 7 (carrier practice).
- A phytosanitary certificate is issued by the plant-protection authority of the exporting country for plants and plant products, in IPPC model format — source: trade-transport-standards.md section 5.
- Pre-arrival lodging of customs declaration data enables advance risk analysis and faster release on arrival — source: trade-transport-standards.md section 12.
- Many customs administrations, including Ghana's, use a channel model sorting declarations into release without inspection, document check, or physical inspection — source: trade-transport-standards.md section 12.
- Ghana's customs clearance is processed electronically through ICUMS, the national end-to-end customs management system used at the country's ports of entry and exit — source: trade-transport-standards.md section 12 (Ghana's single window operates through ICUMS) and logistics-scm-frameworks.md section 9 (Ghana anchor: end-to-end e-customs platform at all ports of entry and exit).
- Carriers and terminals allow a limited period of free time after arrival, after which time-based charges (storage, demurrage, detention) accumulate; amounts and periods are purely contractual and no standard figures exist, and none are stated in this lesson — source: trade-transport-standards.md section 11.
- Refrigerated (reefer) containers carry perishables, and cold-chain logistics means temperature-controlled storage and transport with continuous monitoring — source: trade-transport-standards.md section 6; logistics-scm-frameworks.md section 1.
- Consolidation (groupage) combines several shippers' smaller loads in one container or vehicle, cheaper per unit but with more handling and a need for accurate marking — source: logistics-scm-frameworks.md section 6.
- An ocean bill of lading records receipt of the goods and the terms of carriage; named only in passing, with document details reserved for a later lesson — source: trade-transport-standards.md section 4.
- Air cargo is accepted by carriers against acceptance requirements including security screening and acceptance checks — source: trade-transport-standards.md section 8.
- The order-to-receipt flow runs from order to shipment to goods receipt checked against the order, with invoicing and payment following on the basis of delivery records — source: logistics-scm-frameworks.md section 3.
- Track-and-trace records shipment position and status as scan or status events from booking to delivery — source: logistics-scm-frameworks.md section 9.
