# DRAFT — Lesson 10.4: Preparing and Evaluating Logistics Quotations

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: What a logistics quotation is and what it is for
BODY:
A logistics quotation is a commercial offer stating proposed charges and conditions for specified logistics services. When a business asks three freight forwarders what it would cost to move four pallets of goods from Accra to Antwerp, the documents those forwarders return are quotations: each states what the service will cost, what the service covers, and on what conditions the price stands.

The quotation serves both sides of the transaction. For the buyer, it converts an enquiry into a price that can be compared, budgeted, and approved internally. For the provider, it is the moment at which the business commits itself: once the customer accepts the quotation on its terms, the provider is expected to perform the stated scope at the stated price. The quotation therefore has a commercial purpose beyond information. It is the document on which the booking is based, the standard against which later invoices should be checked, and the boundary between a smooth service relationship and a string of disputes.

It is equally important to know what a quotation is not. A quotation is an offer, not a contract of carriage. It becomes commercially binding through acceptance on its terms, within the period for which it is offered; the contract of carriage itself arises later, through the booking and the transport document. That distinction, examined in the closing notes of this lesson, drives much of the professional discipline taught here.

The lesson has two halves. First, preparing a quotation: the costing discipline by which a provider estimates each cost family, adds the commercial layer, and states scope, validity, and conditions. Second, evaluating a quotation: the professional checklist by which a buyer compares competing offers on total cost and commercial risk rather than on headline price.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The information required to prepare an accurate quotation
BODY:
Lesson 5.2, Freight Quotations and Shipment Booking, examined the enquiry from the shipper's side: what the buyer must tell the forwarder so that a meaningful price can be returned. This lesson picks up the same document from the other side of the desk, where the costing professional receives the enquiry, tests it, and prices it. The information required to prepare an accurate quotation covers the following.

1. The cargo. Description, number of packages or pallets, gross weight, dimensions, and packaging type. Vague descriptions prevent accurate costing; the professional confirms what the cargo actually is, because weight, volume, handling requirements, and regulatory treatment all hang on it. Lesson 5.2's rule that chargeable weight is priced on the greater of actual weight and volumetric weight is the arithmetic expression of the same fact.

2. The route and its scope. Origin point and destination point, stated precisely: a warehouse door in Accra or a port of loading; a warehouse in Lomé or a port of discharge. Door-to-door and port-to-port are different services with different prices, and a quotation that does not say which one it covers cannot be accurate.

3. The commercial frame. For international sales, the agreed Incoterms 2020 rule, which allocates carriage, insurance, and clearance between seller and buyer and therefore determines which legs the quotation must cover.

4. The schedule. When the cargo will be ready, how often it moves, and whether the enquiry concerns one shipment or a recurring programme, because readiness dates and frequency change both the costing and the commercial terms.

5. Special requirements. Temperature control, dangerous goods, out-of-gauge pieces, lifting arrangements, and the documents and permits the movement will need.

The costing professional's first discipline is to refuse to price guesses. Where the enquiry is silent, the professional asks before quoting, because a quotation built on assumed cargo figures is the first and most common pricing error, and it converts the quoting stage into a dispute in waiting.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Costing the job: from cost families to cost price
BODY:
Behind every selling price sits an estimate of what the service will cost the provider. The cost price is the amount incurred in obtaining or delivering a service before adding a selling markup, and building it honestly is the heart of quotation preparation. Lesson 10.1, Components of Total Logistics Cost, supplied the map of cost families; the costing professional now estimates each family for the specific job.

1. Transportation. The line-haul the provider must buy or perform: ocean or air freight with its surcharges, or road transport, priced from the carrier offers actually available, not from memory.

2. Handling. Collection from the customer's premises, consolidation, terminal and warehouse handling, and delivery at destination, each estimated against the cargo's real weight, volume, and handling needs.

3. Documentation and administration. The paperwork the movement requires and the office effort of managing it: bookings, declarations where the scope includes them, permits, and communication across the movement. Lesson 10.1 showed that administration scales with the number of orders and documents, so a recurring programme carries it every month.

4. Time-based risk. Lesson 10.3, Storage, Detention, Demurrage, and Other Charges, showed how waiting becomes invoiced once free time expires. A quotation whose scope touches ports, terminals, or border crossings carries a realistic allowance for that exposure: an estimate of the delay risk the job realistically generates, priced as a provision rather than ignored. Omitting the tail does not remove it; it transfers the tail onto the provider's own account.

5. Own overhead. Lesson 10.1 distinguished direct costs, attributable to one job, from indirect costs shared across the operation. Rent, salaries, systems, and insurance are indirect, and cost allocation spreads a reasoned share of them onto each job. A quotation built from direct costs alone prices the truck and forgets the depot that dispatched it.

The sum of these five estimates is the cost price: the floor below which the provider would be working at a loss.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Cost price, selling price, markup, and profit margin
BODY:
Four terms describe the money in every quotation, and professionals keep them exactly apart.

The cost price is the amount incurred in obtaining or delivering a service before adding a selling markup, built in the way the previous concept described.

The selling price is the amount charged to a customer for the product or service: the figure that appears on the quotation.

The markup is the amount added to cost to determine a selling price, commonly expressed as a percentage of cost. A markup of 15 per cent on a cost price of 100 GHS adds 15 GHS and produces a selling price of 115 GHS.

The profit margin is the proportion of selling revenue remaining after specified costs are deducted. In the same example, 15 GHS remains out of 115 GHS of selling revenue, so the margin is 15 divided by 115, approximately 13 per cent.

Markup and margin therefore describe the same money measured on different bases: markup on cost price, margin on selling price. Because the bases differ, the percentages differ, and the same deal can truthfully be called a 15 per cent markup or an approximately 13 per cent margin. Confusing the two is a classic pricing error with real consequences. A manager who promises a 20 per cent margin but calculates it as a 20 per cent markup on cost delivers only about 17 per cent of true margin, and the shortfall repeats on every job priced that way.

No industry rule fixes what the markup should be. Each business sets its own percentage per service, per market, and per risk, as a commercial decision informed by its costs, its competitors, and the value of what it delivers. Every figure used in this lesson is illustrative for teaching purposes; no percentage here is offered as an industry norm.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Pricing rules: cost-plus and market-based selling prices
BODY:
With cost price and markup in hand, the selling price follows a stated pricing rule. Two families of rule dominate logistics pricing.

1. Cost-plus pricing. The selling price is the cost price plus a markup calculated on it, so the selling price equals the cost price plus the cost price multiplied by the markup percentage. Cost-plus pricing recovers the provider's costs by construction, which makes it the natural rule wherever the cost build is reliable. Its weakness is inherited directly from that build: if the cost estimate guessed the cargo figures, forgot the overhead share, or ignored the time-based tail, the rule faithfully marks up an error.

2. Market-based pricing. The selling price is anchored to what the market charges and what the service is worth to the buyer, and then tested against the cost price as a floor. Providers read the market differently at different volumes and horizons: a spot rate is a one-off price at the current market, while a contract rate is an agreed rate for a committed volume or period, often indexed to fuel or other moving inputs. Most providers blend the two across their business.

In practice the two rules meet in a discipline: the cost price sets the floor, the market pressures the ceiling, and the markup is the provider's commercial decision in between. An example of the calculation: a project move costs 4,000 GHS in total to deliver, the company's pricing rule adds a 15 per cent markup, so the selling price is 4,000 + (4,000 × 0.15) = 4,600 GHS, quoted subject to the validity and conditions described in the next concepts. All figures are illustrative.

A quotation below cost price is not generosity; it is a forecast loss. The professional checks every selling price against the cost build before the document leaves the office.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The service scope: inclusions, exclusions, and assumptions
BODY:
A price without a scope is a number without a meaning. The service scope is the activities and responsibilities included in a proposed service, and stating it precisely is what turns a quotation from a price list into a professional document. Three instruments do the work.

1. Inclusions. The services and charges expressly covered by the quotation: collection from a named point, carriage between named points, handling, the documentation the scope includes, and delivery to a named point. Inclusions answer the buyer's first question: what am I paying for?

2. Exclusions. The services, charges, or responsibilities not covered by the quotation: government duties and taxes, cargo insurance, storage beyond stated allowances, and charges arising after free time expires. Exclusions answer the buyer's sharper question: what is not covered, and who will carry it? An exclusion is not a gap or an evasion; stated in writing, it is information, and it is the buyer's protection against discovering the boundary of the price only after the invoice arrives.

3. Assumptions. The facts on whose truth the price depends: the cargo figures, such as three pallets of 900 kilograms each; the readiness dates; the regularity of a programme; the absence of special handling needs. Assumptions convert uncertainty into stated conditions. If the actual cargo turns out to be six pallets, the quotation has not been cheated; its assumptions no longer hold, and the professional path is a revised quotation, not an argument.

Lesson 10.3 showed how delay charges turn into disputes when nobody established in advance who was responsible for what, and how expensive such disputes are to fight. The quotation is where that establishment happens. This is why professionals describe the quotation as contract-like: it records what is offered, on what scope, at what validity, and under what assumptions, and the discipline of writing assumptions and exclusions down is precisely the discipline that prevents tomorrow's disputes. A professional would rather lose a deal to a clearly stated exclusion than win it on a silence.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Quotation validity and commercial terms
BODY:
A quotation prices a service using inputs, carrier rates, fuel, handling tariffs, and exchange rates, that move. Quotation validity is the period during which a commercial offer remains effective under its stated conditions, and it exists because the provider can promise its price only while its inputs hold. Validity protects both sides. The provider is not bound indefinitely to a price the market has left behind, and the buyer knows exactly how long the offered figures may be relied on. Lesson 5.2 introduced validity as an expiry on the offer; the costing view adds that validity is also a pricing instrument: the provider prices against the market of the validity window, and the buyer must match that window to the reality of its own decision-making. A quotation that expires before the buyer's approval process finishes is worth nothing, however attractive its figures.

Around the validity sit the commercial terms: the conditions governing pricing, payment, service delivery, and related obligations. The recurring elements include the following.

1. Payment terms: when invoices fall due and on what events, such as before carriage, on delivery, or within an agreed number of days after invoice.

2. Currency and rate basis: which currency the quotation uses, and who bears exchange-rate movement within or beyond the validity period.

3. Liability and conditions of service: the terms under which the service is performed, often by reference to the provider's standard trading conditions.

Commercial terms are not decoration. They determine what the quoted figure costs the buyer in practice: a low price payable before carriage, in a currency the buyer must purchase at an uncertain rate, can prove dearer than a higher price on settled terms. Lesson 10.1's warning applies here in commercial form: compare the whole offer, not the headline line.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The components of a professional freight quotation
BODY:
The essential components of a professional freight quotation assemble the concepts so far into one document. A complete quotation contains the following.

1. Parties and reference. Who is quoting, to whom, the enquiry it answers, and the date of issue, which starts the validity clock.

2. Scope statement. The route and service level in plain terms: named origin point, named destination point, door-to-door or port-to-port, and the Incoterms 2020 basis where an international sale is involved.

3. Itemised charges. A line for each cost family: carriage, handling, documentation, and the other charges the scope includes. Itemisation mirrors the provider's cost build and allows the buyer to compare offers line by line; a single opaque total forces the buyer to evaluate a mystery.

4. Total and currency. The sum of the lines, in a stated currency.

5. Validity period. The date until which the offer stands, stated explicitly.

6. Payment terms. When and how payment falls due.

7. Inclusions. The services and charges covered, summarised so the scope can be read at a glance.

8. Exclusions. The charges and responsibilities not covered, listed explicitly.

9. Assumptions. The cargo figures, dates, and conditions on which the price depends.

10. Conditions. The commercial terms and trading conditions that govern performance.

The list is deliberately unglamorous, and its value is that every later disagreement has a written home. If the cargo figures change, the assumptions paragraph governs. If free time runs out, the exclusions paragraph governs. If rates move, the validity paragraph governs. The worked examples of this lesson build a quotation with exactly these components for a cross-border programme, and then apply a buyer's checklist to two quotations that differ precisely in how many of these components they contain. Components are not paperwork around the price; they are the price made evaluable.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Evaluating a quotation: normalise the scope, check the clock, test completeness
BODY:
Quotation evaluation is the assessment of a commercial offer against cost, service, risk, and operational requirements. Lesson 5.2 taught the beginning of the skill, reading beyond a headline price; the costing professional extends it into a checklist that can be applied to any offer, line by line. Rate comparison, the evaluation of competing prices and commercial conditions, begins only after three preparatory steps.

1. Normalise the scope. Offers are comparable only when they describe the same service. A door-to-door quotation and a port-to-port quotation differ by whole legs: collection at origin, terminal and documentation charges at both ends, and delivery at destination, the very lines Lesson 7.4, Shipping Charges, Freight Rates, and Surcharges, took apart. Before comparing figures, restate each offer on the same scope: what must be added to the narrow offer to equal the wide one, and what the wide one already contains. Only equalised totals are prices for the same thing.

2. Check validity against the decision timeline. Note each offer's validity and set it against how long the buying decision will actually take. A quotation that lapses before the order is placed is worth nothing, whatever its headline; and an offer valid for ten days quietly demands a decision the buyer may not be able to make.

3. Test completeness. Read every offer for what it does not say. Is insurance included or excluded? Destination terminal handling? Customs fees and agency charges? Storage if the consignee is slow to collect? Each silence is one of two things: an exclusion waiting to be confirmed, or a hidden cost that will surface as an invoice after booking. The professional converts every silence into a written question before comparing prices, because hidden-cost risk hides exactly where the document is quiet. The habit Lesson 10.2, Freight Costing and Landed-Cost Calculations, enforced on landed-cost builds, that a build which omits lines flatters the decision, applies equally to a quotation under evaluation.

Only when two offers have been normalised to one scope, confirmed live for the decision window, and completed to the same visible content do their numbers describe the same purchase.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Beyond the price lines: the offerer, sensitivity, and the total-cost view
BODY:
Three further steps complete the checklist.

4. Assess the offerer. A quotation is also a sample of its provider: whether the document is precise or vague, whether assumptions are stated or smuggled, whether the provider asked questions before pricing. Beyond the paper, the professional weighs capability and reliability: can this provider perform the stated scope, with the equipment, network, agency arrangements, and references to show it? For a recurring service, the instrument is the service-level agreement, an agreement defining specified service expectations, performance measures, and responsibilities. Its typical components, scope of services, key performance indicators with targets, measurement and reporting cadence, remedies or service credits, governance and review, and exit terms, convert promises into measurable commitments. One line forward: the full discipline of selecting and contracting a provider belongs to Module 15, Strategic Logistics Management; at the quotation stage the professional's job is to weigh the evidence the offer itself provides.

5. Test sensitivity. Ask of every line what happens if reality moves. If volumes fall below the assumptions, does the price survive? If readiness slips, which time-based charges from Lesson 10.3 come alive, and on whose account? If the market moves, spot lines reprice at renewal while contract lines hold to their terms, which is why the spot-versus-contract structure of an offer is itself an allocation of risk between the parties.

6. Apply the total-cost view. Lesson 10.1's lens closes the checklist: the cheapest quotation is an input, not a conclusion. Transit time, reliability, service coverage, and commercial risk are part of what the money buys, and this lesson's criteria, total cost, reliability, transit time, service coverage, and commercial risk, are weighed together rather than one at a time.

The checklist also exposes the recurring pricing errors made on the provider's side of the desk: quoting on guessed cargo figures; omitting the overhead share; confusing markup with margin; pricing from a rate base that has since expired; leaving the time-based tail unpriced; and trusting an unstated exclusion to remain unstated. Every one of them is cheaper to fix before the quotation is issued than after it is accepted.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The negotiation moment: clarify, negotiate openly, document
BODY:
Between receiving a quotation and accepting one lies the negotiation moment, and the professional handles it in three motions.

First, clarify before you bargain. Questions about scope, assumptions, and completeness are not negotiation; they establish what is actually being priced, and providers expect them. Ask before you bargain: a discount negotiated on a misunderstood scope is an agreement to disagree. The buyer who has normalised the scope and tested completeness, the checklist's first three steps, knows exactly which silences need answering and which differences between offers are real.

Second, negotiate openly and reprice scope, not silence. Where the buyer genuinely needs something the quotation excludes, insurance, storage flexibility, a wider cargo allowance, the professional path is to ask for it and accept a revised price for a revised scope. Unpriced assumptions are tomorrow's invoices: anything the buyer persuades itself is probably included will arrive later as a charge nobody budgeted, with none of the negotiating leverage that an open request would have had. The same discipline binds the provider. Absorb an unstated assumption quietly and the cost is real; requote and the cost becomes a number the customer can accept or refuse.

Third, document the accepted scope. The purchase order or booking that follows acceptance should mirror the quotation: same scope, same figures, same assumptions, same validity terms. When the documents mirror each other, later invoices can be checked against an agreed standard mechanically, and the procurement habit of matching order, service received, and invoice before payment becomes routine rather than forensic. Where circumstances change after acceptance, the change is handled in writing, as a revised quotation or a written amendment, never as a conversation both parties later remember differently. In logistics pricing, the memory that counts is the one on paper.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Building the quotation: a monthly Accra to Lomé consolidation programme
BODY:
A manufacturer of packaged food products in Accra ships a regular monthly consignment to its distributor in Lomé, Togo, and asks a freight forwarder in Accra to quote for the programme: collection from the factory, consolidated road carriage to Lomé on the coastal corridor that links Ghana with Togo, and delivery to the distributor's warehouse. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: Fix the facts of the enquiry. Three pallets per month, 900 kilograms each, standard stackable pallets, non-dangerous goods, collected in one pick-up each month in an agreed week, delivered door to door, recurring for the life of the offer. The costing officer confirms each point with the client before pricing: the entire cost build hangs on them.

Step 2: Cost each family for one month. Transport, the allocated share of the consolidated line-haul: 2,700 GHS, that is 900 GHS per pallet. Handling, collection, consolidation handling, and delivery in Lomé: 900 GHS. Documentation for the cross-border movement: 600 GHS. Time-based risk, an allowance for border and terminal waiting on a road programme: 300 GHS. Overhead allocation, a share of the depot, salaries, and systems: 600 GHS. Total cost price: 5,100 GHS per month, or 1,700 GHS per pallet.

Step 3: Apply the pricing rule. The company's rule for this lane is a 15 per cent markup on cost: 5,100 × 0.15 = 765 GHS, giving a selling price of 5,865 GHS per month, or 1,955 GHS per pallet. This is a 15 per cent markup but only about a 13 per cent margin: 765 of every 5,865 GHS invoiced.

Step 4: Write the commercial layer. Validity: 30 days from the date of issue. Payment terms: invoiced monthly, payable within 14 days. Inclusions: collection in Accra, consolidation, road carriage, cross-border documentation, and delivery in Lomé. Exclusions: import duties and taxes in Togo and import clearance at destination, which remain the consignee's responsibility; cargo insurance, quoted separately on request; storage at destination beyond two working days. Assumptions: three pallets per month at up to 900 kilograms each, stackable and non-dangerous, ready in the agreed collection weeks; quantities outside these figures will be requoted.

Step 5: Assemble the document, stating the parties and date of issue, the door-to-door scope, the monthly charge of 5,865 GHS, the validity, the payment terms, and the inclusions, exclusions, and assumptions, each in its own paragraph.

What does this example demonstrate?

Every figure on the finished document traces back to a costing decision, and every condition protects the offer from a future argument. Key lesson: a professional quotation is a cost build plus a commercial layer, and the commercial layer is what makes the document an offer rather than a number.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Evaluating two quotations for one project move, Mumbai
BODY:
A manufacturer in Mumbai must import a production line, twelve crates totalling 18 tonnes, from a supplier near Shanghai, discharged at Mumbai and delivered to the factory. Two freight forwarders return quotations. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Forwarder A's quotation is itemised. Ocean freight including surcharges: 2,600 US dollars. Origin handling and documentation: 350 US dollars. Destination terminal handling: 450 US dollars. Destination documentation and agency fee: 300 US dollars. Inland delivery, port to factory: 500 US dollars. Total: 4,200 US dollars. Validity: 30 days. Exclusions, stated: customs duty and taxes, cargo insurance, and any detention or demurrage beyond the free time allowed. Assumptions, stated: twelve crates, 18 tonnes, ready for collection in week six.

Forwarder B's quotation is one line: door-to-door delivery, 3,900 US dollars, all-inclusive. Validity: 10 days. No exclusions, no assumptions, no cargo basis.

The buyer applies the checklist.

Step 1: Normalise the scope. A's total covers the port-to-port movement plus named destination lines; B claims door-to-door but does not say whether destination terminal handling and destination documentation sit inside its total. Until B answers, the two prices are not prices for the same service.

Step 2: Check the clocks. The buyer's capital approval takes about three weeks. B's offer expires in ten days, before the decision can be made; A's thirty days cover it.

Step 3: Test completeness. A names duty, insurance, and free-time excess as exclusions; B is silent, and silence is not inclusion. If B's unstated destination lines merely cost the same as A's comparable lines, an illustrative supposition, B's true comparable total is 3,900 plus 750 US dollars, about 4,650, some 450 US dollars above A, and the buyer discovers this only after booking.

Step 4: Assess the offerers. A asked for the cargo list, stated its assumptions, and attached references from similar projects; B returned a price and nothing else.

Step 5: Test sensitivity. If the crates exceed 18 tonnes, B's price has no stated basis on which to be repriced, while A's assumptions make the position testable.

Step 6: Weigh total cost. A's 4,200 US dollars is transparent and live for the decision window; B's 3,900 is a headline attached to an unevaluable offer.

What does this example demonstrate?

The vague quotation need not be dishonest, but it is unevaluable, and an unevaluable offer transfers all of its risk to the buyer. Key lesson: apply the checklist before the prices; prices become comparable only when the offers describe the same service on the same stated conditions.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: The validity miss: six weeks against a 14-day offer
BODY:
An importer in Accra buys general merchandise overseas and asks its freight forwarder for a quotation for the next consolidated shipment. The forwarder prices the job on the rates then available and issues a quotation valid for 14 days. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

The quotation reaches the importer's managing director just before he travels. Approval waits for his return, then for the finance meeting, then for the overseas supplier's own confirmation. Six weeks after issue, the importer writes: we accept your quotation, please proceed.

The forwarder now holds an acceptance of an offer that expired more than four weeks earlier. In the meantime the market has moved: the shipping line serving the lane has announced a general rate increase, an across-the-board upward adjustment of base freight rates, and the forwarder's own input costs now exceed the quotation. The quoted price no longer describes a service the forwarder can buy at a profit.

Who bears the movement? The quotation's own terms answer the question: the offer stood for 14 days and then lapsed. The importer cannot insist on a price whose validity it allowed to pass, and the forwarder is not obliged to perform at a stated loss, although honouring a lapsed quotation as a gesture to a valued customer remains the forwarder's commercial choice to make, not its obligation.

How does the professional handle it? The forwarder responds promptly and in writing: it explains that the offer has expired, shows the movement in its inputs, and issues a fresh quotation at the current market. The importer now has real choices: accept the new figures, exercise rate comparison and seek alternative offers, or discuss a contract-rate arrangement, an agreed rate for a committed volume over a period, which would buy stability for a recurring programme at the price of a commitment. What the parties do not do is what they would most regret: the forwarder silently honouring a loss it cannot afford, or silently repricing the old document without saying so.

What does this example demonstrate?

Both parties could have prevented the moment. The importer could have matched its approval timeline to the validity, or asked for an extension before expiry; the forwarder could have flagged that rates on the lane were due to move. Key lesson: validity periods protect both sides, and the professional honours them or renegotiates openly, in that order.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: A quotation is an offer, not a contract of carriage
BODY:
A misconception follows quotations out of the classroom into real trading: that accepting a quotation is the same thing as having a contract of carriage, or that a quotation binds the provider until the customer releases it. Neither is true, and the difference matters operationally.

A logistics quotation is a commercial offer: a statement of proposed charges and conditions for specified services. It becomes binding through acceptance on its terms, and its terms include its validity period and its assumptions. Accept the offer within validity, on the stated basis, and the parties have a commercial agreement for that scope at that price. Accept it after validity, or on a different basis, and there is nothing yet to bind: the offer has lapsed, and the acceptance is really a new negotiation.

The contract of carriage is a separate instrument. It arises from the booking and is evidenced by the transport document, which Module 5 treated in detail; the quotation is neither of these. That is also why a quotation can be honest, complete, and professional, and still cease to be available: its inputs moved, its window closed, and the offer, unlike a carriage already performed, simply ended.

The practical rules follow. Providers honour their validity periods or renegotiate openly before them. Buyers accept within validity or ask for a fresh offer. Neither side treats a lapsed quotation as a grievance. The document that binds is the one both parties accepted on the day.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Never quote or accept on unstated assumptions
BODY:
The sentence worth carrying out of this lesson is short: unpriced assumptions are tomorrow's invoices. It applies on both sides of the quotation.

The provider who quotes on guessed cargo figures, an assumed readiness date, or an unexamined belief that no special requirements apply has not avoided the risk; the provider has bought it. The goods arrive heavier than assumed, the collection slips into a rate change, the cargo needs lifting equipment nobody priced, and each discovery arrives as a choice between absorbing a loss and starting a dispute with a new customer. Every one of these outcomes was visible at the enquiry stage, which is why the first discipline of quotation preparation is to ask before pricing and to write the answers down as assumptions.

The buyer runs the same risk in reverse. Accepting a quotation whose assumptions are hidden, whose exclusions are silent, and whose validity does not match the decision timeline means accepting obligations that no one has priced. Lesson 10.3's disputes were born in silences of exactly this kind; the quotation is where they are strangled, by an inclusions list, an exclusions list, and an assumptions list that the buyer has read and tested rather than merely filed.

Scope changes after acceptance are normal and legitimate. Cargo grows, dates move, programmes expand. The professional response is never silence in either direction: it is a revised quotation or a written amendment, so that the accepted scope and the invoiced scope remain the same document. In logistics pricing, what is written down is what was agreed; everything else is a forecast of an argument.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A costing officer at a freight forwarder in Accra builds a cost price of 8,000 GHS for a project move. The company's pricing rule adds a 25 per cent markup on cost to determine the selling price. All figures are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Which option best describes the figures that should appear in the quotation?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. A selling price of 10,000 GHS, which the quotation may describe as earning a profit margin of 25 per cent of the selling price.
2. A selling price of 10,000 GHS, which earns a profit margin of 20 per cent of the selling price, because markup is calculated on cost price while margin is measured against the selling price.
3. A selling price of approximately 10,667 GHS, because a 25 per cent requirement must always be applied by dividing the cost price by 0.75.
4. A selling price of 8,000 GHS, because the markup is the provider's internal profit and is never included in the price shown to the customer.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The markup rule works on the cost price: 25 per cent of 8,000 GHS is 2,000 GHS, so the selling price is 10,000 GHS. The profit margin is then measured on the selling price: 2,000 GHS remaining out of 10,000 GHS invoiced is 20 per cent. Markup and margin describe the same money on different bases, so the two percentages differ, and a professional document keeps them distinct.

Answer 1 restates the classic confusion this lesson dismantles: a 25 per cent markup on cost is a 20 per cent margin on selling price, not a 25 per cent one. Answer 3 applies a different pricing rule; dividing the cost price by 0.75 is how a 25 per cent margin on the selling price would be built, which is not the company's markup rule. Answer 4 denies the purpose of the markup: the selling price exists to recover the cost and earn the margin, so it must include the markup.

The takeaway: markup lives on the cost price, margin lives on the selling price, and the two figures are both true only when each is measured on its own base.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
A buyer in Kumasi receives two quotations for the same import shipment. The first quotes 5,200 US dollars, fully itemised, with its exclusions listed and a validity of 30 days. The second quotes 4,750 US dollars as an all-in price, lists no exclusions, and is valid for 10 days. The buyer's internal approval will take about four weeks. All figures are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Which option best explains the professional conclusion?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The all-in quotation is the better commercial choice, because a single inclusive price transfers every risk of the movement to the provider.
2. The all-in quotation cannot yet be compared with the itemised one, because its scope is unverified, its silences may conceal charges, and its validity expires before the buyer's decision can be made.
3. The two quotations are equally valuable, because the buyer can accept the all-in price whenever the internal approval finishes, regardless of its stated validity.
4. The itemised quotation is automatically the more reliable offer, because any provider who itemises charges always operates better than one who does not.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The checklist of this lesson is applied before the prices are compared. The all-in offer has not stated what its price covers, so the places where destination charges, insurance, or customs fees would sit remain untested; an unstated scope may hide costs that surface as invoices after booking. Its validity of 10 days also expires long before the buyer's four-week approval concludes, so even its headline figure may no longer be available when the decision is made. Until the scope is normalised and the validity is extended, the lower number describes an unevaluable offer.

Answer 1 fails because silence transfers risk to the buyer rather than away from them: an all-in price with no exclusions stated means nobody knows what happens when an unlisted charge appears. Answer 3 ignores quotation validity: an offer lapses at the end of its stated period, and accepting afterwards begins a new negotiation instead of conferring a right. Answer 4 over-generalises; itemisation is encouraging evidence of transparency in this scenario, but the checklist weighs scope, validity, completeness, the offerer, sensitivity, and total cost together rather than awarding the decision to a document style.

The takeaway: normalise the scope, check the clock, and test completeness before letting headline prices compete.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
An exporter in Kumasi receives a freight quotation for six pallets of shea butter shipped to a customer overseas, valid for 14 days. Its board approves the shipment in week six and writes to accept the original figures. In the meantime, the costs underlying the quotation have risen, and the forwarder can no longer deliver the quoted scope at the quoted price.

Which statement best describes the position?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The forwarder must honour the quotation, because a quotation remains binding on the provider until the customer decides to release it.
2. The exporter owes a late-acceptance penalty to the forwarder, calculated from the day the quotation expired.
3. The offer lapsed at the end of its validity, so the exporter cannot insist on the original price; the professional path is a fresh quotation and open renegotiation, which protects both sides.
4. A telephone call accepting the quotation during the first week would have fixed the price permanently, so the exporter should have accepted by phone and organised the shipment later.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

Quotation validity is the period during which the offer remains effective under its stated conditions, and it protects both sides: the provider is not bound indefinitely to a price its market has left, and the buyer knows exactly how long the figures may be relied on. When the exporter allowed the 14 days to pass, the offer ended, and a later acceptance cannot revive it. The forwarder may choose to honour the old figures as a commercial gesture, but that is a choice, not an obligation; the professional handling is to say so promptly, explain the movement in inputs, and issue a fresh quotation, leaving the exporter free to accept it, compare alternative offers, or discuss a contract-rate arrangement for stability.

Answer 1 reverses the nature of the document: a quotation is an offer with its own terms, not an open-ended commitment that only the customer can end. Answer 2 invents a rule that does not exist; the consequence of a lapse is renegotiation, not a penalty. Answer 4 misreads validity in the opposite direction: an acceptance made within validity creates an agreement on the quoted terms for that service, not a permanent price fix against future market movement.

The takeaway: professionals match validity to their decision timelines, and where an offer lapses, the remedy is honest repricing, not grievance.
