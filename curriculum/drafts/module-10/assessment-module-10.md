=== ASSESSMENT Q1 | tests lesson 10.1 ===
QUESTION:
A distributor of imported ceramic tableware in Takoradi buys its packaging as sets, one carton with inner crating per set, from Supplier P at 18 GHS a set. A rival supplier, Q, offers equivalent-looking sets at 14 GHS. The distributor ships 1,000 cartons a month and switches to Q. Three months later, the share of cartons arriving damaged has risen from about one in a hundred to about four in a hundred, and each damaged carton generates about 350 GHS of replacement transport, re-handling, credit notes and claims administration. All figures are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Which option best evaluates the switch?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The switch raised total logistics cost: packaging is a damage-prevention cost, and the switch's tail, roughly thirty extra damaged cartons a month at about 350 GHS each, some 10,500 GHS, outweighs the 4,000 GHS monthly saving by about 6,500 GHS.
2. The switch lowered total logistics cost, because returns, re-shipments and claims are estimates rather than invoiced amounts, and only invoiced amounts enter the total.
3. The two effects cannot be compared, because packaging is a direct cost of each consignment while damage losses are indirect costs of the operation as a whole.
4. The switch is justified, because the per-carton packaging price fell and unit cost is the measure that matters; breakage is a customer-service matter outside the costing decision.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

The lesson teaches that packaging expenditure is not only a materials cost but a damage-prevention cost, and that skimping on it usually shows up later in the failure families. The arithmetic runs through the whole lens. The switch saved 4 GHS a set across 1,000 sets a month, some 4,000 GHS. The damage rate rose by about three cartons in every hundred, some thirty extra damaged cartons a month, each carrying about 350 GHS of replacement transport, re-handling, credit notes and claims administration, some 10,500 GHS a month. The visible saving of 4,000 is outweighed by the invisible tail of 10,500, so the switch makes the operation dearer by about 6,500 GHS a month, before counting customers who quietly reduce their orders after repeated breakages.

Answer 2 applies the misconception the module exists to dismantle: failure costs are real money whether or not a single invoice names them, scattered as they are across transport, wages and write-offs. Answer 3 uses classification as an evasion; the direct and indirect labels describe attribution, not comparability, and total-cost thinking exists precisely to compare effects across families. Answer 4 optimises the single visible line and waves the tail away as a service matter; the fallacy of optimising one line is exactly what the cheaper-looking per-carton price conceals.

The takeaway: before signing for the cheaper input, price the tail the choice makes likely and compare the whole effect on the total, not the movement of one line.

=== ASSESSMENT Q2 | tests lesson 10.1 ===
QUESTION:
A distributor of consumer electronics in Accra normally buys 500 units a month from its overseas supplier at 1,000 GHS a unit. The supplier offers a five per cent price reduction if the distributor takes 3,000 units in one six-month bulk purchase at 950 GHS a unit, paid upfront. The purchasing manager presents the 150,000 GHS discount as pure saving. The finance officer estimates that carrying the much larger stock for the longer period costs about 190,000 GHS in capital tied up, insurance, obsolescence as newer models arrive, and shrinkage. All figures are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Which statement best evaluates the offer?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The offer is free money: the goods are simply bought earlier, the company's warehouse already exists, and no invoice charges the company for owning its own stock.
2. The offer raises total cost: the 150,000 GHS discount is real, but the holding side is real too, and at about 190,000 GHS it outweighs the discount by some 40,000 GHS, even though no invoice will ever name that figure.
3. Holding cost applies only while goods wait in a public warehouse under a storage tariff; stock in the company's own building costs nothing beyond the rent already paid.
4. The only holding cost relevant here is the warehouse rent, which does not change, so the discount stands at the full 150,000 GHS.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Order size is a trade-off, not a free win: the third classic trade-off of Lesson 10.1 balances ordering savings against carrying cost. The discount side is real, 50 GHS less on each of 3,000 units, some 150,000 GHS. The carrying side is assembled from equally real components: the additional capital tied up in slow-moving stock that cannot fund anything else, insurance on goods sitting for months, obsolescence as newer models arrive, and shrinkage. The finance officer prices that side at about 190,000 GHS, so the deal is about 40,000 GHS dearer in total. Nothing about that conclusion requires an invoice addressed to the stock: inventory holding cost is the standing proof that money can be fully spent without a document naming it.

Answer 1 is the argument Lesson 10.1's own knowledge check dismantles: goods already paid for are capital already committed, which is the cost, not the absence of one. Answer 3 confuses the inventory holding family with the facility tariff charges treated in Lesson 10.3; holding cost accrues in the company's own building from the first day. Answer 4 mistakes the boundary between families: an unchanged rent means the warehousing family is unchanged, while the holding family, driven by value and time held, has grown with every extra unit on the shelf.

The takeaway: compare a purchasing saving against the whole holding tail it creates, and treat the assembled holding cost as a first-class figure in the decision.

=== ASSESSMENT Q3 | tests lesson 10.1 ===
QUESTION:
A finance officer at a distribution company in Tamale must classify four warehouse costs: casual discharge labour hired by the tonne as each consignment arrives; special crating built for one out-of-gauge machine; monthly lease instalments on the forklift fleet; and the annual licence for the warehouse's management software.

Which option best classifies them?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. All four are indirect costs, because each of them supports the warehouse operation as a whole rather than any single consignment.
2. The forklift lease and the software licence are direct costs because invoices arrive for them every month, while the discharge labour and the crating are fixed costs because they arise only occasionally.
3. The discharge labour and the special crating are direct costs, the labour varying with the tonnage handled, while the forklift lease and the software licence are indirect costs that remain largely unchanged within the company's current operating range.
4. All four are variable costs, because each of them could be reduced or cancelled if the company's activity fell.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The two sortings ask different questions. Direct versus indirect asks whether the expense attaches to one specific shipment, service or activity: the discharge labour is hired for named consignments as they arrive and the crating is built for one machine, so both are direct; the forklift fleet and the management software serve every pallet in the building and can only be shared across the operation through cost allocation, so both are indirect. Fixed versus variable asks whether the expense moves with the level of activity: labour hired by the tonne rises and falls with throughput, while lease instalments and an annual licence remain relatively unchanged within the relevant operating range. The crating sits with the labour on the direct side even though it arises only occasionally, because occasional is not the definition of fixed; the fixed test is about staying unchanged across a period and range.

Answer 1 fails because supporting the whole operation is exactly what makes a cost indirect, and the labour and crating do not have that character. Answer 2 repeats the visibility error: an invoice arriving monthly says nothing about attributability, and frequency of invoicing is not the fixed-versus-variable test. Answer 4 stretches variable to mean cancellable; the test is whether the amount changes with activity, and the lease and licence amounts do not, within range.

The takeaway: ask of every cost both questions, attributable to what, and driven by what volume, and the two answers together place it correctly.

=== ASSESSMENT Q4 | tests lesson 10.2 ===
QUESTION:
A forwarder in Accra tenders an air consignment of five identical cartons of machine components. Each carton measures 120 by 100 by 80 centimetres and weighs 96 kilograms gross. Volumetric weight is computed at the IATA standard conversion factor of 6,000 cubic centimetres per kilogram, as taught in this module. The airline's tariff quotes an illustrative GHS 90 per chargeable kilogram for the lane. All figures are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Which option states the consignment's chargeable weight and the resulting freight charge?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. 480 kg and GHS 43,200, because air freight is charged on the gross weight that the airline's scales record.
2. 640 kg and GHS 57,600, because the chargeable weight per carton is the average of its gross weight and its volumetric weight.
3. 160 kg and GHS 14,400, because the five identical cartons are charged as a single piece on one volumetric weight.
4. 800 kg and GHS 72,000, because each carton's volumetric weight of 160 kilograms exceeds its gross weight of 96 kilograms, so every carton is charged on 160 chargeable kilograms.
CORRECT: 4
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 4.

Chargeable weight in air freight is the higher of the actual gross weight and the volumetric weight, computed piece by piece under the applicable factor. Each carton measures 120 by 100 by 80 centimetres, a volume of 960,000 cubic centimetres, which divides by 6,000 to a volumetric weight of 160 kilograms. That exceeds the 96-kilogram gross weight, so each carton is charged on 160 kilograms and the five-carton consignment on 5 x 160 = 800 chargeable kilograms. At the illustrative GHS 90 per chargeable kilogram, the freight is 800 x 90 = GHS 72,000. The pricing basis, not the scales alone, decides the number.

Answer 1 applies the dense-cargo habit to bulky cargo: gross weight governs only where it is the greater of the two, and here it is not. Answer 2 invents an averaging step that exists in no tariff; the rule takes the greater figure precisely because the airline is paid for whichever scarce resource the cargo consumes, here hold space rather than payload. Answer 3 forgets that the quotation prices a consignment: five identical pieces each carry their own chargeable weight, and charging one piece's volume against the whole shipment understates the freight by four-fifths.

The takeaway: compute volumetric weight per piece, compare it with the gross weight per piece, charge on the greater of the two for every piece, and check the conversion factor the applicable tariff actually specifies.

=== ASSESSMENT Q5 | tests lesson 10.2 ===
QUESTION:
A distributor in Cape Coast imports 400 cartons of packaged toiletries. The costing sheet lists, all figures illustrative for teaching purposes: goods value GHS 96,000; ocean freight GHS 8,400; cargo insurance GHS 1,600; import duty, shown as an illustrative assessment placeholder, GHS 9,200; combined taxes and levies, illustrative placeholders, GHS 19,600; terminal handling and port charges GHS 3,900; inland transport to the warehouse GHS 4,500; clearance and agency fees GHS 2,800; and a storage and contingency allowance of GHS 2,000. A colleague proposes deleting the allowance as only an estimate, and adding a profit line of GHS 4,000, arguing that the goods must also carry their profit.

Which option correctly states the landed cost and the evaluation of the colleague's proposal?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. GHS 146,000, or GHS 365.00 per carton, because a contingency allowance is a forecast rather than an expenditure and must wait until an invoice exists.
2. GHS 152,000, or GHS 380.00 per carton, because the profit line makes the figure honest about what the business needs to earn from the consignment.
3. GHS 148,000, or GHS 370.00 per carton; every line the buyer will really pay belongs inside the build, including a stated tail allowance, while profit is added on top when the selling price is set, never inside the cost it is meant to recover.
4. GHS 150,000, or GHS 375.00 per carton, because the colleague is right on both counts: estimates never belong in a landed cost, and profit is part of what the goods cost.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The build is added in order: 96,000 plus 8,400 plus 1,600 plus 9,200 plus 19,600 plus 3,900 plus 4,500 plus 2,800 plus 2,000 gives GHS 148,000, and dividing by 400 cartons gives the unit landed cost of GHS 370.00. The colleague's two challenges fail in opposite directions. The storage and contingency allowance belongs inside: the discipline of Lesson 10.2 is that a landed-cost model is only as honest as its tail, and an allowance for the unknown, stated next to the total, is information rather than error; deleting it forecasts a best case and presents it as a measurement. The profit line belongs outside: landed cost answers what the goods cost to place ready for sale, and profit is added on top when the selling price is set. Building profit into the very cost the price must recover destroys the figure's meaning as the floor beneath pricing.

Answers 1 and 4 both act on the deletion of the allowance and so understate the build. Answers 2 and 4 both admit the profit line and so convert a cost measure into an aspiration. Note also what the landed cost is not: the customs value on which the duty was computed is a legally determined base, and the landed total carries destination and tail lines the assessment never shows.

The takeaway: every line the buyer will really pay belongs in the build, the stated tail included, and nothing that is not a cost does.

=== ASSESSMENT Q6 | tests lesson 10.2 ===
QUESTION:
An importer in Accra buys machinery spare parts valued at 20,000 US dollars on FOB terms and builds the landed cost at an assumed illustrative rate of 12 cedis to the dollar, pricing the goods line at GHS 240,000 and setting selling prices from the finished build. The ocean freight and a fuel surcharge are also quoted in US dollars. The parts arrive and are paid for three months later, when the assumed illustrative rate is 13 cedis to the dollar. All figures are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Which statement best describes what the currency movement has done to the landed cost, and the professional response?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Every dollar line converts at the rate ruling when payment is made: at 13 rather than 12 cedis to the dollar the goods line alone costs some 20,000 GHS more and the dollar-quoted freight rises proportionally, while locally quoted cedi lines do not move, so the build, which is a snapshot dated to its rate, must be re-run before the selling prices are relied on.
2. Nothing has changed, because the invoice still reads 20,000 US dollars and the landed cost was fixed on the day the order was placed.
3. Only the duty and tax lines can change, because government charges respond to currency movement while commercial prices are fixed by contract.
4. The importer should now price from the customs value instead of the landed cost, because currency movement makes landed-cost builds unreliable.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

A landed-cost build is a snapshot dated to the rate it used. Every foreign-currency line converts at the rate ruling when the money is actually paid, not when the order was placed. At 13 cedis to the dollar instead of 12, the goods line alone rises by one cedi on each of 20,000 dollars, some GHS 20,000, and the dollar-quoted freight and fuel surcharge rise proportionally with the same movement. The locally quoted cedi lines, such as terminal handling and inland transport, do not move. The build made at order time therefore no longer describes the cost, which is why the lesson's disciplines are to date every conversion, to re-run the build when the rate moves, and to budget with a stated planning rate, all before the selling prices derived from the old snapshot are relied on.

Answer 2 commits the snapshot error: the invoice's dollar amount is unchanged, but the cedi cost of those dollars is no longer the number in the build. Answer 3 inverts the exposure; currency movement touches the commercial lines first, while government charges follow their own legally defined bases on the day of assessment. Answer 4 reaches for the wrong instrument altogether: the customs value answers the legal question of what duty is computed on, and pricing from it understates the true cost exactly as Lesson 10.2's trainee error showed.

The takeaway: a build is alive only for the day of its rate; re-run it before each price review and each reorder.

=== ASSESSMENT Q7 | tests lesson 10.3 ===
QUESTION:
A consignee's container is discharged at Tema and made available for collection. For illustration, the booking allows five free days for collection, and the terminal's tariff grants four free storage days from the same availability. The container is collected on the ninth day after availability. Seven days after gate-out, the empty container is returned to the shipping line's depot, against a permitted return allowance of three days. In the following weeks the consignee receives invoices from both the shipping line and the terminal. All periods are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Which statement correctly accounts for the charges?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. One of the two invoices must be erroneous, because a single container can incur only one time-based charge for any given day.
2. The terminal's charge is a duplicate of the line's demurrage, because storage and demurrage are two names for the same terminal-time charge.
3. The line bills demurrage for the four days the container stood in the terminal past its collection allowance, and detention for the four days the empty was retained past the return allowance; the terminal separately bills storage for the occupied days beyond its own shorter allowance, because demurrage and detention are the carrier's charges for its equipment and time while storage is the terminal's charge for its space.
4. No charge can apply after collection, because collecting the container from the terminal ends every free-time clock on the shipment.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The journey runs through three charges with two creditors. Inside the terminal, the container stood four days past the carrier's five-day collection allowance, so the line bills demurrage for those chargeable days. The same visit also crossed the terminal's own shorter four-day storage allowance, so the terminal bills storage under its own tariff for the occupied days beyond it, a different organisation pricing a different resource, the space rather than the equipment's time inside the gate. After gate-out, the empty was retained seven days against a three-day return allowance, so the line bills detention for the four days its equipment was held outside the terminal. Demurrage and detention can legitimately arrive from the same creditor for different legs, and storage from the second creditor alongside the first, which is why a delayed container can owe several charges for the same week.

Answer 1 is the double-billing suspicion the lesson corrects: the charges stack because they price different resources, and several invoices for one container can all be valid. Answer 2 merges the terminal's charge into the line's; storage is the facility's own tariff charge, not a second name for demurrage. Answer 4 assumes collection ends every clock; collection ends the demurrage leg and begins the detention leg, which ran until the empty's return.

The takeaway: place each time-based charge by its clock, where the container stood, under whose allowance, and whose tariff, and expect legitimate invoices to stack.

=== ASSESSMENT Q8 | tests lesson 10.3 ===
QUESTION:
An importer's booking at Tema carries, for illustration, a free-time allowance of six days and a progressive demurrage tariff: chargeable days one to five at GHS 250 per day, chargeable days six to ten at GHS 600 per day, and every chargeable day from the eleventh at GHS 1,000 per day. The container is collected on the tenth chargeable day. All figures are illustrative for teaching purposes.

What demurrage charge does the tariff produce?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. GHS 4,250: five chargeable days at GHS 250 plus five chargeable days at GHS 600.
2. GHS 2,500, because all ten chargeable days are priced at the first band's rate.
3. GHS 6,000, because all ten chargeable days are priced at the second band's rate.
4. GHS 3,650, because the day of collection is never billed, so only nine chargeable days are charged.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

A progressive tariff prices each chargeable day from the band in which that day falls, and the reconstruction runs band by band. Chargeable days one to five sit in the first band: five days at GHS 250, which is GHS 1,250. Chargeable days six to ten sit in the second band: five days at GHS 600, which is GHS 3,000. The container was collected on the tenth chargeable day, so the third band is never reached, and the total is GHS 1,250 plus GHS 3,000 = GHS 4,250. Every chargeable day in the period is billed, including the day of collection, which a tariff's part-day convention commonly makes fully billable even when the truck arrives part-way through it.

Answer 2 applies the first band's rate to days that have already moved into the second band, and Answer 3 applies the second band to all ten days, including the five that belong to the first; both flatten a stepped structure into a single rate, and both change the total by real money. Answer 4 waives the collection day, which no tariff does: the chargeable period runs until collection, and dropping its day understates the charge by one full billable day. All four misreadings share one root, trusting a headline rate instead of rebuilding the charge from the tariff's bands, which is also how incorrect invoices slip past untrained eyes.

The takeaway: reconstruct every time-based charge band by band and day by day before accepting the figure, and audit the invoice against that reconstruction.

=== ASSESSMENT Q9 | tests lesson 10.3 ===
QUESTION:
An importer's consignment requires a regulatory permit that is still in process. Three working days before the last free day, the clearing agent reports that the approval will be late and that collection will overshoot the free-time allowance by about a week.

Which response reflects the discipline this module teaches?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Take no action yet: on a well-run shipment the expected time-based charges are zero and the shipment budget already says so, so it is too early to involve the shipping line.
2. Escalate immediately and ask the line, before the charges accrue, for an extension of free time, in writing, with the reason and a realistic new collection date; before accrual this is a routine commercial request, while the same request after accrual is an argument about money the tariff already says is owed.
3. Wait for the demurrage invoice and then dispute it in writing, because charges caused by a public regulator's delay are automatically withdrawn on request.
4. Refuse any demurrage invoice in advance on the ground that free time cannot lawfully expire while a government permit is outstanding.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The lesson's asymmetry is the whole answer: time-based charges are negotiable only before they accrue. With three working days before the last free day and a foreseeable overshoot of about a week, the escalation should leave the importer's office at once, as a written extension request to the line carrying the credible reason, a realistic new collection date, and the shipment's history of prompt collections behind it. Made then, it is the routine commercial conversation that lines handle every day, and it can hold the exposure near zero. Made after accrual, the identical request becomes an argument about money the tariff already says is owed, conducted with a creditor that holds the records and owes the importer nothing.

Answer 1 twists the expected-zero budget into an excuse for passivity; the zero is a prediction with a process behind it, and the process at this moment is the early request, not silence. Answer 3 waits for the invoice and then assumes an automatic waiver for regulator-caused delay; no such rule exists, and disputes succeed on evidence about the charge itself, not sympathy about its cause. Answer 4 invents a legal suspension of free time that no tariff contains, and converts a solvable delay into a certain dispute that damages credibility.

The takeaway: the cheap day to solve a foreseeable delay is the day before the last free day; once the clock starts, only the invoice's price is left to argue about.

=== ASSESSMENT Q10 | tests lesson 10.4 ===
QUESTION:
A freight forwarder in Takoradi has cost-priced a project move at GHS 4,000. The commercial manager promises the board a profit margin of 20 per cent of the selling price. A colleague therefore quotes GHS 4,800, adding 20 per cent to the cost price. All figures are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Which option best evaluates the quotation?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The quotation is correct, because a 20 per cent markup on cost and a 20 per cent margin on the selling price are the same instruction expressed in different words.
2. The quotation overprices the job: a margin is already provided for inside a properly built cost price, so the correct quotation is GHS 4,000.
3. The quotation under-delivers the promise: GHS 4,800 carries only 800 GHS of margin, about 17 per cent of the selling price, while the promised margin requires a selling price of 4,000 divided by 0.80, which is GHS 5,000, on which 1,000 GHS is exactly 20 per cent.
4. Either figure is acceptable, because markup and margin are two names for the same percentage and the board will read them interchangeably.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

Margin and markup measure the same money on different bases: markup on the cost price, margin on the selling price. A promised margin of 20 per cent of the selling price means the 4,000-cedi cost price must form 80 per cent of the quotation, so the selling price is 4,000 divided by 0.80 = GHS 5,000, on which 1,000 GHS of margin is exactly 20 per cent. The colleague's GHS 4,800 adds a 20 per cent markup, 800 GHS, which leaves a margin of 800 out of 4,800, about 17 per cent of the selling price. The shortfall is small on one job and structural across a programme, because it repeats on every price built the same way, which is precisely how promised margins quietly fail to arrive.

Answer 1 states the confusion verbatim: the two percentages coincide only at zero, and nowhere else. Answer 2 denies that the quotation must carry the margin at all; the cost price is the floor below which the provider works at a loss, and the margin is added above it, never hidden inside it. Answer 4's equivalence is false for the same reason Answer 1 is, and a board promised a margin and shown a markup has been promised one number and shown another.

The takeaway: markup lives on the cost price, margin lives on the selling price, and a promise stated in one must be computed in the same one.

=== ASSESSMENT Q11 | tests lesson 10.4 ===
QUESTION:
An importer in Accra receives two quotations for the same machinery shipment from Shanghai, each totalling GHS 48,000. For illustration, the first covers port-to-port carriage to Tema only and is valid for 21 days; the second covers door-to-door delivery to the importer's Accra warehouse and states that it includes destination terminal handling, customs clearance fees, and inland delivery, and is valid for 14 days. The importer's decision will take about ten days. All figures are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Which option best describes the professional comparison?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The two are equally acceptable, because the totals are equal and both validity periods outlast the decision.
2. Equal totals do not describe equal purchases: the first offer omits destination terminal handling, clearance and inland legs that the second includes, so on a normalised door-to-door scope the first is effectively the dearer offer, and the second's stated inclusions should be confirmed in writing before acceptance.
3. The first is automatically the better offer, because a port-to-port scope in which the buyer arranges destination services always costs less than having a forwarder include them.
4. The second's 14-day validity makes it the riskier offer regardless of scope, so the first should be chosen.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The evaluation checklist runs before the prices: normalise the scope, check the clock, test completeness. The clock is satisfied here, since both validity periods comfortably cover a ten-day decision. The scope is not: the first offer ends at Tema, leaving destination terminal handling, customs clearance fees and inland delivery to be arranged and paid separately, while the second claims those legs inside its total. Until the missing legs of the first offer are priced and added, the two 48,000-cedi figures describe different purchases, and on a normalised door-to-door basis the first is effectively the dearer offer by whatever its omitted legs cost. The second's claimed inclusions should then be converted from prose into confirmed written lines before acceptance, because silence and assertion are both tested in the same step.

Answer 1 compares headlines, and equal totals over unequal scopes are the classic case the normalisation step exists to catch. Answer 3 generalises from a habit to a rule; arranging destination services oneself is sometimes cheaper and sometimes dearer, and the word always is doing work that only pricing the missing legs can do. Answer 4 lets validity override scope; an offer that is live but narrow is the risky one, and a wide offer with adequate validity is not endangered by a longer validity elsewhere.

The takeaway: only offers normalised to the same scope, live for the decision window, and complete to the same visible content have prices that describe the same purchase.

=== ASSESSMENT Q12 | tests lesson 10.4 ===
QUESTION:
An exporter in Kumasi receives a logistics quotation for a consolidated shipment, valid for 30 days. Internal approvals delay the decision, and the exporter writes to accept the quoted figures on day 45. In the meantime, freight rates on the lane have fallen, and the forwarder's current input costs are now below the quoted price. All dates and figures are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Which statement best describes the position?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The exporter can insist on the original figures, because a quotation binds the provider until the customer chooses to release it.
2. The original price applies, because with the forwarder's costs lower, performing at the quoted price has become profitable, and profitability is what determines whether an offer can be enforced.
3. The acceptance relates back to the day of issue, so a binding agreement was formed on the day the quotation was written and its price governs.
4. The offer lapsed at the end of its validity whichever way the market moved; the forwarder responds promptly in writing, issues a fresh quotation at current inputs, and the price that governs is the one in the offer the parties actually accept, since honouring the original figures would be a commercial gesture rather than an obligation.
CORRECT: 4
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 4.

Quotation validity is the period during which the offer remains effective under its stated conditions, and it protects both sides by defining exactly how long the figures may be relied on. At day 45 the offer had been dead for a fortnight, and lapse does not ask which way the market moved: the offer's inputs changed and its window closed, so it simply ended. What follows is method, not grievance. The forwarder replies promptly and in writing, explains the position, and issues a fresh quotation at current inputs, here likely a lower one, and the price that governs is the one in the offer the parties actually accept. Honouring the original figures remains the forwarder's commercial choice, a gesture to a valued customer, never an obligation.

Answer 1 reverses the nature of the document: a quotation is an offer with its own terms, not a commitment that only the customer can release. Answer 2 makes profitability the source of obligation; profitability may make the forwarder willing, but only an offer alive within its window makes a price enforceable, and the two ideas must not be merged. Answer 3 invents a relation-back rule; acceptance binds an offer that is still alive, and cannot reach back past its own expiry to revive it.

The takeaway: match approval timelines to validity, or ask for an extension before expiry; where an offer lapses, the remedy is a fresh quotation openly accepted, in either market direction.
