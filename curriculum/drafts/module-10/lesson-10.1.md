# DRAFT — Lesson 10.1: Components of Total Logistics Cost

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Total logistics cost: a lens, not an invoice
BODY:
Courses 1 to 3 treated logistics as an operational discipline: how goods are transported, warehoused, handled through ports, documented, and sold across borders under trade terms. Module 10 now attaches money to those operations, and this opening lesson supplies the map of where that money goes.

Total logistics cost is the combined expenditure associated with planning, transporting, handling, storing, managing, and distributing goods.

The first thing to understand is that total logistics cost is not a document anyone receives. No invoice arrives labelled with that title. The freight forwarder invoices freight, the terminal invoices handling, the clearing agent invoices its fee, the landlord invoices rent, the insurer invoices its premium, and the finance department records the money tied up in stock. Total logistics cost is what you see when you gather all of those separate lines into one view. It is an accounting lens: a deliberate way of looking at the business that collects every cost incurred because goods must move, wait, be handled, be cleared through borders, and be served to customers.

The lens matters because businesses make decisions with it. Prices must be set high enough to recover the full cost of getting goods to customers, not merely the freight. Profitability depends on costs that are actually understood rather than partly seen. And service promises are never free: every commitment about speed, availability, and condition is paid for somewhere in the cost structure. An organisation that watches only its freight invoices will understate what its logistics truly costs, and it will make decisions that look excellent on one line and expensive in total.

This lesson therefore does four things. It names the cost families that make up the total. It sorts costs into direct and indirect, and into fixed and variable. It teaches total-cost thinking, the discipline of judging a decision by its effect on the whole rather than on one line. And it shows who inside a business actually owns these costs, which is rarely one person. The next lesson, Freight Costing and Landed-Cost Calculations, builds on this map to calculate landed cost properly.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The movement and storage families: transportation, warehousing, and cargo handling costs
BODY:
The first three cost families pay for the physical work of logistics, the work every earlier module described operationally.

1. Transportation cost. Transportation cost is the expense incurred in moving goods between locations. It covers the long international legs, ocean freight with its surcharges and air freight, and the domestic distribution legs that carry goods from ports and warehouses to customers' premises. Lesson 7.4, Shipping Charges, Freight Rates, and Surcharges, took a freight invoice apart into base freight, surcharges, and local charges; under the total-cost lens, all of those lines belong to one family, and so does the fuel and maintenance of a business's own delivery fleet. Whether carriage is bought from carriers or performed in-house, the cost of movement enters the same family.

2. Warehousing cost. Warehousing cost is the expense associated with operating storage facilities and handling goods within them. It includes the space itself, whether paid as rent or borne as the cost of owning a building, and the labour of receiving, put-away, picking, packing, and dispatch that Module 8 described as the warehouse's daily work. Equipment such as forklifts and racking, utilities, security, and the systems that record stock movements all belong to this family.

3. Cargo handling cost. Cargo handling cost is the expense incurred in loading, unloading, lifting, moving, and securing cargo. It arises at every interface: cranes and terminal equipment at a port, stevedoring at a bulk berth, forklift work at a warehouse dock, lifting arrangements at a customer's premises for heavy items. Sometimes handling is invoiced separately, as when a carrier charges a terminal handling charge; sometimes it is embedded inside a wider charge or performed by the business's own staff. The lens counts it either way.

These three families are the visible backbone of logistics expenditure. They are also the lines managers argue about most, which is why the lesson later asks whether the visible backbone is where the real decisions are won.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Holding stock and running the paperwork: inventory holding and administrative costs
BODY:
The next two families are quieter than freight and rent, and one of them never appears on an invoice at all.

1. Inventory holding cost. Inventory holding cost, also called inventory carrying cost, is the cost of maintaining inventory over time, including capital, storage, insurance, and obsolescence expenses. Its elements deserve to be named separately. Capital cost is the money tied up in the goods themselves: cash converted into stock is cash that cannot repay loans, fund other purchases, or earn returns elsewhere. Insurance is the premium that protects the stock while it sits. Obsolescence and deterioration are the losses that accumulate as goods age out of fashion, pass expiry dates, corrode, or otherwise lose value with time. Shrinkage is the gradual disappearance of stock through theft, damage, or record errors. Note the boundary with the previous family: warehousing cost pays for the facility, while inventory holding cost is largely the cost of the stock sitting inside it, and it grows with the value of the goods and the time they are held. These are different families even though they share a building.

2. Order processing and administration. Every order raised, booking confirmed, customs declaration prepared, and invoice matched consumes administrative effort: communication with suppliers, carriers, agents, and customers; data entry and record keeping; and the systems that support them. Each single transaction is inexpensive, but administration scales with the number of orders, shipments, and documents an operation generates, so an operation built on many small orders carries a proportionally heavier administrative burden than one built on few large ones.

Professionals learn to distrust the quiet families. Transportation and warehousing announce themselves on invoices; holding and administration must be sought out, which is exactly why businesses that never look for them systematically underestimate their total logistics cost.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Crossing borders, protecting goods, and the costs of failure
BODY:
The remaining families complete the picture, and two of them concern failure rather than flow.

1. Customs and trade-compliance costs. For cross-border trade, duties, taxes, and levies are a genuine cost of trading, imposed by law and driven by how goods are classified. In Ghana, an import assessment consists of import duty plus additional taxes and levies, all computed when ICUMS, the country's single-window e-customs platform, assesses the declaration, with payment made at a participating bank. This lesson teaches the categories and their behaviour, never amounts; the closing note of this lesson returns to that rule and its reasons. Alongside the government charges sit brokerage and agency fees: the professional fee of the licensed clearing agent who lodges the declaration and arranges duty payment, and, depending on the goods, the cost of permits and certificates from regulatory agencies such as the Food and Drugs Authority for food and medicines or the Ghana Standards Authority for standards conformity.

2. Packaging and protecting goods. Protective packaging, pallets, crating for machinery, and labelling all cost money, and so do the insurance premiums that protect cargo in transit. Packaging expenditure is not only a materials cost; it is a damage-prevention cost, and skimping on it usually shows up later in the failure families below.

3. Quality failure costs. Damaged goods, lost goods, returns, re-shipments, and the administration of claims are logistics costs caused by things going wrong. Reverse logistics, the flow of goods back toward their origin, is part of logistics rather than an exception to it, and it consumes transport, handling, and administrative effort every time it runs.

4. Stockout costs. The final family is the cost of not having stock when a customer wants it: sales lost to competitors, customers lost to disappointment, production stopped for want of materials, and expensive expediting to recover. No invoice records a stockout, but the lesson treats it seriously precisely because it is a real, sometimes severe, cost that hides completely.

A complete cost picture therefore spans flow, storage, administration, compliance, protection, and failure. Missing any family distorts every decision built on the picture.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Direct and indirect costs
BODY:
The first great sorting of logistics costs asks a simple question about each expense: can it be attached to one specific shipment, service, or activity?

A direct cost is an expense that can be specifically attributed to a particular shipment, service, or activity. The ocean freight on a named consignment is direct: it belongs to that consignment and no other. So are the fuel consumed on one delivery run, the clearing fee charged for one declaration, and the special crating bought for one machine.

An indirect cost is an expense shared across multiple activities that cannot be directly attributed to a single shipment without allocation. The warehouse manager's salary serves every pallet in the building, not one order. The rent, the facility insurance, the telephone system, and the general software licences support the operation as a whole. No document will ever tell you which consignment caused them.

Cost allocation is the process of assigning shared expenses to specific operations, customers, or services. Because indirect costs are real, businesses that want to know what a product, a customer, or a delivery lane truly costs must spread the shared expenses across them using reasoned bases: floor space occupied, weight or volume carried, number of orders or lines handled, or hours of equipment used. Allocation is never perfect, and different reasonable bases give different answers, which is why allocation choices should be stated openly rather than buried.

The distinction matters operationally. Direct costs answer questions about individual shipments: what did this consignment cost to deliver? Indirect costs answer questions about the operation as a whole: what does our logistics infrastructure cost per period, and how should that burden be shared? A quotation built only from direct costs will underprice, because it ignores the share of the warehouse roof that every order uses. A manager who treats an indirect cost as if it were direct, and charges it wholly to one unlucky customer, will misread that customer's profitability. The next lesson's landed-cost arithmetic works with direct costs; the total-cost lens needs both kinds.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Fixed and variable costs
BODY:
The second sorting asks a different question about each expense: does it change when the level of activity changes?

A fixed cost is an expense that remains relatively unchanged within a relevant operating range over a specified period. Warehouse rent is fixed: the same amount falls due whether the warehouse ships one hundred orders a month or a thousand, provided the operation stays within the range the facility can handle. Salaried supervisors, equipment lease instalments, and software subscriptions behave the same way. The qualifier matters: fixed costs are fixed within a relevant range and period. Outgrow the warehouse and the rent steps up; the term is relative, not absolute.

A variable cost is an expense that changes according to the level of activity or service provided. Fuel rises with kilometres driven, casual handling labour rises with tonnes moved, packaging materials rise with orders dispatched, and carrier charges rise with shipments booked. At zero activity most variable costs fall toward zero; fixed costs remain.

Many real costs are mixed. A warehouse combines fixed rent with variable handling labour; a delivery fleet combines fixed vehicle finance with variable fuel and maintenance; a clearing agent's invoice may combine a fixed per-declaration fee with disbursements that vary with the shipment. Experienced managers therefore ask not whether a cost is fixed or variable in principle, but what drives it and over what range.

The sorting earns its keep in decision-making. Fixed costs reward utilisation: once the rent is paid, every additional pallet through the same building lowers the fixed cost carried by each pallet, so capacity that sits idle is capacity quietly bleeding. Variable costs follow volume decisions: every extra order adds its own incremental cost, which is why the marginal order, not the average, should be compared against the marginal revenue. And the distinction feeds the trade-off thinking later in this lesson, because many logistics decisions are precisely exchanges of fixed for variable cost, or of one variable cost for another.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Cost drivers and the classic trade-offs of total-cost thinking
BODY:
A cost driver is a factor that influences the amount of expenditure incurred by a logistics activity. Distance drives transport cost; time held and value drive inventory holding cost; order count drives administration; errors drive failure costs. Total-cost thinking is the discipline of identifying the drivers behind each family before optimising any of them, because the drivers are connected: pull one down and another rises.

The fallacy this discipline dismantles is optimising a single line. The cheapest transport offer can mean slower transit, which means more stock in transit and on the shelf, more capital tied up, and more expediting when demand outruns the slower pipeline. The cheapest warehouse can mean careless picking, which means more returns, more claims, and more re-shipping. In both cases the saving is real but the cost it triggers elsewhere is larger, and the business that compares only the invoice lines will congratulate itself into losses.

Three classic trade-offs recur throughout logistics.

1. Transport cost versus inventory cost. Faster, dearer transport shortens the pipeline and reduces the stock that must be held to cover the time goods spend moving and waiting; slower, cheaper transport does the reverse. Neither mode is right in itself; the choice depends on the value of the goods, the demand pattern, and what stockouts would cost.

2. Warehouse count versus delivery cost. More, smaller warehouses place stock closer to customers and shorten the final delivery legs, improving service and cutting outbound transport; but they multiply facilities, duplicate stock in several places, and raise total holding cost. Fewer, larger warehouses do the opposite. Network design is this trade-off made concrete.

3. Order size versus carrying cost. Ordering in large lots keeps ordering and administrative effort low but holds average stock high, raising holding cost; ordering in small lots does the reverse. The classic economic order quantity model balances the two pressures to find the order size that minimises their combined total, and its logic, in words, is exactly this balancing act. Treat it as a model, not a law: it assumes steady demand and simple costs, and Lesson 8.3, Inventory Control, FIFO, and FEFO, has already supplied the reorder-point and safety-stock ideas with which it works in practice.

The lesson of all three trade-offs is the same: the question is never which line can be made smallest, but which combination of lines can be made smallest together without harming service.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Visible and hidden costs: the invoice and the tail
BODY:
Sort the same cost families by how they announce themselves and a second discipline emerges.

Visible costs are the ones that arrive on documents: the freight invoice, the warehouse rent, the clearing fee, the insurance premium. They are easy to see, easy to budget, and therefore the lines that attract nearly all managerial attention.

Hidden costs are the ones embedded in operations without a dedicated document. Dwell time, the hours and days cargo waits in yards and terminals, consumes space and equipment and the importer's patience while appearing on no invoice until it overflows into charges. Demurrage, detention, and terminal storage, introduced in Module 9's treatment of port storage and to be calculated in this module's Lesson 10.3, Storage, Detention, Demurrage, and Other Charges, are dwell time that has finally become invoiced, usually after the expensive part of the waiting has already happened. Re-work and re-handling consume labour twice for one order. Expediting turns a planning failure into premium transport. Claims administration pays office salaries to process what damage caused. None of these appears in the accounts under its own name; they scatter across wages, fuel, and write-offs.

This is why landed-cost thinking must include the tail. Landed cost is the full cost of placing goods at their final destination: purchase price, international freight, insurance, duties, taxes and levies, local charges, and inland transport, together with the tail of delay and failure costs that the journey realistically generates. A landed cost built only from quoted lines is a forecast of the best case, not a measurement of the operation. Professionals add the realistic tail: the probable waiting, the occasional expediting, the share of shipments that will be damaged and re-served.

The next lesson formalises the landed-cost calculation line by line. The work of this lesson is the habit that makes the calculation honest: after listing what will certainly be invoiced, ask what else this decision makes likely, and price that likelihood too.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Who owns logistics cost
BODY:
If total logistics cost is a lens, who is supposed to look through it? In most businesses, no one person does, and that is precisely the management problem.

Procurement controls how goods are bought: the agreed Incoterms 2020 rule in the sales contract decides who arranges and pays for carriage, insurance, and clearance, so the buyer's logistics cost is partly fixed before its logistics department is ever consulted. The warehouse team owns space, labour, and equipment spending. The transport team owns fleet, fuel, and carrier purchases. Finance owns the money tied up in inventory, the insurance arrangements, and the payment of duties and levies, and it sees the aggregate bills that nobody else views whole. Sales, meanwhile, makes service promises whose costs land in all four of the other functions.

Each function therefore sees one slice of the total, and each can optimise its slice at the others' expense. Procurement saves money by buying in huge lots; the warehouse drowns in stock and the holding cost rises. Transport chooses the cheapest, slowest carrier; expediting and stock costs rise somewhere no transport manager is measured on. A business that evaluates each function only on the costs it directly controls will systematically reward decisions that raise the total.

Assembling a true total-cost view therefore requires coordination: shared figures, agreed allocations, and decisions taken across functions rather than within them. Cost allocation, introduced earlier in this lesson, is the practical tool, because shared expenses must be spread before anyone can see what a customer, product, or lane truly costs. The full strategic treatment of coordinating logistics decisions, and of choosing between doing the work in-house and buying it from service providers, belongs to Lesson 15.1, Logistics Strategy and Service-Provider Selection; here, the point is the diagnosis. When a total refuses to fall although every line manager insists their line has been cut, look for the boundary between functions: the saving is usually being paid for in the department next door.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Reducing cost without damaging service: profitability and customer satisfaction
BODY:
Reducing logistics expenditure without compromising service quality is a legitimate goal, and the cost families point to where it is honestly found.

1. Raise utilisation. Fill vehicles and containers properly, consolidate small loads, and keep warehouse capacity working. Higher utilisation spreads fixed costs across more output, which lowers unit cost without touching service.

2. Cut avoidable waiting. Fast, accurate documentation, early declaration lodgement, and disciplined coordination with terminals keep cargo inside its free time and out of the delay charges. Waiting removed is cost removed, and service usually improves in the same motion.

3. Right-size inventory. Better forecasting, disciplined reorder points, and order sizes chosen with holding cost in view reduce the capital, insurance, and obsolescence burden. Lesson 8.3's stock-control tools are the operating instruments of this lever.

4. Reduce errors. Accurate picking, careful handling, and sound packaging cut the returns, claims, and re-shipments of the failure families. Quality spending is often the cheapest cost reduction available, because every error prevented removes several downstream costs at once.

5. Buy intelligently. Blending contracted rates for predictable volumes with spot purchases for peaks, and comparing offers on total cost rather than headline rate, disciplines the largest single family without a single operational change.

What all five levers share is that they lower cost while preserving or improving what the customer experiences. A cut that slows deliveries, thins availability, or increases damage is not a saving; it is a transfer of cost to the customer, and it returns as lost revenue.

That return path is the connection to profitability. Operating margin is the proportion of revenue remaining after deducting operating expenses, calculated according to the applicable accounting method, and logistics cost sits directly inside that deduction: every cedi or dollar removed from avoidable logistics cost flows toward the margin. Customer satisfaction works on the other side of the account. Reliable, accurate, available service retains customers and generates repeat revenue; service failures quietly drain it. Logistics cost and customer service are therefore not opponents to be balanced once, but a pair to be managed continuously: the purpose of the total-cost lens is to find the money that is being spent without buying either efficiency or service.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: One machinery consignment, many cost lines: a Kumasi importer's total-cost picture
BODY:
A machinery dealer in Kumasi imports one consignment of industrial equipment, shipped by sea and discharged at Tema, one of Ghana's two seaports, which are managed by the Ghana Ports and Harbours Authority. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes; they show how a total-cost picture is assembled, not what any real shipment costs.

Step 1: Fix the frame. The agreed Incoterms 2020 rule in the sales contract determines which of the coming lines fall to the importer's account. Here the rule leaves carriage, insurance, and import clearance to the buyer, so every line below belongs to the Kumasi dealer's total-cost picture.

Step 2: The movement lines. Ocean freight on the equipment, including fuel-related and other surcharges, is invoiced at an illustrative 2,400 US dollars. Cargo insurance for the transit leg costs an illustrative 150 US dollars.

Step 3: The compliance lines. The dealer's licensed clearing agent lodges the declaration in ICUMS. The system assesses import duty plus additional taxes and levies, which for this illustration total 18,000 GHS, paid at a participating bank. The agent's professional fee is 3,000 GHS. These amounts are purely illustrative: real amounts depend on tariff classification and the law in force, and this course never teaches figures for them.

Step 4: The local lines. Terminal handling, documentation, and other local charges at the port come to an illustrative 4,500 GHS, and road carriage from Tema to Kumasi adds an illustrative 6,500 GHS.

Step 5: The tail, deliberately zero. Demurrage, detention, and terminal storage for this consignment are zero, and the zero is a result, not luck. The agent lodged documents early, the assessment was paid promptly, and the container left the terminal within the free time allowed by the carrier and terminal. On a consignment where clearance had drifted past that point, the same lines could have carried substantial charges; here, fast clearance kept the entire tail at zero.

Step 6: The invisible lines. No invoice names them, but the picture is not complete without them: the money tied up in the duty payment and in equipment awaiting sale, insurance on the goods in store, and the dealer's staff time across the transaction.

What does this example demonstrate?

The freight invoice, 2,400 US dollars, was the number the dealer's accountant would once have called the logistics cost. Assembled through the lens, the picture holds five families, two currencies, one deliberately zero tail, and a set of invisible lines. Key lesson: a total-cost picture for one import is a ledger assembled from many documents and a few honest estimates, and the assembler who stops at the freight invoice has read perhaps a fifth of the book.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Air versus sea for spare parts: reading the trade-off honestly
BODY:
A service company in Singapore maintains industrial equipment for customers across the region and sources spare parts from a supplier in Hamburg. Management must choose between replenishing them by sea and by air. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: Price the two pipelines. By sea, freight plus surcharges and local charges at both ends cost an illustrative 400 US dollars per shipment, with a door-to-door lead time of several weeks. By air, the equivalent charge is an illustrative 1,800 US dollars per shipment, arriving within days. On the freight invoice alone, air is more than four times dearer, and the meeting proposes sea.

Step 2: Trace what lead time does to stock. Lesson 8.3 taught that a reorder point must cover demand expected during the lead time, plus safety stock against variability. A several-week pipeline therefore forces the company to hold substantially more stock in Singapore than a days-long one: more parts on the shelf while the next shipment sails.

Step 3: Price the extra stock. Holding that larger buffer costs money every cycle: capital tied up in parts, insurance, and, for parts that age or are superseded, obsolescence. For an illustration, suppose the sea pipeline's extra buffer adds 2,000 US dollars of holding cost per replenishment cycle across the part range, against 300 US dollars for the air pipeline. The sea option's apparent 1,400 US dollars of freight saving per cycle now competes against 1,700 US dollars of additional holding cost per cycle, and the arithmetic has changed sides.

Step 4: Price the stockout exposure. When a critical part fails at a customer's site, the company expedites regardless of policy, paying premium freight and risking the relationship. A short pipeline shrinks that exposure; a long one enlarges it, and the value of the difference depends on how often failures occur and what each costs, a question about the company's own history, not freight tariffs.

Step 5: Decide per part, not per policy. The honest conclusion is mixed. For heavy, low-value, predictably consumed parts, the sea pipeline's holding penalty is small and sea wins. For high-value critical parts, the air premium is small against the holding cost and stockout exposure it avoids, and air wins. A split policy, sea for the routine bulk and air for critical or high-value items, is often the true minimum.

What does this example demonstrate?

The freight comparison, four times dearer by air, was not the deciding comparison. Key lesson: transport decisions are inventory decisions in disguise; compare the total cost of each pipeline, including the stock it forces you to hold and the stockouts it permits, and let different parts ride different pipelines.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: The cheap warehouse and the error tail
BODY:
A distributor of fast-moving consumer goods in Accra outgrows its warehouse and compares two facilities. Warehouse A quotes a rent of 19,000 GHS per month; warehouse B quotes 23,000 GHS per month but operates with tighter picking discipline and better scanning. Management chooses A for its 4,000 GHS monthly saving. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: Establish the error rates. In this illustration, warehouse A mis-picks three order lines in every hundred; warehouse B mis-picks half a line in every hundred. The distributor processes 2,000 order lines a month. Warehouse A therefore generates about 60 errors monthly, warehouse B about ten: fifty extra errors every month, purely illustrative rates for a purely illustrative pair of facilities.

Step 2: Price one error's tail. Each picking error triggers a small chain: a re-delivery or return journey, say 300 GHS of transport; re-picking, re-packing, and claims handling, say 200 GHS of labour and administration; and, when goods are wrong or late, a credit note or replacement that erodes the month's margin. At an illustrative 500 GHS of direct tail per error, fifty extra errors cost about 25,000 GHS per month.

Step 3: Compare the totals. Warehouse A's rent saving was 4,000 GHS per month. Its error tail costs roughly 25,000 GHS per month. The cheap warehouse is the expensive one by around 21,000 GHS per month, before counting the quiet losses: customers who quietly reduce their orders after repeated wrong deliveries, and staff time spent apologising rather than selling. Those losses are real but never appear under the heading "warehouse error cost", which is why the cheap option survived the original comparison.

Step 4: Ask why the comparison failed. The rent was visible, invoiced, and easy to compare; the error tail was hidden, scattered across transport, wages, and lost revenue, and attached to no single budget. The distributor compared the only two numbers it could see and mistook them for the whole decision.

What does this example demonstrate?

Quality failure costs are the classic hidden family: small per error, frequent in aggregate, and invisible in the accounts. Key lesson: before signing for the cheaper facility, price the tail, the returns, claims, re-ships, and eroded trust that its error rate makes likely, and compare the rent saving against the whole of what the saving buys.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: The cheapest invoice is not the cheapest logistics
BODY:
The misconception this lesson has been dismantling throughout deserves one final, blunt statement: the cheapest invoice is not the cheapest logistics. A decision that lowers one cost line can raise the total, because the lines are connected through the cost drivers. Cheaper transport usually means slower transport, and slower transport means more inventory holding cost and more stockout exposure. A cheaper warehouse usually means a less careful warehouse, and carelessness means returns, claims, and re-shipping. A larger order size means fewer ordering efforts but more money sleeping on shelves.

The corrective is not a rule of thumb but a habit of comparison: when an offer lowers a visible line, ask which other lines this offer changes, find where they are recorded or estimate them honestly, and compare the offer's full effect on the total against the alternative's full effect. Sometimes the cheap option survives that comparison intact, because the hidden lines genuinely do not move. The discipline is not that cheap is wrong; it is that cheap must be proven against the total, not declared against the line. A saving that costs more elsewhere than it saves where you are looking is not a saving at all, whatever the invoice says.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Inventory carrying cost is real money even though no invoice names it
BODY:
Learners trained on invoices tend to believe that a cost exists only when a document charges it. Inventory holding cost is the standing refutation. When stock sits in a warehouse, no invoice arrives for holding it, yet real expenditure accumulates continuously: the capital tied up in the goods is money not repaying loans or funding the business; insurance premiums protect it; obsolescence and deterioration quietly reduce its value; shrinkage removes pieces of it. All of this is genuine cost, and on high-value stock held for a long time it can rival the freight that delivered the goods.

The reason the cost escapes notice is bookkeeping, not economics. Its elements are scattered: financing cost inside finance charges, insurance inside insurance premiums, deterioration inside write-offs, shrinkage inside stock discrepancies. Nothing in the accounts aggregates them under the name "holding cost", so a manager reading the accounts sees only fragments. The professional response is to assemble the fragments deliberately, as this lesson's worked examples do, and to treat the assembled figure as a first-class cost in every decision about order sizes, stock levels, and transport speed. Money does not need an invoice to be spent; it needs only to be committed, and holding stock commits it.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Duty and levy amounts are a matter of classification and current law: this lesson teaches categories, never figures
BODY:
Throughout this lesson, customs charges have been named as categories and never as amounts, and the restraint is absolute, not stylistic. Duty, tax, and levy amounts depend on how goods are classified in the tariff, on the value customs accepts, and on the law in force at the time of assessment, all of which change. In Ghana, the assessment is computed when ICUMS processes the declaration, and its composition follows the tariff treatment of the specific goods on the specific day. Any figure memorised from a lesson would be wrong somewhere, soon, and expensively.

The professional habits this lesson can teach are the ones that do not expire: know which family a charge belongs to; know that the licensed clearing agent lodges the declaration and the assessment is paid against the bill the system generates; know that classification and valuation drive the amounts, which is why both are treated as serious technical work elsewhere in this course; and know that the landed-cost method, built in the next lesson, always takes its duty and levy figures from the live assessment, never from memory. When you need an amount, you consult the current tariff position for the goods or the assessment itself. When you teach or explain, you name the categories and point to the source. That is the discipline of a cost professional, and it begins here.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A purchasing manager at a manufacturing company in Takoradi selects the lowest ocean freight quote for its monthly raw-material imports. The cheaper service has a noticeably longer transit time. Three months later, the finance officer reports that overall logistics spending has risen, driven by higher warehouse stock levels and occasional urgent air shipments arranged to cover production shortages.

Which option best explains why total logistics cost rose even though the freight line was reduced?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The cheaper, slower service lengthened the lead time, so the company had to hold more inventory to cover the longer pipeline and pay for expediting when demand outran stock; those increases outweighed the freight saving.
2. The finance officer has double-counted, because total logistics cost is the sum of the freight invoices and the freight invoices fell.
3. Warehouse stock levels rose because the cheaper carrier must have damaged more cargo, and damaged cargo always increases inventory.
4. Ocean freight quotes are inherently unreliable, so choosing the lowest offer is always a mistake regardless of transit time or inventory effects.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

This is the classic transport-versus-inventory trade-off taught through total-cost thinking. The cheaper service's longer transit lengthened the lead time, and a longer lead time raises both the stock that must be held to cover demand during the pipeline and the exposure to stockouts, which the company experienced as urgent air shipments. The freight saving was real, but the cost it triggered in the inventory and failure families was larger, so the total rose.

Answer 2 fails because it restates the very misconception the lesson dismantles: total logistics cost is not the sum of freight invoices; it spans holding, administration, compliance, failure, and stockout costs as well. Answer 3 invents a mechanism; the report attributes the increase to higher stock levels and expediting, not to damage, and slower transit affects inventory through lead time, not through condition. Answer 4 over-generalises in the opposite direction: the cheapest offer is wrong only when its hidden effects outweigh the saving, which the discipline of comparing totals reveals case by case.

The takeaway: judge any cost reduction by its effect on the whole lens, never by the line it improves.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
A logistics manager in Accra is asked to classify four expenses: the monthly salary of the warehouse supervisor, the fuel consumed on each delivery run, the ocean freight charged for one specific consignment, and the annual rent of the warehouse building.

Which option best describes how these costs should be classified?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. All four are variable costs, because every cost changes when the business grows.
2. All four are direct costs, because all of them appear on invoices or payroll records received by the company.
3. The fuel per delivery run and the freight for the specific consignment are direct and variable, while the supervisor's salary and the warehouse rent are indirect and largely fixed within the company's current operating range.
4. The salary and rent are direct costs of the warehouse, while the fuel and freight are indirect costs shared across all shipments.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The two sortings answer different questions. Direct versus indirect asks whether the expense can be attributed to one specific shipment, service, or activity: fuel burned on one delivery run and freight invoiced for one named consignment can, while the supervisor's salary and the building's rent support the whole operation and can only be shared through cost allocation. Fixed versus variable asks whether the expense changes with activity: fuel and shipment charges rise and fall with volume, whereas salary and rent remain relatively unchanged within a relevant operating range over the period.

Answer 1 fails because salary and rent are the textbook fixed costs; growth eventually steps them up, but within the current range they do not vary. Answer 2 confuses visibility with directness: appearing on an invoice does not make a cost attributable to one shipment, which is exactly why allocation exists. Answer 4 reverses both labels, treating the shared, range-stable expenses as attributable and the attributable, activity-driven ones as shared.

The takeaway: ask two separate questions of every cost, who or what it belongs to, and whether it moves with activity; the two answers together place any expense correctly.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A distributor in Kumasi keeps a large stock of finished goods in its own warehouse. The managing director argues that the stock costs the company nothing while it sits, because the supplier has already been paid, the warehouse is already built, and no invoice arrives for goods the company owns.

Which statement best explains the flaw in this argument?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Holding costs apply only to rented warehouse space; goods a company owns and stores in its own building incur no further cost.
2. Inventory holding cost is real expenditure even without a dedicated invoice: capital tied up in the stock, insurance, obsolescence and deterioration, and shrinkage all accumulate while goods are held, even though the accounts record them only as scattered fragments.
3. The stock costs nothing provided it is sold before the end of the financial year, because holding costs are only charged after twelve months.
4. Inventory holding cost is a government charge collected through customs on goods held in warehouses after importation.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Inventory holding cost is the cost of maintaining inventory over time, including capital, storage, insurance, and obsolescence expenses. None of these requires an invoice addressed to the stock. The cash converted into goods is money unavailable for other uses; the insurance premium protects the goods; value drains away as goods age, expire, or fall out of demand; and shrinkage removes part of the quantity. The accounts scatter these fragments across finance charges, insurance premiums, write-offs, and stock discrepancies, which is precisely why a manager reading only invoices concludes the stock is free.

Answer 1 fails because ownership of the building removes the rent line only, while the capital, insurance, obsolescence, and shrinkage elements continue regardless of who owns the shelves. Answer 3 invents a rule that does not exist; holding cost accrues from the first day, not after any anniversary. Answer 4 confuses a business's internal cost of holding stock with government charges at importation, which belong to the customs family and follow the assessment, not the storage.

The takeaway: no invoice is needed for money to be spent. Committed capital and accumulating risk are costs, and the total-cost lens exists to make them visible.
