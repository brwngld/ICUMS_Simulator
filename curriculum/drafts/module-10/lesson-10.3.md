# DRAFT — Lesson 10.3: Storage, Detention, Demurrage, and Other Charges

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: From the price of movement to the price of delay
BODY:
The previous lesson, Freight Costing and Landed-Cost Calculations, answered the question every importer asks first: what will this shipment cost to bring in? It built the landed cost from freight, insurance, duties, taxes, and destination charges. That calculation prices the planned journey, the shipment behaving exactly as the quotation assumed.

This lesson prices a different thing: what the shipment can cost when the plan slips, together with the family of service charges that surrounds the movement itself. Lesson 10.1 mapped the components of total logistics cost and separated direct from indirect costs. Lesson 10.2 priced the plan. This lesson prices the tail: the charges that accrue at the end of the journey, after the vessel has berthed, when a container waits, a document fails, or equipment comes back in poor condition.

The mindset shift is the first learning. A buyer who asks only what the freight costs has priced the middle of the shipment and left both ends open. The professional question is broader: what can the tail of this shipment cost, which of those costs are within my control, and what would it cost to prevent them?

In costing language the family has two wings. The time-based charges, demurrage, detention, and storage, were defined and explained in Lesson 9.4, and this lesson refers back to those definitions rather than repeating them. Around them sits the wider family that this lesson's title calls other charges: documentation fees, cleaning and repair charges, weighing and examination-related costs, and the internal costs a delay generates inside the importer's own business.

None of these are exotic. Every one of them appears on ordinary import invoices, and every one of them is either budgeted and managed, or unbudgeted and suffered.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The three time-based charges as budget lines
BODY:
As the previous module taught, demurrage is a charge commonly applied when a container remains within a terminal or designated facility beyond the applicable free-time allowance; detention is a charge commonly applied when a container is retained outside the terminal beyond the permitted allowance; and a storage charge is a fee for keeping cargo or containers at a facility under the applicable tariff. Lesson 9.4 explained where each clock runs and when it starts. This lesson treats the same three as cost lines, and the costing view adds three distinctions the definitions alone do not show.

1. The creditor differs. Demurrage and detention are charged by the ocean carrier under the carrier's arrangement, while storage is charged by the terminal or facility under its own tariff. Two invoices from two organisations can arrive for the same container in the same week.

2. The resource priced differs. Demurrage prices a full container occupying terminal time, detention prices the carrier's equipment held out of circulation, and storage prices physical space. Because the resources differ, the charges can stack on the same days, which is why a delayed container costs more than newcomers expect.

3. The arithmetic differs from freight. Time-based charges are per container and per day, so the cost scales with time rather than with quantity or value, and under a progressive tariff the rate itself rises as the delay lengthens.

For budgeting, each of the three has the same expected value on a well-run shipment: zero. Each also carries the same tail risk: an escalating exposure that grows every day a process problem persists. That combination, expected zero with a heavy tail, is exactly the profile a budget must handle deliberately, and the rest of this lesson builds that handling.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Free time and the last free day as costing instruments
BODY:
Free time is a specified period during which particular container or storage charges are not applied, and the last free day is the final day of that allowance. Lesson 9.4 explained how the allowance works; the costing lesson is what the allowance does to money.

Free time is the buffer that converts a potential charge into a zero. On the planned shipment, clearance, collection, and empty return all happen inside the allowance, so the time-based cost of the shipment is nil. The allowance is therefore not paperwork trivia. It is the risk margin built into the shipment's cost structure: the number of days of operational slack the importer owns before delay starts converting directly into invoice value.

Two costing consequences follow. First, the expected cost of time-based charges on any shipment is zero if the plan holds, and the plan holds when the disciplines of earlier lessons hold: documents and permits complete and verified before arrival as Lessons 6.3 and 6.4 taught, the release chain worked early as Lesson 9.3 taught, and transport committed with margin. Second, the allowance's length sets how thin the margin is. A shorter allowance, which refrigerated equipment commonly carries, leaves less room for the ordinary frictions of clearance and collection, so the same slip produces charges on one shipment and none on another.

The last free day is the switch between two economies. Before it, an extra day of delay costs effort: a phone call, an overtime hour, a courier fee. After it, an extra day costs tariff money, at rates that commonly rise. Costing a shipment forward therefore means costing the effort that keeps it on the right side of that switch, which is the prevention economics this lesson builds later.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: How the charges accrue and land as invoices
BODY:
Lesson 9.4 explained that time-based charges accrue day by day and are invoiced after the event, with no visible meter at the gate. The costing lesson is what that behaviour does to a business's accounts, and it has four features.

1. Accrual is per container and per day. The unit of cost is one box for one billable day, and the chargeable period, meaning the period for which fees are calculated under the applicable tariff or agreement, runs from free-time expiry until collection for demurrage, from gate-out until empty return for detention, and for as long as cargo occupies charged space for storage. A container collected part-way through a day can still make the whole day billable, as Lesson 9.4 explained.

2. The invoice arrives after the money has already been committed by time. By the time it lands, the chargeable days are history. The finance team's first sight of the cost comes weeks after the operations team created it, which is why these charges feel like surprises even when the delay that caused them was well known inside the business.

3. The party invoiced is usually the consignee. Under Incoterms 2020, import clearance is the buyer's obligation under every rule except Delivered Duty Paid, so in most import transactions the party arranging clearance and collection is the party the clocks bill, as the previous module explained.

4. The amounts are reconstructed, not metered. A correct invoice is simply the tariff's arithmetic applied to the chargeable period, which means every time-based invoice can and should be rebuilt from first principles: days, bands, rates. That reconstruction is the audit skill taught later in this lesson.

Seen this way, a demurrage invoice is not an anomaly. It is the accounting shadow of an operational event.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Interpreting daily rates and progressive tariffs
BODY:
A progressive tariff is a charging structure in which daily rates increase over successive periods. Lesson 9.4 introduced the shape; this lesson develops the interpretation, because the difference between a flat rate and a stepped rate changes every forward-looking number in a logistics budget.

Interpreting a tariff for costing purposes means extracting four facts from it.

1. Where each band begins and ends. A stepped tariff might price the first chargeable days at one rate, the next block of days at a higher rate, and every day beyond at a higher rate still. The bands, not the headline rate, determine the cost of any given delay.

2. What each band costs per container per day. Because rates are stated per container per day, a multi-container shipment multiplies both the daily cost and the number of days the whole pattern runs.

3. How days are counted and from which event. The start event, the counting convention, and the part-day rule all come from the tariff, as Lesson 9.4 explained.

4. Whether the clocks run separately or in combination, because a combined arrangement changes which allowance protects which leg of the journey.

One fact-safe rule governs all of it: rates and free-time allowances are purely contractual terms of each line and terminal, and no standard amounts exist internationally. The only rates worth costing are the ones in the tariff attached to your own booking, and the only constant across tariffs is the shape of the behaviour: cost grows faster than time. When a tariff steps from one rate to a higher one, the marginal day is dearer than the average day, which is why a long delay devastates margins that a flat-rate estimate said were safe.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The wider family of other charges
BODY:
The time-based three are the famous members of the family, but an import budget must expect a wider cast of other charges, most conditional on events that good operations make rare. The recurring ones are these.

1. Documentation fees. A fee charged for preparing, issuing, or processing specified documents, such as transport documents. A close cousin is the amendment fee, charged when a document already issued must be corrected and reissued, which is a defect fee in disguise: it prices an error, not a service.

2. Terminal handling charges. A terminal handling charge is a charge associated with specified cargo or container handling services at a terminal, typically settled around release among the applicable payments Lesson 9.3 described. It is planned, unlike the clock charges, and belongs in every landed-cost calculation as Lesson 10.2 taught.

3. Container cleaning fees. A charge for cleaning a container when its return condition requires cleaning under the applicable equipment-return conditions. Residue, staining, odour, or pest traces can trigger it.

4. Container repair charges. A charge for repairing container damage where responsibility is established under the applicable agreement. The qualifier matters: responsibility must be established, and the equipment interchange record from Lesson 9.3 is usually where it is established or refuted.

5. Weighing fees. Where a shipment or container must be weighed, for example when a verified gross mass is established by weighing the packed container, the facility may charge for the service.

6. Inspection-related costs. When customs selects a consignment for examination, the unstacking, handling, and re-presentation of cargo take labour and time, facilities may charge under their tariffs, and the importer's agent spends hours in attendance.

7. Delay-linked internal costs. Re-lodgement of a corrected declaration, agency overtime, rebooked trucks, warehouse rescheduling, and the staff hours in between. They never appear on an external tariff, but they are real money, and Lesson 9.4 named them the standstill costs.

8. Storage beyond the port. The storage charge concept is not confined to terminals; cargo moved into a bonded or private warehouse sits under that facility's tariff instead.

9. Insurance excesses on claims. Where damage becomes an insurance claim, the policy's excess, the portion of each claim the insured bears, costs money even when the claim succeeds.

The family shares one character: every line is conditional on an event, and every event is one that planning can usually prevent.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: How delays in documents and collection become cost
BODY:
The clearest way to see why documentation delays and slow collection cost money is to trace the cascade, because the clock charges are only its most visible stage.

Consider a documentation delay. A required permit is not in hand when the vessel berths, or a commercial invoice disagrees with the transport document, and clearance stalls, as Lessons 6.3 and 6.4 each explained in their own territory. The stages that follow are these: the chargeable period opens and demurrage and terminal storage begin; the agent's time goes into corrections, re-lodgement, and follow-up; amendment fees may be incurred to fix documents already issued; the booked truck misses its slot and is rebooked; the goods reach the market later than the sales plan assumed; and, if the delay is long, private warehouse storage or an insurance claim may follow. One missing document, many line items.

Consider a collection delay. Release is complete but no truck is committed, or the warehouse is full, or stripping drags on. Demurrage runs until gate-out, then detention runs until the empty box is returned, and the terminal's space charge can run alongside the first of them. The cascade here is shorter, but the arithmetic is identical: a gap between two operational events is filled with tariff money.

The advanced insight is that the invoice understates the true cost. The external charges are receipted and visible; the internal costs, staff hours, missed sales, credibility with customers, are mostly invisible and never invoiced by anyone. Businesses that cost delays only by their invoices systematically understate what poor coordination costs them, and therefore underinvest in prevention. Once the whole cascade is priced, the case for spending on documents, permits, and transport booking before arrival becomes overwhelming.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Costing forward: the expected-charges budget
BODY:
Everything so far explains how the charges behave after the event. The professional discipline is to cost them forward, before the vessel sails, shipment by shipment.

The tool is an expected-charges budget built for each shipment alongside the landed-cost calculation of Lesson 10.2. It lists every charge the shipment can expect with its source: ocean freight and surcharges from the quote, terminal handling and documentation fees from the tariff, duties and taxes from the assessment basis, transport from the haulier's rates. The time-based charges are budgeted at expected zero, because on a shipment whose documents, permits, release chain, and transport are all prepared, zero is genuinely the expected outcome. Expected zero is not wishful thinking; it is a prediction with a process behind it.

Alongside the expected lines sits a contingency understanding. A reserve is held for the tail, sized from the importer's own history of delays, and its purpose is to absorb a genuine shock, a customs examination, a storm-delayed vessel, without destabilising the month's accounts. The contingency is management's acknowledgment that plans meet reality; it is not a licence to be casual about the last free day, and a contingency that is regularly consumed is a signal that the underlying processes, not the budget, need work.

The discipline completes with tracking. Actual charges are recorded against the budget for every shipment, and variances are investigated with the same seriousness as a stock discrepancy: a cleaning fee, a detention charge, or a demurrage invoice is a variance with a cause, and the cause sits in a process. This is cost avoidance, the practice of preventing unnecessary expenditure before it occurs, and it differs from cost negotiation, which happens after the expenditure exists and recovers only part of it.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Every demurrage invoice is a process failure
BODY:
When a time-based charge accrues despite the expected-zero budget, the costing response is neither to pay and forget nor simply to argue the invoice down. It is to ask what process failed. Handled this way, every demurrage invoice becomes diagnostic: it tells the business exactly which discipline broke.

The recurring root causes are few, and each belongs to an earlier lesson.

1. Permits and certificates not obtained in time, so clearance cannot complete. Lesson 6.3 covered these documents; the failure sits in the pre-arrival document plan.

2. Document discrepancies discovered at lodgement, so release stalls while papers are corrected. Lesson 6.4 covered verification; the failure is in checking before arrival rather than after.

3. The release chain left incomplete, with shipping line release, customs release, or terminal release still outstanding while free time drains. Lesson 9.3 covered the three keys; the failure is in starting them early.

4. Transport not booked, or booked without margin, so a released container waits for a truck. The failure is in collection planning.

5. Communication failures, where a foreseeable delay was not escalated early enough to be managed. Lesson 5.4 covered tracking and communication; the failure is in escalation.

The working tool is a root-cause table with one row per invoice: the shipment, the chargeable days, the immediate cause, the underlying process failure, the owner of that process, the corrective action taken, and the date the fix was verified as working. The table converts invoices into an improvement pipeline.

Two rules make it work. First, fix the process, not the invoice: a negotiated discount leaves the failure in place to bill again next month. Second, verify the fix: if the same root cause produces a second invoice, the corrective action did not work and must be redesigned. The worked example later in this lesson shows one importer's quarterly table in full.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The economics of prevention and the KPI of zero
BODY:
Prevention has a price: an agent working overtime, a courier carrying a certificate, a permit application lodged weeks early, a phone call to confirm a truck. Delay has a price too: chargeable days at rates that rise in steps, stacked with storage and standstill costs. The economics of this lesson rest on comparing the two prices honestly, and the comparison is rarely close. Prevention buys a bounded, one-off effort cost, typically one day of expedited work. Delay buys an open-ended, escalating tariff cost that continues until someone acts. The worked example that follows puts illustrative numbers on exactly this comparison, and the pattern holds whenever the comparison is made seriously: prevention is almost always cheaper, often by several times.

Two management practices convert that economics into routine.

First, act inside the window where action is cheap. Time-based charges are negotiable only before they accrue: a request for an extension of free time, made early with a credible reason, is a routine commercial request that lines handle every day; the same request after the charges have accrued is an argument about money already owed. The cheap day is the day before the last free day, not the day the invoice arrives.

Second, measure the tail. The key performance indicator for this cost family is time-based and other avoidable charges as a percentage of total logistics spend, tracked period by period. No universal benchmark exists, and none is needed: the comparison is with your own past quarters. The goal is a trend toward zero, because zero is what a well-run import operation should accrue. Track the count of invoices as well as their value; a falling total with recurring invoices means a few processes still fail, and the root-cause table names them.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Reviewing and disputing incorrect charges
BODY:
Because these charges are reconstructed from tariffs rather than metered, some of them are simply wrong: days miscounted, rates applied from the wrong band, a container billed after it was returned, damage charged that predates the consignee's custody. The review discipline is therefore not optional scepticism but routine audit: every time-based invoice and every equipment-related charge is checked against the tariff and records you already hold before it is paid. An accepted invoice is accepted practice; pay whatever arrives unexamined and you have certified its accuracy for every shipment that follows.

The audit reconstructs the charge from first principles. For a time-based charge: the container number matches your shipment; the availability, gate-out, and return dates match your records; the chargeable days are counted correctly under the tariff's convention; each day is priced from the correct band of the progressive structure; the arithmetic totals; and charges from different creditors correspond to their own tariffs and allowances, since the line's demurrage and the terminal's storage are separate charges that can legitimately both apply. For equipment charges: the cleaning or repair is described specifically enough to verify, and the responsibility question is answered by the equipment interchange record from Lesson 9.3, which recorded the container's condition at each interchange.

Where the reconstruction fails, the remedy is an invoice dispute, a formal challenge concerning the accuracy or validity of a billed charge. A dispute is conducted in writing, raised promptly, and supported by the evidence trail: the booking and its tariff schedule, the reconstruction, the interchange records, and photographs taken at unpacking and at empty return. The worked example later in this lesson shows one charge paid because the evidence supported it and another withdrawn because the evidence contradicted it, which is exactly the intended outcome of a disciplined review: valid charges are paid promptly, and invalid ones are not paid at all.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: The arithmetic of one late container at Tema
BODY:
A distributor of packaged food products in Accra is importing one forty-foot container of cooking oil through the Port of Tema. All figures and periods in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: The booking terms. The booking confirmation carries an illustrative free-time allowance of five days for collection, counted from the day the container is made available, and a progressive demurrage tariff: for illustration, chargeable days one to three at GHS 300 per day, days four to six at GHS 500 per day, and every day from the seventh at GHS 800 per day. The terminal's illustrative storage charge is GHS 150 per day. The distributor's expected-charges budget carries demurrage and storage at zero.

Step 2: The failure. The goods require an import permit, and the application was never lodged before arrival, repeating the exact failure Lesson 6.3 warns against. Clearance cannot complete, and the container is not collected until the eighth chargeable day.

Step 3: The accrual table. Reconstructing the demurrage:

- Chargeable days one to three: three days at GHS 300, which is GHS 900.
- Chargeable days four to six: three days at GHS 500, which is GHS 1,500.
- Chargeable days seven and eight: two days at GHS 800, which is GHS 1,600.
- Demurrage total: GHS 900 plus GHS 1,500 plus GHS 1,600, which is GHS 4,000.

Terminal storage runs for the same eight days at GHS 150 per day, adding GHS 1,200. The two invoices together total GHS 5,200, and the rebooked truck adds an illustrative GHS 400.

Step 4: The counterfactual. Suppose instead that, in the week before arrival, the company had paid its clearing agent for one day of overtime at an illustrative GHS 600 and an illustrative GHS 250 courier fee to obtain the outstanding supporting document in time. Clearance completes inside free time, the container gates out on day four, and every time-based charge is zero. Total prevention cost: GHS 850.

Step 5: The comparison. GHS 5,200 against GHS 850: the delay cost roughly six times the prevention, and the GHS 4,350 difference bought nothing but days. The goods also reached the market eight working days later, which cost sales that nobody invoiced for.

What does this example demonstrate? The escalation pattern makes each late day dearer than the one before, so a delay of eight chargeable days costs far more than twice a delay of four. The invoice records only the external half of the loss. And the prevention side of the ledger is small, bounded, and known in advance.

Key lesson: when one day of expedited effort is compared honestly with one week of escalating charges, prevention is not a virtue. It is arithmetic.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A quarterly review: three invoices, three root causes
BODY:
Kwabena Trading Ltd is a distributor importing consumer goods through the Port of Tema, its customs declarations lodged in ICUMS by its clearing agent, as Ghanaian import practice generally runs. In one quarter the company received three demurrage invoices, and its new logistics manager did what this lesson prescribes: treated each invoice as a process failure and built the root-cause table. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Invoice one, an illustrative GHS 2,350 on a consignment of food products requiring a permit from the Food and Drugs Authority. Immediate cause: clearance could not complete. Root cause, traced with the agent: the permit application had not been lodged before the vessel sailed from its last port, so the authority's processing time was consumed after arrival instead of before it. Owner: the import officer responsible for regulatory documents. Corrective action: every permit application lodged at booking confirmation, with the expected approval tracked before the vessel's arrival, as Lesson 6.3 implies.

Invoice two, an illustrative GHS 1,050 on a consignment of household goods. Immediate cause: the commercial invoice's goods description did not match the transport document, and the declaration required correction. Root cause: documents were compiled in the arrival week instead of verified before it, the habit Lesson 6.4 teaches against. Owner: the documentation clerk. Corrective action: a pre-arrival verification checklist, with the agent confirming the consistency of descriptions, quantities, and references before lodgement.

Invoice three, an illustrative GHS 1,500 on a machinery consignment. Immediate cause: release was complete, but the first available truck came four days later. Root cause: transport was booked after release instead of against the planned release date. Owner: the transport coordinator. Corrective action: haulage committed at the same time as the release chain starts, a backup haulier identified, and the last free day diarised as a hard deadline, as Lesson 9.4 recommended.

The quarter's total, an illustrative GHS 4,900, matters less than the table's last column: three different owners, three different processes, and no common villain. The line, the terminal, and customs appear nowhere in the root causes. In the following quarter, with the three fixes implemented and verified, the company recorded no time-based charges on its Tema arrivals.

What does this example demonstrate? Demurrage invoices look like port costs and are usually process costs. A root-cause table converts a bad quarter into a work list, assigns each item to an owner, and makes each fix verifiable.

Key lesson: the review that pays for itself is not the one that argues with the invoices. It is the one that finds the three processes behind them and closes each one.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Cleaning and damage charges in Singapore: paying with evidence, disputing with evidence
BODY:
An importer of industrial spare parts in Singapore collects a container from the terminal, unpacks it at its warehouse, and returns the empty box to the line's depot within the allowance. Two weeks later an invoice arrives with two lines: a container cleaning fee and a container repair charge. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: Read the lines against the definitions. A cleaning fee is chargeable when the return condition requires cleaning under the equipment-return conditions, and a repair charge where responsibility is established under the applicable agreement. The audit question is the same for each line: what event produced this charge, and does the evidence support that event?

Step 2: The cleaning charge. The line's photographs show dried cargo residue on the container floor: several cartons of machine lubricant leaked during the voyage and in the warehouse. The importer's own unpacking report, compiled the day the container was stripped, records the same leakage. The evidence supports the charge, so an illustrative US dollars 180 cleaning fee is paid without dispute. The deeper lesson goes into the root-cause table: the leak was a packaging failure, and the corrective action is an improved packing specification on the next order, which is cost avoidance applied to a charge the importer would otherwise pay again.

Step 3: The repair charge. The line claims an illustrative US dollars 420 for a dented door panel and a cut door seal, implying damage in the importer's custody. The importer checks its copy of the equipment interchange record, the document Lesson 9.3 introduced as the record of condition at each interchange. The record signed at gate-out from the terminal already notes the dent and the cut seal as existing damage at the moment of collection. Damage that exists at collection cannot have been caused after it.

Step 4: The dispute. The importer writes to the line promptly, encloses the interchange record and the unpacking photographs, and states that responsibility for the damage is not established. The line checks its own interchange records, confirms the damage was pre-existing, and withdraws the charge.

What does this example demonstrate? The audit found one valid charge and one invalid one, and the evidence decided both. Paying the valid charge promptly preserved credibility; disputing the invalid one with documents recovered US dollars 420 that blind payment would have donated. Paying the invoice unexamined would have taught the opposite lesson to everyone, including the importer's own team.

Key lesson: every charge line is a claim until the evidence confirms it, and the evidence, the tariff, the unpacking report, the interchange record, the photographs, is assembled during the shipment, not after the invoice arrives.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Extensions are routine before the clock starts; arguments after it stops
BODY:
Free-time allowances feel fixed, but they sit inside commercial relationships, and lines extend them for credible operational reasons more often than newcomers expect. The timing is everything. A request made days before the last free day, explaining the delay, proposing a realistic new collection date, and backed by a shipment history of prompt collections, is a routine commercial conversation. The same request made after the charges have accrued is something quite different: an argument about money the tariff already says is owed, conducted with a creditor who holds the records and owes the importer nothing.

This asymmetry has a budgeting consequence. The cheap time to solve a foreseeable delay costs a phone call; the expensive time costs the invoice. So the moment a delay becomes foreseeable, a permit running late, an examination notified, a truck unavailable, the escalation should go to the line's agent immediately, as Lesson 5.4's communication discipline implies, while the request still costs nothing.

The mirror-image rule applies to the invoices they send you. An accepted invoice is accepted practice: pay a time-based invoice without checking it against the tariff you were given, and you have verified nothing, but you have certified everything. Audit first, pay promptly what survives the audit, and dispute what does not.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Budget for the tail or the tail will budget for you
BODY:
The landed-cost calculation of Lesson 10.2 prices the plan, and a budget built only from landed costs has a hole exactly the shape of this lesson. The hole closes in one of two ways. Either the importer prices the tail deliberately, an expected-zero line for the time-based charges, planned lines for handling and documentation, and a contingency reserve sized from its own delay history, or the tail prices itself, arriving as invoices nobody expected and taking its share from margins nobody protected.

Unpriced tail costs do their worst damage quietly. They inflate the true landed cost of the affected shipments above the figure used to set prices. They arrive in months whose budgets have already been spent. And because they are infrequent, they evade the per-unit costing routines that catch ordinary cost creep, so a business can run for years treating demurrage as bad luck before anyone adds up what the luck costs.

The discipline is not complicated. Add the tail lines to every shipment budget. Record actuals against them. Feed every variance into the root-cause table. Track the family's share of logistics spend over time and aim its trend at zero. None of this prevents a genuine shock, but it converts the tail from a surprise into a managed, measured, shrinking category, which is the whole difference between a cost you suffer and a cost you run.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: A dispute is an evidence exercise, conducted promptly and in writing
BODY:
Disputing a charge is not a negotiation of mood or volume. It is an evidence exercise, and it has a method.

State the challenge in writing, promptly, while records are fresh and before the claim ages. Identify the precise defect: the days miscounted, the band misapplied, the container returned before the billed date, the damage that predates your custody. Attach the reconstruction and the documents: the booking and its tariff schedule, the equipment interchange record from each interchange, the unpacking report, and the photographs taken at collection and at return. Ask for the specific remedy: a corrected invoice or a withdrawal.

Keep the dispute narrow. Where an invoice carries five lines and two are wrong, dispute the two and pay the three, saying so in the letter. Withholding an entire account over one disputed line damages credibility and, with time-based charges that lines commonly require to be settled before release, can stall a container and breed exactly the charges being disputed. Credibility is the disputing party's main asset: the importer who audits everything, pays what survives, and disputes with documents is believed; the importer who disputes everything is filtered out.

One boundary completes the discipline. A dispute challenges accuracy and validity; it does not manufacture sympathy. The evidence either supports the charge or it does not, and a business that has kept the records knows which before the letter is written.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
An importer's booking at Tema carries an illustrative progressive demurrage tariff: chargeable days one to three at GHS 200 per day, days four to six at GHS 400 per day, and every day from the seventh at GHS 700 per day. The container is collected on the fifth chargeable day.

What demurrage charge does the tariff produce?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. GHS 1,000, because all five days are priced at the first band's rate.
2. GHS 1,400, because the first three days are priced at the first band and the remaining two days at the second band.
3. GHS 2,000, because all five days are priced at the second band's rate.
4. GHS 3,500, because once the highest band is reached its rate applies to the whole chargeable period.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

A progressive tariff prices each chargeable day from the band that day falls in, so the calculation is built band by band. Days one to three sit in the first band: three days at GHS 200, which is GHS 600. Days four and five sit in the second band: two days at GHS 400, which is GHS 800. The total is GHS 600 plus GHS 800, or GHS 1,400. The third band is never reached, because the container was collected on the fifth chargeable day.

Answer 1 applies the first band's rate to days that have already moved into the second band. Answer 3 applies the second band's rate to all five days, including the three that belong to the first. Answer 4 describes a structure in which the highest reached rate applies retrospectively to every day; that is not how stepped progressive tariffs are read, and it is precisely the misreading that a band-by-band reconstruction prevents.

The takeaway: reconstruct every time-based charge from the tariff, band by band and day by day, before accepting the figure.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
Over one quarter, an importer receives three demurrage invoices on three different Tema arrivals, and each charge is valid under the applicable tariffs. At the quarterly review, which response best reflects the discipline this lesson teaches?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Negotiate a standing demurrage discount with the shipping line so that future invoices are smaller.
2. Increase next quarter's demurrage budget line so that the charges are covered in advance.
3. Trace each invoice to its process root cause, assign an owner to each cause, implement corrective actions, and track time-based charges as a share of logistics spend with the goal of trending to zero.
4. Accept the charges as an unavoidable cost of shipping through busy ports and spread them across all shipments in the pricing.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The lesson's central discipline treats every demurrage invoice as a process failure with a discoverable root cause: a permit lodged late, a document discrepancy, transport not booked with margin. The root-cause table converts the invoices into an improvement pipeline with owners and verified fixes, and the key performance indicator, time-based charges as a share of logistics spend, trending toward zero, measures whether those fixes work. On a well-run shipment the expected time-based charge is zero, so zero is the legitimate target.

Answer 1 negotiates the symptom and leaves the failures in place to bill again next quarter. Answer 2 institutionalises the cost: budgeting more for demurrage is planning to keep failing, when the expected line should be zero with a contingency reserve reserved for genuine shocks. Answer 4 surrenders control, treating avoidable process costs as fate; valid charges are payable once, but their causes are fixable, and a business that prices them into every shipment taxes its good shipments for its bad processes.

The takeaway: the charges are valid, so the argument is not with the invoices. It is with the processes that produced them.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
An importer receives an invoice from a shipping line containing a container cleaning fee, supported by photographs of cargo residue from cartons that leaked inside the box, and a container repair charge for door damage. The importer's copy of the equipment interchange record signed at collection records the same door damage as already existing when the container was handed over.

Which statement best describes the correct handling of the two charges?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Both charges should be paid, because an invoice issued by a shipping line is presumed accurate.
2. Both charges should be refused, because charges arriving after the cargo has been delivered cannot be valid.
3. The cleaning fee should be paid because the evidence supports it, while the repair charge should be disputed promptly in writing with the interchange record and photographs, because the damage predated the importer's custody.
4. The repair charge should be paid now and disputed later if similar charges recur, while the cleaning fee should be passed to the supplier of the goods for immediate payment.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

A container repair charge is claimable where responsibility for the damage is established under the applicable agreement. The equipment interchange record from Lesson 9.3 is the document that records a container's condition at each interchange, and its note of the damage at collection places the dent before the importer's custody, so responsibility for it is not established. A prompt, written dispute with the record and photographs attached is the correct remedy. The cleaning fee is the opposite case: the residue is cargo-derived, and the photographs and unpacking report support it, so it is a valid cost of the shipment's own packing failure, to be paid and then prevented through a better packing specification next time.

Answer 1 is the blind payment this lesson warns against: an accepted invoice becomes accepted practice. Answer 2 refuses valid and invalid charges alike, sacrificing credibility and ignoring the evidence. Answer 4 delays a dispute it could win now, and misdirects the cleaning fee, which is the importer's own packaging lesson to pay for and fix.

The takeaway: audit every charge line against the evidence, pay promptly what the evidence supports, and dispute in writing what it contradicts.
