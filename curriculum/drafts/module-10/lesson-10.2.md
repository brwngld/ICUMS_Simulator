# DRAFT — Lesson 10.2: Freight Costing and Landed-Cost Calculations

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: What landed cost means
BODY:
Lesson 10.1, Components of Total Logistics Cost, mapped the families of expense that a logistics operation generates: transportation, warehousing, inventory holding, cargo handling, administration, insurance, and distribution. This lesson points that cost-family lens at a single consignment and asks the question every importer must answer: what did it really cost to get these goods from the seller's door to my warehouse, ready for sale or use?

The answer is called the landed cost. The landed cost is the total cost of acquiring and bringing goods to a specified destination, including the expenses defined by the applicable costing policy. In plain terms, it is the price of the goods plus every charge needed to move them, insure them, clear them through the border, handle them at the destination, and carry them to the buyer's premises, together with the quiet costs at the end of the journey.

The usual families in a landed-cost build for an import are these: the goods value on the supplier's invoice; the international freight; cargo insurance; duties, taxes, and levies; terminal and handling charges at the destination port or airport; inland transport to the warehouse; and a cost tail of clearance fees, storage exposure, and contingencies. Lesson 10.1 taught the families; this lesson prices one shipment through all of them, line by line.

The phrase "defined by the applicable costing policy" matters. Which lines belong in a particular party's build is not a matter of taste: it follows from the commercial arrangement between buyer and seller, above all the Incoterms rule in the sales contract, treated later in this lesson. A landed cost is always somebody's landed cost, counted from that party's door.

Landed cost matters for three decisions above all: setting a selling price that truly covers what the goods cost, choosing between suppliers in different source markets, and telling management the honest truth about margins. A buyer who knows only the invoice price knows only the first slice of the number.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Freight costing and the freight rate
BODY:
Freight costing is the process of determining the expenses associated with transporting goods. Its central instrument is the freight rate: the price charged for transporting goods according to an agreed pricing basis. A freight rate is never just a number; it is a number attached to a basis, and the basis decides how the arithmetic runs. "GHS 600" means nothing on its own; "GHS 600 per tonne or per cubic metre, whichever is greater" is a complete pricing rule.

The common pricing bases are these.

1. By weight. Dense, heavy cargo such as ingots or machinery castings is charged per tonne, because weight, not space, is what the cargo consumes.

2. By measurement, that is by volume. Light, bulky cargo such as empty plastic ware or expanded foam is charged per cubic metre, because space, not weight, is what it consumes.

3. By weight or measurement, whichever is greater. Ocean carriers carrying less-than-container-load (LCL) cargo combine the two bases into one rule, examined in the next block.

4. By container. A shipper with a full container load (FCL), met in Lesson 7.3, Containerisation: FCL, LCL, and Container Types, usually pays a rate for the box, not for the contents: a rate per twenty-foot or forty-foot container whether the box travels full, heavy, or light.

5. Per kilogram of chargeable weight. Air cargo prices by the kilogram, but the kilogram that counts is a calculated one, covered in its own block below.

Lesson 7.4, Shipping Charges, Freight Rates, and Surcharges, explained how a freight quotation is assembled: a base rate plus surcharges and ancillary charges such as terminal handling, documentation, and security fees. This lesson does not repeat that assembly. Here the freight quotation arrives as one number for the consignment, and the task is to apply the pricing basis behind it and fold the result into a landed cost.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Weight or measurement: the rule for LCL ocean freight
BODY:
When cargo does not fill a container, the carrier or consolidating forwarder collects many shippers' consignments into one box. Each consignment then pays its share of the container, and the share is settled by the weight-or-measurement rule, in the trade simply called W/M: the consignment is charged on its weight in tonnes or on its volume in cubic metres, whichever gives the carrier the greater revenue. The chargeable unit that results is often called the revenue tonne: one tonne of weight or one cubic metre of space, whichever is being paid for.

The logic is fairness of a hard kind. A vessel earns revenue from space and is limited by payload. A consignment of machinery castings that weighs 8 tonnes but occupies 2 cubic metres consumes the ship's weight allowance, so it pays on 8. A consignment of ping-pong balls that weighs 100 kilograms but occupies 5 cubic metres consumes the ship's space, so it pays on 5. As an illustrative shape of the rule: a consignment measuring 3 cubic metres and weighing 2 tonnes is charged on 3, because 3 is the greater; the same goods turned around, measuring 2 cubic metres and weighing 3 tonnes, would be charged on 3 again, this time by weight.

Two practical consequences follow. First, a shipper can often lower the chargeable quantity by better packing: smaller cartons and tighter stacking reduce the measurement on which an LCL consignment is charged. Second, once a shipper's cargo grows to fill a container, the arithmetic changes shape entirely: above a break-even fill, the flat rate for the whole box costs less than paying by revenue tonne, which is why consolidators quote both and shippers compare. That comparison belongs to freight negotiation; this lesson takes the quoted rate and runs the numbers it produces.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Chargeable weight and volumetric weight in air freight
BODY:
An aircraft is scarce in two dimensions at once: it can carry only so many kilograms of payload and only so many cubic metres of volume. A consignment of tungsten drill bits would exhaust the weight allowance long before filling the hold; a consignment of feather pillows would fill the hold while barely registering on the scales. Air cargo resolves the tension with a calculated weight.

Volumetric weight is a calculated weight based on shipment dimensions and an applicable conversion factor. Under the IATA standard factor, the volumetric weight in kilograms is the length multiplied by the width multiplied by the height of the piece, all in centimetres, divided by 6,000. In other words, the standard treats every 6,000 cubic centimetres as one chargeable kilogram, which is the same as treating one cubic metre as about 166.7 kilograms. Some contracts or carriers publish different divisors, but 6,000 is the IATA standard and the factor this course uses.

Chargeable weight is the weight used to calculate freight charges under the applicable transport pricing rules. In air freight it is the higher of the actual gross weight and the volumetric weight. The airline weighs the cargo, measures it, computes both figures, and charges on whichever is greater: the drill bits pay on their real weight, the pillows pay on the air they displace.

The freight charge then follows mechanically: chargeable weight multiplied by the per-kilogram rate quoted for the lane, plus the air-side surcharges of the kind Lesson 7.4 described. One caution belongs to the costing office: the conversion factor is a rule of the pricing agreement, so the factor specified in the applicable tariff or contract governs the calculation, and it should be checked before any figure is trusted. A worked computation follows in the third worked example of this lesson.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: From consignment freight to freight per unit
BODY:
A freight quotation covers a consignment, but a business sells cartons, bags, or pieces. To land the number where decisions are made, the consignment's freight must be divided among the units it contains: freight per unit equals the total freight charges for the consignment divided by the number of sale units carried. This freight cost per unit is the standard comparator in performance measurement, and it is the freight slice of the unit landed cost developed at the end of this lesson.

The division sounds trivial, but three habits make it honest.

1. Use the consignment's full freight, not the base rate alone. The surcharges and ancillary charges attached to the shipment, from terminal handling to documentation, all travelled with the cartons and all belong in the numerator.

2. Allocate on a sensible basis. Where the units are identical cartons, an equal split is correct. Where they differ in size or weight, allocate in proportion to what drove the charge: a consignment charged by measurement should share its freight out by volume, and one charged by weight should share by weight.

3. Watch what the result teaches. Freight per unit falls when consignments are consolidated into fuller containers, falls when packing is tightened, and rises when a half-empty box sails. Two importers buying identical goods at identical freight rates can carry very different freight per unit purely because of how they pack and consolidate.

In the first worked example below, a consignment's LCL freight of GHS 10,800 across 500 cartons puts GHS 21.60 of ocean freight inside every carton before a single other cost is counted. That per-carton figure is the freight line of the per-carton landed cost, and it is the number a pricing manager actually uses.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The landed-cost build and its cost breakdown
BODY:
A landed cost is produced by a build: an ordered list of cost lines added from the goods value to a total. A cost breakdown is an itemised presentation of the expenses contributing to a total, and the build is simply a disciplined cost breakdown in a fixed sequence, so that nothing that will actually be paid can silently vanish. The standard lines of an import build run as follows.

1. Goods value. The price of the goods on the supplier's commercial invoice, converted into the buyer's own currency at the rate that applies when payment is made. This is the only line most quotations ever show.

2. International freight. The ocean or air charge for the consignment, computed on the pricing basis of the previous blocks.

3. Cargo insurance. The premium for covering the transit risk, whether bought by the buyer or, under some seller-paid arrangements, arriving embedded in the goods price.

4. Duties, taxes, and levies. Import duty is a customs duty imposed on imported goods under applicable tariff and customs laws; import tax is a tax imposed on imported goods under applicable national legislation, value added tax being the common example; levies are further statutory charges attached to importing. Each is computed by rule on its own base, as the next block explains. In Ghana, an import assessment consists of import duty plus additional taxes and levies, and the component names include import duty, VAT, the National Health Insurance Levy (NHIL), the GETFund levy, the ECOWAS levy, and the AU levy; the assessment is generated as a tax bill when the declaration is processed in ICUMS and is paid at a participating bank.

5. Terminal and handling charges. Destination-side charges such as the terminal handling charge, documentation, and security fees at the port or airport.

6. Inland transport. The road or rail leg from the port or airport to the buyer's warehouse.

7. Clearance and agency fees. What the licensed clearing agent and any other service providers charge for lodging the declaration and arranging the entry.

8. The cost tail. Storage, time-based charge exposure, financing, and a contingency margin, the quiet lines that an honest build refuses to ignore.

Builds differ: a policy may merge lines or split them further. What no honest build does is omit a line the buyer will really pay.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Customs value versus landed cost
BODY:
The most consequential base in the build is the customs value: the value determined for customs purposes under the applicable customs valuation rules. It is the figure on which import duty is computed, and it is not the same animal as the landed cost.

Under the international valuation rules administered by the World Customs Organization, valuation proceeds through six methods applied in strict order, and the first method is transaction value: the price actually paid or payable for the goods when sold for export, adjusted for prescribed additions where the rules require them. Only where that method cannot be used do the fallback methods apply in sequence. The general principle for this lesson is simple: duty is computed on a customs value determined by rule, at the applicable tariff rate for the goods. The rate itself comes from the tariff schedule in force on the day; this course never teaches rates, only structure.

In Ghana, the customs value on which duty is computed is the CIF value, and Ghana Revenue Authority guidance explains that the subsequent import tax, VAT, is computed on the duty-inclusive value. CIF, cost, insurance and freight, is the Incoterms 2020 rule under which the seller pays the costs, freight, and insurance to the named destination port; the next block treats these rules properly. The costing point here is that the valuation base itself is built from goods value, freight, and insurance, and each later assessment component has its own legally defined base stacked on the ones before it.

Now set the two concepts side by side. The customs value answers a legal question: on what value does the law compute duty for this consignment? The landed cost answers a business question: what did getting these goods to my warehouse, ready for sale, actually cost me? The landed cost contains the customs value's consequences, the duty and taxes computed on it, but also freight-side, destination-side, and tail costs the customs value never carries. The two figures coincide only by accident. Using one where the other is wanted produces two classic errors: pricing goods off the customs value, which understates the true cost, and filing the landed cost as the valuation base, which misstates the duty computation.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Incoterms 2020 and who pays which line
BODY:
A landed cost is always built for a particular party, and the Incoterms 2020 rules decide which lines belong in that party's build. Incoterms 2020 is the International Chamber of Commerce's set of eleven three-letter rules allocating tasks, costs, and risk between seller and buyer in an international sale; they do not transfer ownership or fix payment terms. Lesson 4.3, Incoterms and Allocation of Responsibilities, taught the full allocation; this lesson applies the costing consequences.

Free on Board (FOB) is an Incoterms rule under which the seller delivers goods on board the vessel at the named port of shipment. Cost, Insurance and Freight (CIF) is an Incoterms rule under which the seller pays specified costs, freight, and insurance to the named destination port while risk transfers earlier at shipment.

Four costing consequences drive every build.

1. Main carriage. Under the E and F rules, including EXW and FOB, the buyer arranges and pays the main carriage, so the buyer's build carries a full freight line. Under the C and D rules, including CFR, CIF, CPT, CIP, DAP, DPU, and DDP, the seller pays carriage to the destination, so that cost reaches the buyer inside the goods price.

2. Risk travels separately from cost. Under the four C rules the seller pays carriage to the destination, but risk passes to the buyer already at shipment, on board the vessel at the port of shipment under CIF. A CIF buyer still carries the voyage risk and may insure in its own interest.

3. Insurance. Only CIF and CIP oblige the seller to insure: under CIF at minimum Institute Cargo Clauses (C) cover, under CIP at the higher (A) level. Under every other rule, insurance is the risk-bearing party's own arrangement.

4. Import clearance. Import formalities, duties, and taxes at destination are the buyer's side under all rules except DDP, where the seller carries them.

The practical rule for the build follows: every line the contract puts on the buyer appears in the buyer's landed cost, and every line it puts on the seller arrives already inside the goods price. A CIF Tema purchase needs no ocean-freight line in the buyer's build, but it still needs duty, destination charges, inland transport, and the tail.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Exchange rates and the moving build
BODY:
An exchange rate is the rate at which one currency is exchanged for another. It is the quiet multiplier sitting on top of most of a landed-cost build, because international trade is invoiced across currencies: a Ghanaian importer typically buys goods in US dollars or euros, while selling them in Ghana cedis.

The arithmetic is direct. Every foreign-currency line in the build, the goods value above all, and commonly the ocean or air freight and some surcharges as well, is converted at the rate ruling when the money is actually paid. If the invoice is 10,000 US dollars, the cedi cost is 10,000 multiplied by whatever the rate is on payment day, not on order day. Between those two days the rate moves, and a movement that looks small per dollar becomes a large number across a consignment.

Three costing disciplines follow.

1. Date every conversion. A build should state which date's rate it used, because a build made at order time and a payment actually made weeks later can sit on different rates.

2. Re-run the build when the rate moves. A landed cost is a snapshot, not a permanent fact. Before reordering, and before setting a new selling price, the build is recomputed at the current rate.

3. Budget with a stated planning rate. Businesses that quote selling prices months ahead use a planning rate and treat the difference between it and the eventual rate as a risk to be managed, not as a surprise to be suffered.

Freight brings its own currency exposure. Fuel- and currency-related surcharges of the kind met in Lesson 7.4 move with the dollar side of shipping, so a weaker local currency can raise a freight quotation even when the underlying service is unchanged. Exchange-rate fluctuation is not a footnote to the landed cost; it touches several lines at once, and it can reverse a sourcing decision, as the second worked example shows.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Unit landed cost as the decision number
BODY:
The end of every build is a division. Unit landed cost is the landed cost allocated to one unit of an imported product: the consignment's total landed cost divided by the number of sale units it contains, with the same care over the allocation basis as freight per unit required.

Unit landed cost is the number three kinds of decision run on.

1. Pricing. A selling price must recover the unit landed cost plus a margin. Pricing off the invoice price of the goods alone leaves freight, duty, destination charges, and the tail to be absorbed by money that was never there.

2. Sourcing and purchasing. Suppliers are compared on landed totals, not on ex-factory price tags. The second worked example reverses an apparently obvious choice precisely by running both suppliers through the full build.

3. Margin honesty. Management accounts that record goods at invoice value and discover the rest of the cost later make products look profitable until the freight bill, the assessment, and the storage invoices arrive. Recording inventory at landed cost keeps the profit calculation honest from the day the goods arrive.

Unit landed cost also behaves dynamically, and a costing team watches its drivers: the freight rate and the pricing basis behind it, the packing density that sets chargeable quantity, the exchange rate, the tariff treatment of the goods, and the tail charges that follow clearance speed. When any driver moves, the unit landed cost moves with it, which is why the build is kept as a live model and re-run rather than filed as a one-off calculation.

This closes the frame of the lesson: landed cost is the total, the cost breakdown is its anatomy, and unit landed cost is the decision number that pricing, purchasing, and margin reporting should all quote.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Building the landed cost of 500 cartons of fruit juice
BODY:
Step 1: The consignment. Adwoa Foods Ltd, a distributor in Kumasi, imports 500 cartons of bottled fruit juice from an overseas supplier on FOB terms; the goods travel as an LCL consignment via Tema Port, then by truck to Kumasi. The invoice is 10,000 US dollars, worth GHS 120,000 at the assumed illustrative rate of 12 cedis to the dollar. All figures are illustrative.

Step 2: The freight line by weight or measurement. Each carton measures 0.4 by 0.3 by 0.3 metres and weighs 18 kilograms gross, so the consignment occupies 18 cubic metres and weighs 9 tonnes. Weight or measurement, whichever is greater: 18 beats 9, so the chargeable quantity is 18 revenue tonnes. At an illustrative GHS 600 per revenue tonne, ocean freight is 18 x 600 = GHS 10,800, which is GHS 21.60 inside every carton.

Step 3: Insurance. Cargo insurance for the sea leg: an illustrative premium of GHS 1,200.

Step 4: Duties, taxes, and levies. The customs value on the CIF basis is goods 120,000 plus freight 10,800 plus insurance 1,200 = GHS 132,000. Import duty is computed on that customs value at the applicable tariff rate, shown here as the illustrative assessment figure GHS 11,880. The import tax (VAT) and levies such as the NHIL, the GETFund levy, the ECOWAS levy, and the AU levy, each on its own legal base, are shown as a combined illustrative figure of GHS 24,460. These amounts are placeholders that teach structure only; the live figures are whatever the tariff and tax laws prescribe on the day of assessment.

Step 5: Destination and tail lines. Terminal handling and port charges at Tema: GHS 4,500. Inland transport to Kumasi: GHS 6,000. Clearance and agency fees: GHS 3,500. A storage and contingency allowance for the cost tail: GHS 2,000.

Step 6: Total and per unit. The build: goods 120,000; freight 10,800; insurance 1,200; duty 11,880; taxes and levies 24,460; terminal handling 4,500; inland transport 6,000; clearance and agency 3,500; allowance 2,000. Total landed cost: GHS 184,340. Unit landed cost: 184,340 divided by 500 = GHS 368.68 per carton. The goods contributed GHS 240 per carton; everything after the invoice added GHS 128.68, more than half as much again.

What does this example demonstrate? A landed cost is a build, run line by line in a fixed order: the freight line computed from a pricing basis, the duty lines on legally defined bases, the tail included. The per-carton figure is the only output a pricing decision can use.

Key lesson: price the consignment through the whole build, and label the placeholders for what they are: the live numbers come from the tariff and the market on the day.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: When the cheaper supplier lands dearer
BODY:
Step 1: The decision. Barimah Industrial Supplies, a distributor in Accra, needs 100 water pumps. Supplier A, a pump assembler based in Tema, quotes GHS 2,600 per pump delivered to Accra, inclusive of local taxes. Supplier B, a manufacturer in Shenzhen, quotes 15,000 US dollars for the hundred pumps on FOB Shenzhen terms: GHS 180,000 at the assumed illustrative rate of 12 cedis per dollar, or GHS 1,800 per pump. On price tags, Supplier B looks far cheaper. All figures are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 2: Supplier A's landed cost. The quote is delivered and tax-inclusive, so the build is short: goods GHS 260,000, plus receiving and handling at the Accra warehouse, an illustrative GHS 2,000. Total landed cost GHS 262,000, or GHS 2,620 per pump.

Step 3: Supplier B's freight line. Packed on pallets, the pumps measure 4 cubic metres in total and weigh 3.5 tonnes. Under weight or measurement, 4 beats 3.5, so the consignment pays on 4 revenue tonnes. At an illustrative GHS 900 per revenue tonne, ocean freight is 4 x 900 = GHS 3,600. Cargo insurance adds an illustrative GHS 1,500.

Step 4: Supplier B's duties and destination lines. The customs value on the CIF basis is 180,000 plus 3,600 plus 1,500 = GHS 185,100. Import duty at the applicable tariff rate: illustrative placeholder GHS 22,200. VAT and levies, each on its own legal base, combined: illustrative placeholder GHS 52,000. As before, the placeholders teach structure, not rates. Terminal handling and port charges: GHS 3,800. Clearance and agency fees: GHS 3,200. Inland transport from Tema to Accra: GHS 5,500. Storage and contingency allowance: GHS 1,500.

Step 5: Supplier B's total. 180,000 plus 3,600 plus 1,500 plus 22,200 plus 52,000 plus 3,800 plus 3,200 plus 5,500 plus 1,500 = GHS 273,300, which is GHS 2,733 per pump.

Step 6: The reversal. Supplier B won on the price tag by GHS 800 per pump and lost on landing by GHS 113, about GHS 11,300 across the order: the duty, tax, and destination lines outweighed the goods-price advantage. The comparison is also exposed to the exchange rate. At an illustrative 13 cedis to the dollar, Supplier B's goods alone cost about GHS 150 per pump more, roughly GHS 2,883 landed, before freight and surcharges that are also commonly dollar-linked. Supplier A's cedi price does not move.

What does this example demonstrate? An FOB price is one line of a build, not a total. The ranking of suppliers can reverse, and exchange-rate movement can widen the reversal.

Key lesson: compare source markets on landed totals computed at current rates, never on invoice prices, and re-run the comparison whenever rates or the exchange rate move.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Air or sea for urgent spare parts
BODY:
Step 1: The problem. Naa Densua Machinery Ltd in Tamale maintains a customer's bottling line, which is down awaiting four cartons of spare parts from a supplier in Dubai, invoiced at 18,000 US dollars: GHS 216,000 at the illustrative rate of 12 cedis per dollar. The choice is air through Kotoka International Airport or sea through Tema Port; this example puts numbers on the air-versus-sea trade-off that Lesson 10.1 introduced qualitatively. All figures are illustrative.

Step 2: The air computation. Each carton measures 80 by 60 by 50 centimetres and weighs 30 kilograms gross. Volume per carton is 80 x 60 x 50 = 240,000 cubic centimetres; volumetric weight is 240,000 divided by 6,000 = 40 kilograms. Chargeable weight is the higher of the two, so the consignment pays on 4 x 40 = 160 chargeable kilograms. At an illustrative GHS 130 per chargeable kilogram, air freight is 160 x 130 = GHS 20,800, plus road delivery from Accra to Tamale of GHS 1,200: an air transport total of GHS 22,000. Illustrative door-to-door transit: 6 days.

Step 3: The sea computation. The same four cartons occupy 4 x 0.24 = 0.96 cubic metres and weigh 0.12 tonnes. Weight or measurement: 0.96 beats 0.12, so the consignment pays on 0.96 revenue tonnes. At an illustrative GHS 1,250 per revenue tonne, ocean freight is 0.96 x 1,250 = GHS 1,200, plus road from Tema to Tamale of GHS 2,800: a sea transport total of GHS 4,000. Illustrative door-to-door transit: 35 days.

Step 4: The carrying cost of waiting. At an illustrative holding cost of 20 per cent per year, the spares cost about 43,200 divided by 365 = about GHS 118 per day to carry. Sea's extra wait is 35 minus 6 = 29 days, about GHS 3,430 of extra carrying cost. Air's transport premium is 22,000 minus 4,000 = GHS 18,000, shrinking to roughly GHS 14,570 after the carrying saving.

Step 5: The downtime line. Each day the bottling line stands still costs the customer an illustrative GHS 6,000 of lost production. Sea's 29 extra days therefore cost about 174,000, dwarfing air's GHS 18,000 premium: for this emergency, air wins outright. But for the same spares ordered months ahead for a planned overhaul, the downtime line would disappear and sea's GHS 4,000 would beat air's GHS 22,000.

What does this example demonstrate? The freight difference is one line; time is another. Carrying cost, and where relevant the cost of a stopped operation, belong inside the comparison, and urgency is itself a cost line.

Key lesson: air or sea is a total-cost argument, not a freight-rate argument, and the same shipment can deserve opposite answers at different moments.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: A landed-cost model is only as honest as its tail
BODY:
The most common way a landed-cost calculation misleads is not an arithmetic error; it is an omission at the end. A build that stops at goods, freight, and duty looks respectable and still lies, because the buyer will really pay the lines that come after clearance: the clearing agent's fees, terminal handling, inland transport, storage while goods await collection, and the exposure to time-based charges if anything goes slowly. Lesson 9.4, Demurrage, Detention, and Storage Charges, treated what happens to a container that waits; the next lesson, Lesson 10.3, Storage, Detention, Demurrage, and Other Charges, prices the same family in detail. A build that pretends clearance is instant and storage is free has simply moved those costs off the page, not out of the world.

The discipline is twofold. First, include a realistic tail: the known fees as actual quotations, and an allowance for the unknown, as the worked examples did with their storage and contingency line. Second, state the exclusions next to the total. A figure labelled "landed cost, excluding storage beyond five days and excluding bank charges" is honest even if incomplete; an unqualified total invites decisions made on money that does not exist. When a colleague presents a landed cost, the professional question is not only "what did you include?" but "what did you leave out, and why?"

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Duty and tax figures belong to the tariff law of the day
BODY:
Every duty, tax, and levy figure in this lesson's examples is a labelled placeholder, and the label is the lesson. A template teaches the structure of the computation: goods, freight, and insurance forming the customs value; duty computed on that value at the applicable tariff rate; each further tax and levy computed on its own legally defined base; the assessment generated for payment. The numbers that fill the structure are not teaching material at all. They are set by the tariff schedule and the tax laws in force on the day of assessment, and in Ghana they are computed when the declaration is assessed in ICUMS, with the tax bill paid at a participating bank.

Two cautions follow for any working model. First, never carry a placeholder into a live decision, and never assume yesterday's assessment repeats today's: rates, component lists, and bases change as laws change, and levies in particular are created and renamed over time. The authoritative sources are always the applicable tariff schedule and the revenue authority, the Ghana Revenue Authority in Ghana. Second, the same law-of-the-day caution applies to the freight side: the volumetric conversion factor used in an air computation is the one specified in the applicable tariff or contract, and carriers may publish factors other than the IATA standard. A landed-cost model is therefore a living template with empty cells for the day's numbers, never a finished price list.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Selling on ex-factory cost is how margins die
BODY:
The recurring temptation this lesson exists to dismantle is pricing from the invoice. A trader buys at GHS 240 per carton, adds what looks like a healthy margin, and sells; the freight, the duty, the destination charges, and the tail then take GHS 128.68 per carton that the price never recovered, as the first worked example showed. The business has not priced its product; it has priced its supplier's product and donated the difference. Margin honesty begins with recording inventory at landed cost and quoting the unit landed cost whenever anyone asks what the goods cost.

The same discipline governs purchasing. The second worked example reversed a sourcing decision by GHS 113 per pump, and exchange-rate movement could widen that reversal without either supplier changing a price. A purchasing file that records only quotations records the smallest part of the decision; the file that matters keeps both builds, their dates, and the rates used.

Finally, keep the build alive. Unit landed cost moves when the freight market moves, when the exchange rate moves, when packing density changes, and when clearance slows. The costing habit that protects a business is to re-run the build before each reorder and each price review, so that the number on which pricing, purchasing, and margin reporting all rest is today's number, built today's way, with its tail included and its placeholders clearly marked.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A freight forwarder in Tema receives an LCL consignment of generator parts imported from China for on-carriage to an inland customer: ten pallets measuring 6 cubic metres in total and weighing 4,000 kilograms. The consolidator's tariff charges LCL freight on weight or measurement, whichever is greater.

Which statement correctly describes how the freight charge will be calculated?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. On the gross weight of 4 tonnes, because ocean freight is always calculated on actual weight alone.
2. On 6 revenue tonnes, because the measurement of 6 cubic metres exceeds the weight of 4 tonnes under the weight-or-measurement rule.
3. On 5 revenue tonnes, because the chargeable quantity is the average of the weight and the measurement.
4. On a full-container rate, because every consignment tendered to a consolidator is ultimately charged as a whole container.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Under the weight-or-measurement rule for LCL ocean freight, the consignment is charged on its weight in tonnes or its volume in cubic metres, whichever is greater, and the resulting chargeable unit is the revenue tonne. Here the consignment weighs 4 tonnes and measures 6 cubic metres; measurement is greater, so the charge is computed on 6 revenue tonnes. This mirrors the lesson's fruit-juice example, where 18 cubic metres beat 9 tonnes and set the chargeable quantity.

Answer 1 fails because weight alone is only half of the rule: a dense cargo would indeed pay by weight, but this consignment is bulkier than it is heavy. Answer 3 invents an averaging method that exists in no tariff; the rule takes the greater of the two figures precisely so the carrier is paid for whichever scarce resource, payload or space, the cargo actually consumes. Answer 4 confuses the physical consolidation, in which many LCL consignments share one container, with the charging method, under which each consignment pays its own share by revenue tonne rather than a whole-box rate.

The takeaway: before any freight figure enters a landed-cost build, read the pricing basis, because the basis, not the goods, decides the arithmetic.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
After a costing review, a trainee tells her manager that the customs value shown on the import assessment is simply the landed cost of the shipment under another name, so the assessment figure can be used directly for setting selling prices.

Which statement best describes the relationship between customs value and landed cost?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The trainee is right: customs value and landed cost are two names for the same total, so the assessment can be used directly for pricing.
2. Customs value covers only the transport charges of a shipment, while landed cost covers only the duties and taxes, so together they add up to the total cost.
3. Customs value is the value determined for customs purposes under the applicable valuation rules, on which duty is computed at the applicable tariff rate, while landed cost is the wider business total that also carries freight-side, destination, and tail costs.
4. Customs value is set by whatever figure the seller writes on the commercial invoice, while landed cost is set by the customs authority at the port of discharge.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The customs value answers a legal question: it is the value determined for customs purposes under the applicable valuation rules, starting in general from the transaction value, and duty is computed on it at the applicable tariff rate. The landed cost answers a business question: what did getting these goods to the warehouse, ready for sale, really cost? It contains the consequences of the customs value, the duty and taxes, but also insurance, terminal and handling charges, inland transport, clearance fees, and the cost tail. In Ghana the duty base is the CIF value and the assessment is generated in ICUMS, yet the landed total still carries lines the assessment never shows, which is why the fruit-juice build's landed cost of GHS 368.68 per carton far exceeded the value on which duty was computed.

Answer 1 is the trainee's error restated: the two figures coincide only by accident. Answer 2 swaps the contents of the two concepts entirely. Answer 4 is wrong on both halves: the customs value is determined by rule rather than by whatever the invoice asserts, and the customs authority does not set anyone's landed cost.

The takeaway: use the customs value for the duty computation and the unit landed cost for pricing, and never feed one to the other's decision.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
An importer in Accra purchases a consignment on CIF Tema terms. A colleague preparing the landed-cost spreadsheet adds a line for ocean freight to Tema and another making the seller responsible for paying the import duty in Ghana.

Which option best explains the effect of the Incoterms rule on which cost lines belong in the buyer's build?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Both entries are correct: under CIF the buyer pays the ocean freight to Tema and the seller pays the import duty.
2. Under CIF the seller pays every charge up to the buyer's warehouse, including import duty, terminal handling, and inland transport, so the buyer's build needs no further lines after the goods price.
3. Under CIF the buyer pays no insurance and carries no risk, because the seller's responsibility continues until the goods are discharged at Tema.
4. Under CIF the seller pays freight and minimum insurance to the named destination port, so that cost reaches the buyer inside the goods price and the customs value, while import duty remains the buyer's side and the buyer still bears the transit risk from shipment.
CORRECT: 4
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 4.

CIF, cost, insurance and freight, is an Incoterms 2020 rule under which the seller pays the specified costs, freight, and insurance to the named destination port while risk transfers earlier, when the goods are on board the vessel at the port of shipment. For the landed-cost build this means the ocean freight is not a separate buyer's line; it sits inside the goods price and, in Ghana, inside the CIF value on which duty is computed. Import clearance, duties, and taxes are the buyer's side under every rule except DDP, so the build still needs the duty and tax lines, the destination charges, inland transport, and the tail.

Answer 1 doubles the freight by paying it twice and wrongly hands import duty to the seller. Answer 2 describes something closer to DDP carried to the warehouse, which CIF is not; CIF stops at the named port and only for cost, never for risk. Answer 3 confuses cost with risk: under the C rules the seller pays carriage to destination, but the risk passes to the buyer already at shipment, and the seller's insurance obligation under CIF is only minimum cover for the buyer's benefit.

The takeaway: the Incoterms rule decides which lines move inside the goods price and which stay in the buyer's build; it never deletes them from the total.
