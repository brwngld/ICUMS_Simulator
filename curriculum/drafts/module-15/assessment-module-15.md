=== ASSESSMENT Q1 | tests lesson 15.1 ===
QUESTION:
A national pharmaceutical wholesaler based in Accra supplies hospitals and clinics across the country from its own cold-chain-capable warehouse. Volumes are large and steady throughout the year, the delivery operation is tightly regulated and customer-critical — hospitals depend on daily deliveries — and the company's cold-chain handling and delivery reliability are widely regarded as the reason customers choose it. Capital is available. The finance director proposes outsourcing the entire delivery operation to a general transport provider quoting rates well below the operation's current cost, arguing that transport is never a core activity.

Which option best describes the sound make-or-buy analysis of this proposal?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The outsourcing should proceed, because capability, volume, control and capital are secondary considerations next to the quotation: a provider offering any complete transport service at a lower rate has, by that fact alone, proved itself the better source.
2. The four factors must each be answered honestly, and here they point against full outsourcing: the company already possesses specialist cold-chain capability; its volumes are large and steady enough to keep owned resources busy; the flow is customer-critical and tightly regulated, which argues for keeping it close or holding control through strict performance measures; and available capital weakens the saving argument — so the sounder position is to retain the activity, or outsource only parts of it under measurable commitments.
3. The outsourcing should proceed, because a service contract transfers accountability for delivery performance to the provider, and the wholesaler would no longer need to monitor deliveries or retain internal knowledge of the operation.
4. The decision should turn on the control factor alone: because the flow is regulated, no logistics activity of any kind may ever be outsourced by any business, whatever the other factors show.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The make-or-buy decision is walked through four factors, and rarely does one decide alone. Capability: the wholesaler already performs specialist cold-chain distribution — trained staff, temperature-controlled storage, a delivery record customers buy — so the market is not obviously better here, and the general provider quoting less is pricing a different, simpler service. Volume: large, steady demand keeps owned vehicles and warehouses busy, which is exactly the condition under which ownership earns its keep; it is idle capacity, not ownership itself, that wastes capital. Control: the flow is customer-critical and tightly regulated, and the working rule says keep what you cannot safely surrender control of, or hold control through strict performance measures instead. Capital: with capital available, the saving argument loses the force it carries where ownership would starve the core business. Answer 1 is the price-first reflex: a lower quotation for a different service proves nothing until the services are comparable. Answer 3 repeats the most dangerous sentence in outsourcing — the task transfers, the accountability does not, and the wholesaler would remain answerable for every failed hospital delivery. Answer 4 commits the opposite shortcut: control argues for care, not for an absolute ban, because the discipline is to answer all four factors honestly and respect the result, hybrid positions included.

The takeaway: make-or-buy is a four-factor analysis performed activity by activity — and the lowest quotation is the beginning of the question, not the end of it.

=== ASSESSMENT Q2 | tests lesson 15.1 ===
QUESTION:
A cocoa exporter in Takoradi is selecting a provider for its European export consignments. Before any proposal was opened, the panel agreed a written evaluation framework: criteria drawn from the specification, weighted so that reliability, compliance and systems outrank price; a common scoring scale applied to every bidder; and documented reasoning behind every score. When the weighted totals are tabled, the cheapest bid ranks last of the short list on compliance and systems. The finance director proposes re-weighting the criteria so that price outranks the rest, arguing that the original weights were only a draft and the cheapest bid must be the safe choice.

Which option best assesses the proposal?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The proposal is sound, because weights are only arithmetic and changing them after the scores are known simply corrects a drafting error; the documentation can be updated to match the new totals.
2. The proposal is sound, because price is the only criterion that can be verified objectively, so it must always carry the greatest weight in any evaluation framework.
3. The scoring should be abandoned and the award decided on the reference checks alone, since what customers say is more reliable than any arithmetic.
4. The proposal should be rejected: the weights were agreed before any proposal was opened precisely to keep the evaluation honest, defensible and immune to pressure, and price is considered last — weighed against a genuinely comparable service and its total cost of ownership over the contract term, not against the other bidders' quotations alone.
CORRECT: 4
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 4.

The evaluation framework's whole purpose is to be agreed before any proposal is opened. Criteria drawn from the specification, weights reflecting the service promise, a common scoring scale and documented reasoning are what keep the decision honest under pressure, let the losing bidder be told fairly why they lost, and protect the business if the award is later questioned. Re-weighting after the scores are known reverses that logic: the arithmetic stops being a measurement and becomes a justification for a decision already taken on price. Price, moreover, is considered last by design — meaningful only once the service being priced is genuinely comparable — and the cheapest bid here is the weakest on compliance and systems, exactly the criteria whose failures land on the customer's own shipments. Total cost of ownership asks what that record would cost in claims, expediting and management attention over the term, costs that never appear on a rate card. Answer 1 treats the weights as disposable arithmetic, which is the corruption itself; a framework re-cut to reach a preferred answer was never a framework. Answer 2 is the price-first fallacy: price is no more objective than the evidence behind a reliability or compliance score, and weighting it first recreates the award-on-the-only-number-available failure. Answer 3 discards a documented, comparable process for an impression; reference checks are a verification step inside the framework, not a substitute for it.

The takeaway: weights agreed in advance are the evaluation's integrity — and price is judged last, on total cost over the term.

=== ASSESSMENT Q3 | tests lesson 15.1 ===
QUESTION:
A manufacturer of bottled beverages in Kumasi contracted a third-party logistics provider to run its deliveries to retail distributors. For weeks the provider's vehicles have arrived late and short, and a key retail chain's buyer now telephones the manufacturer to say its stores are being left unstocked, warning that orders will move to a competitor. The logistics manager proposes replying that the delays are entirely the contracted provider's failure, that the buyer should raise them with the provider directly, and that the manufacturer holds no responsibility for work it no longer performs.

Which option best assesses the response?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The response is wrong: outsourcing transfers the task, not the accountability — the end customer experiences the failure as the manufacturer's own, so the manufacturer must manage the provider actively through the agreed measures and remedies, repair the customer relationship, and retain enough internal knowledge to specify, judge and, if necessary, replace the provider.
2. The response is correct: a service contract allocates the customer experience to the provider, so the retail chain's only proper course is to pursue the provider under the provider's own contract.
3. The response is wrong, but for the opposite reason: ownership is the only real form of control, so the manufacturer must cancel the contract and rebuild its own fleet at once, before any customer conversation takes place.
4. The response is correct, provided the manufacturer also withholds the provider's entire invoice for the period, since improvised penalties are the remedy the contract intended for misses of this kind.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

Outsourcing transfers the task, not the accountability. The retail chain's buyer does not contract the provider and does not care who drives the trucks; it experiences the failure as the manufacturer's failure and takes its orders elsewhere — which is why the first response is to establish what happened, hold the provider to the agreed remedies, and repair the only relationship the end customer can see. Managing the provider actively — through the specification, the KPIs, the reporting and the review rhythm — is precisely what the retained accountability requires, together with enough internal knowledge kept inside the business to specify the service, judge the performance and, when necessary, leave the relationship; that knowledge is also the antidote to the dependency that long single-provider operation quietly builds. Answer 2 states the contract-transfer misconception outright: no clause moves the customer's experience to the provider, and pointing the buyer at the 3PL simply confirms that nobody is managing the promise. Answer 3 reaches the opposite conclusion by the wrong route — ownership is only one form of control, and a fleet rebuilt in a crisis would arrive late for the same buyers; the failure indicates weak provider governance, not the absence of painted doors. Answer 4 improvises a penalty the contract did not write; remedies are applied as agreed, and withholding payment outside the agreed machinery teaches both sides that the document is decorative.

The takeaway: outsourcing done well is delegation with control — pointing the customer at the provider is abdication with paperwork.

=== ASSESSMENT Q4 | tests lesson 15.2 ===
QUESTION:
A distributor based in Cape Coast receives a draft service-level agreement containing the commitment: "The provider will achieve an on-time in-full delivery rate of 95 per cent every month." The contract manager declares the commitment now fully measurable and enforceable, because it contains a number and a target. All figures in this question are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Which option best assesses that judgement?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The judgement is correct: a stated percentage is the definition of a measurable commitment, and no further drafting is required once a target appears in the document.
2. The commitment should be strengthened by adding more indicators — a dozen or more KPIs covering every aspect of the service — because a contract carrying many measures cannot be disputed.
3. The judgement is premature: a number alone is not yet a commitment — the clause still needs the indicator's formula, under which an order counts only if delivered both on time and in full; the counting window with rule-bound exclusions; the defined data source and measurer; the reporting cadence; and the pre-agreed remedy that applies when the target is missed. Without the source, every miss becomes an argument about arithmetic; without a remedy, the target is a wish.
4. The judgement is premature because percentages are never enforceable; the clause should be replaced by a general duty to deliver promptly, drafted in legally precise language.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

A number is necessary but nowhere near sufficient. The drafting discipline is that two strangers, given the same data, would score the commitment identically — and this clause fails that test four ways. Its formula is unstated: on-time in-full counts an order only if it arrives both on time and in full, and that strictness must be written down. Its counting window, its exclusions and its reporting cadence are absent. Its data source and measurer — which system of record supplies the numbers, and who computes the score — are absent, and this is the defect that turns every missed month into a dispute: one side's system says the target was met, the other's says it was missed, and the difference is the argument. And no remedy is attached, so a miss changes nobody's behaviour: an SLA without consequences is a wish list. Answer 1 accepts the number as the finish line, mistaking a target for a commitment. Answer 2 reaches for quantity of measurement, but few measures, well chosen, outperform a long list nobody reads — a contract carrying dozens of KPIs is as blind as one carrying none. Answer 4 moves backwards: legal polish cannot make an unmeasurable promise countable, and a general duty to deliver promptly is exactly the aspiration this clause was meant to replace.

The takeaway: the difference between a promise and a commitment is a number, a method, a source and a consequence — all four, or it is none of them.

=== ASSESSMENT Q5 | tests lesson 15.2 ===
QUESTION:
A furniture importer in Accra holds a warehousing and delivery contract whose service-level agreement sets an on-time in-full target, a service-credit schedule that applies only after consecutive months below target, and an escalation ladder: review at the operational meeting, corrective-action plan, service credits, and an exit trigger reserved for persistent failure with no effective plan. This month, for the first time since the contract began, the on-time in-full result falls below target. The commercial manager proposes immediately withholding the whole month's payment and threatening termination, to send the provider a message.

Which option best describes the correct response under the agreement?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The proposal is sound: a first miss should be answered with the strongest available consequence, because severity at the start prevents every later failure.
2. The proposal bypasses the machinery both parties agreed: a first miss is review territory under the ladder — the result is tabled at the operational meeting, the provider's explanation is logged, and a corrective action is agreed and tracked to closure — while the credits wait for the consecutive-miss threshold and the exit trigger waits for sustained, uncorrected failure; improvising harsher measures teaches both sides that the document is decorative.
3. The miss should simply be ignored, because service-level targets are statistical averages that carry no consequence unless they are missed repeatedly for a whole year.
4. The target should be renegotiated downwards at once, so that the provider is not discouraged and the relationship is preserved while performance is restored.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The ladder's value lies in being followed as written, and that discipline condemns improvisation in both directions. A first miss is the ladder's first rung: the result appears in the agreed report, is tabled at the operational meeting, the provider explains, the miss is logged, and a corrective action is agreed and tracked. Many misses end there, which is the design. The service credits wait for the threshold the parties wrote — consecutive months below target — and the exit trigger waits for sustained failure with no effective corrective plan; this scenario meets neither condition. Consequences were priced in advance precisely so that neither mood nor discretion sets them, and the customer who shouts in month one teaches the provider that the document can be renegotiated by volume. Answer 1 is escalation by temper: severity is not a substitute for the procedure, and the strongest consequence deployed first leaves nothing in reserve and no log of what actually happened. Answer 3 errs the other way: a miss on a measured commitment is never mere information — even at step one it must be made visible, explained and corrected, and the issue log exists so that a first miss and a pattern are told apart. Answer 4 rewrites the deal mid-failure; targets are set from evidence just beyond the baseline, and lowering one because it bit once destroys the discipline for every month after.

The takeaway: an SLA is proven in the month the target is missed — the ladder run as written turns failure into procedure instead of conflict.

=== ASSESSMENT Q6 | tests lesson 15.2 ===
QUESTION:
A wholesale business in Tamale receives its provider's monthly report: the on-time in-full and order-accuracy targets have both been missed. The managing director declares the provider in breach of contract, instructs that the dispute-resolution clause be invoked, and asks lawyers to prepare termination letters — treating the missed scores as a legal rupture. The record shows the misses are recent, were explained at the operational meeting, and are covered by corrective-action plans with recovery already visible.

Which option best assesses the managing director's position?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The position is correct: any result below a target stated in the service-level agreement is by definition a breach of a contractual obligation, and escalation to legal mechanisms is the required next step.
2. The position is correct in substance but premature in timing: the lawyers should be engaged and the termination letters prepared the moment the second month's scores arrive, whatever the corrective-action plans show.
3. The position is wrong because service-level targets are voluntary aspirations with no contractual force, so no consequence of any kind can ever follow a missed target.
4. The position confuses two things the contract keeps apart: a missed performance standard is a measured service failure answered through the SLA's machinery — review, corrective-action plan, service credits — while escalation and the dispute provisions are reserved for failures of obligation and for persistent failure that survives correction; treating ordinary misses as legal ruptures destroys the ladder's purpose, which is to keep failures management matters.
CORRECT: 4
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 4.

The distinction between obligations and performance standards runs in both directions. A contractual obligation is a duty to do or not do something — maintain defined insurance, submit the agreed reports, handle goods with care — established by documents and records, and answered through escalation and, if it persists, the dispute and termination provisions. A performance standard is a graduated level to achieve, established by measurement against a defined method, and answered through the SLA's machinery: review, corrective action, service credits. The scenario describes measured misses — recent, explained, with corrective plans and visible recovery — which is ladder territory, not legal rupture. The ladder exists precisely so that ordinary failures stay management matters; escalating to lawyers at the first red month would dismantle the machinery both parties agreed to fund and operate. Answer 1 collapses the standard into the obligation and reads the contract as a trap that springs on any miss. Answer 2 only relocates the trap: termination is governed by the contract's own definition of cause — persistent failure that survives correction — not by a count of red months alone. Answer 3 overcorrects into the opposite defect: targets with no consequences are a wish list, and calling them unenforceable removes exactly the pressure that drives corrective action. One caution completes the picture: the compliance review should also confirm that no duty — insurance, reporting, confidentiality — has failed quietly alongside the red scores, because the two can move in opposite directions.

The takeaway: a missed target is not automatically a broken obligation — and a broken obligation is never measured by the scorecard alone.

=== ASSESSMENT Q7 | tests lesson 15.3 ===
QUESTION:
A fruit-processing exporter in Tamale depends on a single overseas supplier for its packaging materials and moves finished goods to port along one road corridor that floods in the rainy season. Management approves four actions: servicing the vehicles and clearing drainage along the corridor before the rains; qualifying a second packaging supplier and documenting the alternative corridor with its costs, timings and contacts; paying the annual insurance premium under which flood damage to finished stock would be compensated; and, after one flood, pumping out the depot and restoring the damaged section of the route to service.

Which option correctly classifies the four actions?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Vehicle servicing and drainage clearing are risk prevention; qualifying the second supplier and documenting the alternative corridor are contingency planning; the insurance premium is risk transfer; pumping out the depot and restoring the route afterwards is disaster recovery.
2. Vehicle servicing and drainage clearing are contingency planning; qualifying the second supplier and documenting the alternative corridor are risk prevention; the insurance premium is contingency planning; pumping out the depot and restoring the route afterwards is risk prevention.
3. All four actions are risk prevention, because each was arranged before or shortly after the event with the aim of reducing the company's losses.
4. The insurance premium is disaster recovery, because the compensation restores the money after the loss; the other three actions are contingency planning, because all of them prepare alternatives.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

The three operational layers answer different questions on different sides of the event, and the scenario supplies one action for each, plus risk transfer. Servicing vehicles and clearing drainage act before the event to make it less likely or less damaging: risk prevention. Qualifying a second packaging supplier and documenting the alternative corridor assume the event will happen anyway and prepare the ready answer: contingency planning, whose product is not a reduced likelihood but a usable fallback — the qualified alternative, the documented route, priced and contactable in advance. Pumping out the depot and restoring the flooded route after the event is the after-discipline: disaster recovery, the restoration of facilities and operations. The insurance premium is none of the three operational layers: it is risk transfer, compensating the financial loss afterwards while keeping nothing moving, serving no customer and running no system by itself. Answer 2 swaps the two before-the-event layers — the drainage work reduces the likelihood of flooding but prepares no alternative — and files risk transfer under contingency, mistaking a policy for a plan. Answer 3 erases the distinctions the lesson drew, and with them the discipline of asking of every arrangement: does this shrink the odds, shorten the fall, or set the pace of getting up? A business strong in only one layer is prepared for only one half of a disruption. Answer 4 mistakes compensation for restoration: the money returns, but the goods, the customers and the operation do not run on a policy.

The takeaway: prevention changes the odds, contingency prepares the alternative, recovery restores afterwards, and insurance pays — four different purchases a continuity programme buys deliberately.

=== ASSESSMENT Q8 | tests lesson 15.3 ===
QUESTION:
The order-processing system of a wholesale business in Kumasi fails. Before the event, management had set two recovery objectives for the system in writing: the system must be restored and working again within one working day, and it may lose no more than the last hour of recorded transactions. The recovery arrangements, however, run a single backup of the system once every night.

Which option correctly identifies the two objectives and assesses the backup arrangement?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The one-day restoration target is the recovery point objective and the one-hour data-loss limit is the recovery time objective; the nightly backup satisfies both, because a backup made every night is by definition an hourly one.
2. Both objectives are recovery time objectives, because both measure the speed with which the system returns to service; the backup frequency is an operational detail with no connection to either objective.
3. The one-day restoration target is the recovery time objective and the one-hour data-loss limit is the recovery point objective — and the nightly backup cannot honour them: a failure late in the working day puts many hours of transactions at risk, far beyond the one-hour limit, so the backup regime and the recovery arrangements must be redesigned to meet the objectives and then verified by testing.
4. The two objectives were set wrongly, because recovery objectives can only be defined after a failure has shown what is realistically achievable; the nightly backup should continue untouched until then.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The two objectives measure different clocks. The recovery time objective is the targeted maximum period within which the disrupted system should be restored — the one-working-day figure asks how fast the system returns. The recovery point objective is the maximum acceptable amount of data loss measured in time before the disruption — the one-hour figure asks how much of the system's information survives. The nightly backup fails the pairing: a failure late in the working day leaves the last backup at the previous midnight, so the transactions at risk run to many hours, far beyond the limit management set. The objectives come first, agreed before any event; the backup schedule and the recovery arrangements are then designed to meet them, and readiness is proven in test — a failover test measures the achieved restoration time against the recovery time objective, and the backup regime against the recovery point objective. Answer 1 swaps the two definitions and then declares the backup adequate by a definition no lesson teaches. Answer 2 collapses the pair into speed and severs backup frequency from the objectives, when backup frequency is precisely what sets how much data a failure can take between records. Answer 4 inverts the discipline: objectives improvised after a failure are a description of damage, not a commitment — and a business that first meets its disruption on the day it happens is not responding, it is discovering.

The takeaway: the recovery time objective is the clock on restoration, the recovery point objective is the clock on data — both set in writing before the event, both proven in test.

=== ASSESSMENT Q9 | tests lesson 15.3 ===
QUESTION:
An importer of machine parts in Kumasi is reassured by its logistics coordinator: "We are not concentrated — our containers move under three different shipping lines, cleared by two different agents." In fact, all three lines route via the same overseas corridor and discharge at the same port terminal, both agents submit declarations through the same electronic clearance system, and one senior clerk holds the only working knowledge of the transit documentation.

Which option best assesses the reassurance?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The reassurance is correct: dependence is counted by the number of contracts signed, and three shipping lines with two agents is a diversified arrangement that needs no further review.
2. The reassurance is wrong, and the remedy is to eliminate every concentration immediately, whatever the cost, because any concentration at all is a mistake no professional operation may carry.
3. The reassurance is wrong only about the clerk: people can always be replaced at short notice, so they are never single points of failure; the corridor, the terminal and the system are safe because no failure has yet occurred there.
4. The reassurance counts diversification at the wrong level: the concentration map traces each critical activity's dependencies down to the physical chain, so the single corridor, the single terminal, the single clearance system and the clerk's unique knowledge are the true single points of failure — each to be circled, then covered by mitigations whose standing cost is accepted the way an insurance premium is accepted, with the map redrawn annually as the business changes.
CORRECT: 4
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 4.

Dependence is counted at the level of the physical chain, not the number of contracts. The concentration map traces each critical activity's dependencies and circles the ones with no alternative: if all three shipping lines ride the same corridor into the same terminal, a single closure or terminal failure stops every container regardless of whose name is on the bill of lading; if both agents submit through one clearance system, the system is a single point of failure; and people count as much as ports — the clerk's unique knowledge is among the most fragile dependencies on the map. The lesson's warning fits exactly: a business can hold contracts with three carriers and still be fatally concentrated. Answer 1 counts diversification at the wrong level, which is how concentration stays invisible — its savings accrue daily in the cost reports while the exposure is charged in full on one afternoon. Answer 2 over-corrects: concentration is not automatically a mistake, because redundancy costs money and sometimes no alternative exists; the discipline is to name and price the exposure, then buy mitigations deliberately where the impact would concentrate — a qualified alternative corridor, a second clearance route, cross-trained staff, a buffer on the critical lines — accepted the way an insurance premium is accepted, against a rare severe loss. Answer 3 is wrong twice: people are prime single points of failure, and no failure yet is not evidence of no risk — which is why the audit is an annual duty, not a one-off project.

The takeaway: a concentration you have mapped, priced and covered is a managed risk; one you have never drawn is a surprise with your name on it.

=== ASSESSMENT Q10 | tests lesson 15.4 ===
QUESTION:
A beverages exporter based in Tema has moved the steady, forecastable replenishment base of its European flow from air to sea, keeping air freight for genuinely urgent consignments. The marketing team now asks the logistics manager to publish the exact percentage by which the change reduced freight emissions, and to adopt a European retailer's published emissions figures as the company's new targets, since both would make a stronger claim.

Which option best describes the professionally sound response?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Both requests should be accepted: the mode ranking is precise enough to yield exact percentage savings, and figures published by other organisations are the most defensible possible targets.
2. Both requests should be declined: the directional ranking — sea the lowest-emitting main mode per tonne-kilometre, then rail, then road, with air by far the highest — states direction only, because the size of the gaps varies with the vessel, the vehicle, the load carried, the fuel and the method of measurement, so no honest exact figure exists; the company measures its own indicators, such as fuel per tonne-kilometre and the share of tonne-kilometres moved by each mode, and trends them against its own baseline, declining to import numbers produced under other conditions.
3. The publication request should be declined, but for a different reason: no claim of any kind can be made, because the directional ranking of the modes is not reliable knowledge and the change may equally have increased emissions.
4. Both requests should be accepted in part: the percentage can be published provided the company counts only the emissions of vehicles it owns itself, since emissions created by purchased sea freight belong to nobody.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The directional ranking is one of the few environmental claims logistics can state with confidence: per tonne-kilometre, sea is the lowest-emitting of the four main modes, rail is next, road is higher still, and air is by far the highest — so shifting the forecastable base flow from air to sea turns the emissions trend in the right direction, and usually cuts cost in the same stroke. What the ranking does not yield is an exact percentage: the gaps vary with the vessel, the vehicle, the load carried, the fuel and the method of measurement, so any precise figure the manager published would be a decorated number rather than a measurement. The honest instrument is the operation's own trend: fuel per tonne-kilometre, the share of tonne-kilometres moved by each mode, tracked against the company's own baseline in the standard indicator format — a fleet whose fuel per tonne-kilometre falls year on year is measurably improving even if nobody can state its exact tonnage of carbon dioxide equivalents. Answer 1 accepts both decorated claims. Answer 3 denies the one confident direction the lesson teaches; the ordering follows the fuel burned to move a tonne, and its direction does not vary. Answer 4 is the outsourcing self-deception the three scopes dismantle: emissions contracted to a shipping line do not vanish — they move to Scope 3, and a serious footprint counts the freight it buys, not only the vehicles it owns.

The takeaway: measure what you can, trend it honestly, use the directional ranking to point the trend — and decline the number nobody can honestly compute.

=== ASSESSMENT Q11 | tests lesson 15.4 ===
QUESTION:
At a port inspection, an official tells a clearing agent that a consignment of imported cosmetics will be held for a full documentation examination — unless an informal payment is made, after which the consignment will "pass as presented". The agent notices that the packing-list quantities do not match the declared carton count, a genuine discrepancy that should be corrected before release. The company's code of conduct prohibits bribes and facilitation payments.

Which option best describes the situation and the professional response?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The payment demanded would be a bribe, not a facilitation payment: it is sought to change the outcome of the inspection and pass goods that should be examined and corrected — and even a genuine facilitation payment would breach the code, which prohibits both; the response is to decline courteously, escalate through the declared procedure, record the approach factually, and correct the document discrepancy through the proper channel.
2. The payment would be an ordinary facilitation payment, because it is small, routine and common at the post, and it may properly be recorded in the trip expenses as a standard border cost.
3. The payment would be an ordinary facilitation payment, because it would only affect the timing of the release and not the decision itself, and timing alone is never a matter of integrity.
4. The agent should pay to protect the consignment and then say nothing internally, because reporting the approach would expose the company to scrutiny without changing what happened at the post.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

The distinction every professional must be able to state does the classifying here. A facilitation payment expedites a routine, non-discretionary government action whose outcome is already fixed — the documents are in order, the goods are as declared, and the only variable is speed. The payment offered in this scenario is not that: the consignment carries a genuine document discrepancy, the examination is what the process requires, and the money is sought to change the outcome — to pass goods that should be held and corrected. That is a bribe by definition. And even the narrower case would not help: the company code prohibits both bribes and facilitation payments, and the stricter standard that governs the professional is the code, because permitted somewhere is not the test. The response follows the professional sequence: decline courteously, escalate through the declared channel, record the approach factually — and then correct the packing-list discrepancy through the proper process, because the underlying defect does not disappear with the refused payment. Answer 2 misclassifies and launders at once: booking the payment as a standard border cost misstates what happened, and a wrong payment is never repaired by a wrong record. Answer 3 applies the timing rationalisation to a case where timing is not what is on offer — the decision itself is for sale. Answer 4 hides the event entirely; escalation protects the professional as much as the organisation, and an unreported approach teaches the company nothing.

The takeaway: the test is what the payment is for — and ethics has no cost-benefit test, whatever the consignment and whatever the queue.

=== ASSESSMENT Q12 | tests lesson 15.4 ===
QUESTION:
The manager of a regional distribution fleet in Kumasi wants to reduce its fuel consumption and asks how to apply the plan-do-check-act cycle to the problem.

Which sequence best applies PDCA as the module teaches it?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Launch a fuel-awareness campaign across the fleet with posters and a launch event, review the total fuel bill after a year, and repeat the campaign if the figure disappoints.
2. Purchase more efficient vehicles immediately, because equipment is the only real cause of fuel consumption, and compare the new vehicles' consumption with the old fleet's when the first invoices arrive.
3. Measure fuel per tonne-kilometre to establish a baseline, apply root cause analysis to find the biggest underlying cause — for example, a high share of empty return legs — trial a backhaul arrangement on one lane, re-measure against the baseline after an agreed period, then standardise what worked and repeat the cycle on the next-biggest cause.
4. Retrain all drivers once on general fuel-efficient driving, then measure total fuel consumption a single time at the end of the year to record the result of the training.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The cycle is a complete instruction. Plan from measurement: a baseline of fuel per tonne-kilometre, then root cause analysis to find the biggest underlying cause rather than the loudest symptom — here, empty return legs producing kilometres with no freight to show for them, the purest waste in transport. Do on a limited scale: a backhaul arrangement trialled on one lane, not imposed fleet-wide. Check against the baseline: re-measure after an agreed period and compare honestly. Act: standardise what worked into the operating routine, adjust what did not, and restart the cycle on the next-biggest cause. Small, measured, repeated cycles compound; an empty leg fixed permanently saves fuel every week thereafter. Answer 1 is the campaign pattern the module's closing warning dismantles: broad, unmeasured against any baseline, and abandoned when the posters fade — no cause is ever found, so the waste resumes its daily work. Answer 2 buys equipment before measurement: new vehicles cannot fix empty running, because the waste lives in the network, not the engine, and without a baseline even the new fleet's performance cannot honestly be judged. Answer 4 trains everyone once and measures once: it assumes the cause is knowledge when the measured cause may be routing, skips the diagnosis entirely, and a single end-of-year figure provides no before-and-after comparison along the way — the symptom treated, the cause intact.

The takeaway: measure first, fix the cause, trial small, standardise what survives the check, and repeat — the rhythm matters more than the size of any single fix.
