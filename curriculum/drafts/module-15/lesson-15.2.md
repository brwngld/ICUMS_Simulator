=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Contract management and the purpose of contracts
BODY:
The previous lesson, Logistics Strategy and Service-Provider Selection, ended with a decision: an external provider has been chosen to carry part of the logistics operation, on the strength of a specification, a scoring exercise and reference checks. The contract is where that choice becomes a working relationship, and contract management is what keeps the relationship working. Contract management is the administration and monitoring of contractual obligations throughout an agreement's life. A contractual obligation is a duty or responsibility established under a legally binding agreement: an undertaking to perform defined services, to pay agreed charges, to maintain insurance, to report performance, or to keep information confidential. On these definitions, contract management is not producing a signed document. It means everything that happens afterwards — seeing that both parties do what the agreement says, that performance is measured, that failures are corrected, and that the agreement itself is kept current as the business changes.

Contracts are needed in logistics service relationships because promises alone cannot carry an operation. A sound contract does several jobs at once.

1. It converts promises into defined duties, so both sides know precisely what is bought and sold.
2. It records what was agreed, because memories shorten and staff change while the contract endures.
3. It allocates responsibility and risk in advance: who insures, who answers for damage, who carries the cost of delay.
4. It sets the basis of payment — the rates and charges, and the service those prices assume.
5. It establishes accountability, giving the customer a structured route to demand correction instead of a daily quarrel.

Why, then, do logistics contracts fail? Far less often because of the law than because of the gap between what was promised and what was written. Three gaps recur. Vague scope: nobody recorded exactly which activities were included, so work was assumed, omitted or performed twice. Unmeasurable commitments: the document says the provider will perform promptly and professionally, so no failure can ever be demonstrated. And no review mechanism: nothing in the agreement says when the parties will revisit volumes, targets and prices, so the contract hardens while the business keeps moving.

The professional view is therefore that a logistics contract is the relationship's operating system: not a filed certificate of trust, but the agreement the whole service runs on — the document that defines the service, measures it, prices it and provides for its repair.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Contractual obligations and performance standards are different things
BODY:
Two ideas sit close together in every service contract and must be kept apart. A performance standard is a defined level of service quality or operational achievement — for example, that a stated share of deliveries arrives within the agreed window, or that stock records match physical counts within an agreed tolerance. A contractual obligation, by contrast, is a duty to do or not do something at all: perform the services in the scope, handle goods with care, maintain defined insurance, submit monthly reports, keep pricing confidential.

The difference shows in three ways.

1. Nature. An obligation is essentially binary: the insurance is maintained or it is not; the report was sent or it was not. A performance standard is graduated: service was better or worse than the agreed level, by a measured amount.
2. Evidence. Meeting or missing an obligation is established by documents and records. Meeting or missing a performance standard is established by measurement against a defined method and data source.
3. Consequences. A missed standard normally triggers the agreed service remedies — review, corrective action, service credits. An unmet obligation is a failure of contract compliance — adherence to the obligations and requirements established by an agreement — and is handled through escalation and, if it persists, the contract's dispute and termination provisions.

The two ideas also connect. Many contracts provide that persistent failure against a performance standard, left uncorrected, becomes a failure of obligation in its own right: the duty is not merely to deliver quickly but to manage the operation so that the agreed level is sustained. A manager reading a contract should therefore ask two separate questions of every clause. Is this a duty to do something, or a level to achieve? And what happens in this contract when it is not done, or not achieved?

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The anatomy of a logistics services contract
BODY:
Logistics service contracts differ in length and legal detail, but their working parts are broadly consistent. Taught structurally, the essential provisions are these.

1. Parties and definitions. The agreement names the contracting organisations and defines its recurring terms — the services, the premises, business days, data — so that later provisions can be short and precise.
2. Scope of services. What the provider will do, and just as importantly what it will not. Treated in full below.
3. Rates and charges. The prices for each service element, the assumptions behind them, and the rules for surcharges and indexation. Treated in full below.
4. Duration and renewal. How long the agreement runs and how it continues or ends. Treated in full below.
5. Liability and insurance. Who answers for loss or damage, to what agreed extent, and what insurance each party must hold. Treated in full below.
6. Confidentiality. Each party's undertaking to protect the other's commercial information — prices, customer lists, systems, volumes — exchanged in the course of the relationship.
7. Dispute resolution. The process of addressing contractual disagreements through agreed or legally available mechanisms. Well-managed contracts establish an escalation route first: operational discussion, then senior management, and only if those fail, the formal mechanisms. Most commercial disagreements should be settled on the operational ladder long before legal action is contemplated.
8. Termination and exit. The circumstances and procedures for ending the agreement, and the transition duties that follow. Treated in full below.

Around this frame, a service-level agreement usually rides as a schedule or annex: the contract provides the legal structure, and the SLA supplies the measurable heart. The provisions interlock. The scope defines what is measured; the measurements feed the remedies; persistent failure feeds the termination provisions; the renewal decision feeds everything again. A contract should therefore be read as one system, not as a stack of unrelated paragraphs — which is why contract managers insist on reviewing the whole document, not only the clause under dispute.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Scope of services: included work, exclusions, and assumptions
BODY:
The scope of services is the set of activities and deliverables a service provider is required to perform. It is the clause every other clause leans on: the rates pay for the scope, the performance measures judge the scope, and the liability provisions allocate the risks of the scope. A vague scope therefore corrupts everything attached to it.

Good scope discipline, in the same spirit as the lesson on Preparing and Evaluating Logistics Quotations, has three parts.

1. Included activities, stated concretely. Not "warehouse services" but receiving, put-away, storage under defined conditions, picking, packing, dispatch, proof of delivery and returns handling — each with the locations, hours and volumes it assumes.
2. Exclusions, stated honestly. What the provider will not do is as important as what it will: pest control, cleaning, packing materials, after-hours work, haulage beyond named destinations. Exclusions prevent scope creep — the slow accumulation of unpriced duties — and prevent the surprise of work nobody contracted for. An exclusion is not an insult; it is an honest boundary that pricing can respect.
3. Assumptions, stated openly. Contracts are priced against assumptions: weekly order volumes, average lines per order, operating hours, data formats. When the assumptions break, the pricing and the service break with them — which is why change management, treated later in this lesson, exists.

For international flows, one further link matters. The Incoterms 2020 rule chosen in each sales contract allocates the tasks, costs and risk-transfer points between buyer and seller — who arranges carriage, who insures, who clears the goods. The logistics services a provider is hired to perform must be read against those allocations, so that the service scope and the commercial terms describe the same division of work. Incoterms rules do this allocation only: they do not transfer ownership of the goods, fix prices, or themselves constitute a contract of carriage.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Rates, charges, and pricing provisions
BODY:
The pricing provisions answer a deceptively simple question: what does each part of the service cost, and on what assumptions? The previous lesson noted that shippers typically buy capacity in two ways. A spot rate is a one-off price for a single movement at the current market level; a contract rate is an agreed price for a committed volume or period. Core lanes live on contract rates for predictability, and peaks are bought on the spot market. A logistics services contract brings the contract-rate side of that blend into formal terms.

Well-drafted pricing provisions share several features.

1. A rate structure with defined units. Storage per pallet per week, handling per order or per line, delivery per drop or per kilometre — with minimum charges and volume bands stated rather than implied.
2. Tariff references for time-based charges. The charges examined in the lesson on Storage, Detention, Demurrage, and Other Charges — storage beyond agreed free time, detention of vehicles while loading or queueing — arise from time, so the contract should reference the tariff or schedule that defines each charge and states clearly when the clock starts and stops. Surcharges on freight are handled with the same discipline, building on the concepts of the lesson on Shipping Charges, Freight Rates, and Surcharges.
3. Indexation rules, where used. Long agreements face moving fuel and market prices; a pre-agreed formula tied to a published measure adjusts charges without reopening the whole contract.
4. An invoice-checking basis. Charges are verified against the tariff and the measured service before payment — agreed rates matched to services actually delivered.

Unclear pricing provisions do not stay abstract: they surface as invoice disputes, withheld payments and quiet resentment. The test is the one this lesson applies throughout — could two strangers, reading only the document, compute the same charge for the same service?

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Liability, insurance, and force majeure
BODY:
When goods are lost or damaged, or a service failure costs the customer money, the question that decides the commercial outcome is: who carries this loss? Liability, insurance and related provisions answer that question in advance, calmly, instead of leaving it to a crisis.

A liability clause is a contractual provision addressing responsibility for specified losses, damages, or obligations. In logistics contracts such clauses commonly define how responsibility for loss of or damage to goods is approached, and may limit that responsibility to agreed extents or exclude defined categories of loss. An indemnity is an undertaking by one party to compensate another for specified losses or liabilities under applicable conditions — a duty to make good defined losses if defined events occur. The exact working of both depends on the law governing the agreement, which is precisely why such clauses are drafted by qualified advisers and managed by operations. The contract manager's job is not to interpret the law but to know what the clauses require and to see that conduct stays within them.

Insurance expectations connect to liability. Contracts typically require each party to hold defined classes of insurance appropriate to the risks it carries, to keep the cover in force, and to provide evidence of it. How cargo risk is allocated and insured is treated in the lesson on Cargo Insurance and Risk Allocation; the contract's job is structural — to state who must insure, against what, and who must prove it.

Force majeure is a contractual concept addressing certain extraordinary events beyond the parties' control that may affect performance, subject to the agreement and applicable law — events such as natural disasters or conflict, of the kind that has disrupted major trade routes in recent years. Such clauses typically provide that affected obligations may be suspended while the event lasts, with notice duties and, if the disruption is prolonged, a route to exit. These are the provisions nobody reads in a good year and everybody reads in a bad one.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Termination, renewal, and exit planning
BODY:
Every contract has a life cycle, and the provisions governing its end deserve the same care as those governing its start.

A termination clause is a contractual provision specifying the circumstances and procedures for ending an agreement. Structurally, two routes recur. Termination for cause permits ending the agreement when the other party fails fundamentally — persistent unremedied service failure, breach of obligation, insolvency. Termination for convenience permits either party to end the agreement without fault, on agreed notice — a recognition that businesses change, and that a relationship which no longer serves either side should end in order rather than in explosion. The clause also sets procedure: how notice is given, how long it runs, and what must happen during it.

Contract renewal is the extension or continuation of a contractual relationship under agreed conditions. Renewal can happen by drift — the agreement rolls on because nobody acted — or by decision, taken on evidence. The management discipline is to treat renewal as a fresh, evidence-based decision: performance against the SLA across the term, a market comparison from time to time, and the dependency risks the previous lesson described. Some agreements renew automatically unless notice is given by a stated date; a missed date can lock the parties in for another term, which is why renewal dates belong in the contract calendar rather than in anyone's memory.

Exit planning completes the set. A well-built contract provides for transition assistance when the agreement ends for any reason: handover of data and records, verified stock counts, reasonable support for a successor provider, and settlement of outstanding payments. These provisions are negotiated at the start precisely because no one wants to discuss them at the end — at signature there is goodwill, time and leverage; at exit there is usually none of the three. Exit terms also age: stock volumes grow, systems change, records multiply, so the exit plan is one of the items the review cycle, treated below, keeps current.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The service-level agreement: the measurable heart of the contract
BODY:
A service-level agreement, or SLA, is an agreement defining specified service expectations, performance measures, and responsibilities. In logistics practice it usually takes the form of a schedule or annex to the main contract: the contract supplies the legal frame, and the SLA is its measurable heart — the part that turns "good service" into defined, counted expectations.

The previous lesson listed the SLA's components; this lesson treats them as a working structure.

1. Scope of services. What is being measured — the activities, locations and flows covered, consistent with the contract's scope clause.
2. Indicators and targets. The key performance indicators, each with a target: on-time in-full delivery, order accuracy, dock-to-stock time, inventory record accuracy, claim rates, response times.
3. Measurement and reporting arrangements. Who measures, from which system of record, by which method, and how often results are reported. This component decides whether the SLA can function at all.
4. Remedies and service credits. The pre-agreed consequences when targets are missed, and the escalation that follows persistent failure.
5. Governance and review cycle. The meetings that review performance, and the scheduled reviews that revisit targets, volumes and assumptions.
6. Exit and transition terms. What happens if the relationship ends — consistent with the contract's termination provisions.

Each component answers one question: what do we expect, how will we know, who measures, what happens when it is missed, and when do we revisit? Two failures destroy an SLA from opposite ends. An SLA without consequences is a wish list: targets with no remedies change nobody's behaviour. An SLA without a defined data source is an argument: if the parties measure differently, every month's score is a negotiation rather than a fact. The measure, the source and the consequence stand or fall together — the drafting discipline the next key concept examines element by element.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Writing measurable commitments: indicators, targets, and measurement sources
BODY:
A key performance indicator is a measurable indicator used to assess progress towards a specified objective. In an SLA, the indicators are usually drawn from the service measures of the lesson on Logistics Performance Indicators and KPIs: on-time in-full delivery — computed as orders delivered both on time and in full, divided by total deliveries, multiplied by 100, and strict enough that an order fails if it is late or short; order accuracy; dock-to-stock time, the elapsed time from arrival at the receiving dock until goods are available to pick; inventory record accuracy; claims and damage ratios; and the timeliness of documents such as proof of delivery.

The drafting discipline is this: write every commitment so that two strangers, given the same data, would score it identically. A commitment that depends on whose memory is consulted is not a commitment. Each measured commitment therefore needs five elements.

1. The indicator and its formula, defined so that counting is mechanical.
2. The counting window — monthly, per lane, per site — with any exclusions stated and rule-bound, for example delays documented as customer-caused under an agreed procedure.
3. The target, set from evidence. Targets are tied to the parties' own measured baseline — set just beyond it, in the direction the service promise requires — not borrowed from another company's benchmark, which no one can verify applies here.
4. The data source and the measurer. Which system of record supplies the numbers — the provider's warehouse or transport system as described in the lesson on Transport and Warehouse Management Systems, or the shared tracking described in the lesson on Shipment Visibility, Tracking, and Automation — and who computes the score.
5. The reporting cadence — when results are published, and to whom.

Two cautions complete the discipline. Few measures, well chosen, outperform a long list nobody reads; and because people manage what is measured, a badly designed measure gets gamed — reward only on-time arrivals and goods will be held back to make the window. The measure must reflect the promise the customer actually bought.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Remedies and service credits: consequences for missed performance
BODY:
A service credit is a contractual adjustment or credit provided when specified service commitments are not met — in effect, a pre-agreed price reduction for a period in which the service fell short of the promise. Its purpose is not punishment. Pre-agreed consequences price the failure in advance, keep both sides out of improvised penalties, and give the provider a financial reason to invest in correction rather than explanation.

Mature SLAs arrange consequences as an escalation ladder, stepped to the persistence and seriousness of failure. The steps, in the order they are normally written:

1. Review. The miss is visible — reported in the agreed format — and discussed at the operational meeting. Many misses end here: explained, logged, corrected.
2. Corrective-action plan. Where failure repeats, the provider documents the root cause, the fix, the owner and the dates, and progress against the plan is tracked at every meeting until closure.
3. Service credits. Where misses persist beyond the agreed threshold — commonly, consecutive months below target — credits are applied per the schedule in the SLA. The schedule is stated in advance: which indicator, how far below target, what adjustment.
4. Exit trigger. Persistent failure that survives correction becomes grounds for termination for cause under the contract — the ladder's last rung, connected to the termination provisions treated earlier.

The ladder's value lies in being followed as written. Improvised responses — a shouted threat one month, a waived credit the next — teach both sides that the document is decorative. Applied as written, the ladder converts failure from conflict into procedure: the customer enforces without escalating personally, and the provider corrects without renegotiating its survival each month. Some agreements add the mirror image — incentives for sustained outperformance — and that is optional. The remedies ladder is not. An SLA whose consequences have never been applied should prompt a question: is the service excellent, or is nobody measuring?

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Governance and monitoring: keeping the contract alive
BODY:
Contract compliance is adherence to the obligations and requirements established by an agreement. Compliance is demonstrated, not assumed: performance is reviewed against the SLA with evidence, using the measured data of the lesson on Logistics Performance Indicators and KPIs rather than impressions exchanged in corridors.

The working rhythm has two clocks.

1. The operational clock. Regular meetings — commonly weekly or monthly — where the period's results are tabled, misses are explained, the issue log is worked through, and corrective actions are tracked to closure. An issue log is deliberately simple: every raised problem is recorded, owned, dated and closed. The pattern that matters is closure — issues that are logged and never closed are the leading indicator of a relationship that will end badly.
2. The review clock. Periodic business reviews — commonly quarterly — at a more senior level: trends rather than incidents, root causes rather than symptoms, and the health of the relationship itself — candour in meetings, responsiveness when things go wrong, stability of the provider's staff. This is the rhythm the previous lesson introduced as provider governance; under a contract it becomes the mechanism by which the SLA actually operates.

Alongside the rhythm stand two practices. Change management: volumes shift, lanes shift, customers arrive and leave — so a variation process allows the parties to amend scope, volumes, rates or targets by documented, signed agreement, keeping the contract true without tearing it up. Undocumented arrangements — the informal favour, the side email — corrode exactly the clarity the contract exists to create. And the scheduled review of the whole agreement — typically annual — at which assumptions, targets, rates and exit plans are revisited against evidence. Reviews are maintenance, not ceremony: the review cycle is the contract's heartbeat, and a contract whose reviews are skipped goes stale silently. This discipline of structured, evidence-based review is also where contract management meets the continuous improvement treated in this module's final lesson, Sustainability, Ethics, and Continuous Improvement.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Rewriting a vague commitment: a consumer-goods distributor in Kumasi
BODY:
A distributor of packaged consumer goods based in Kumasi contracts a regional provider to run its warehouse and deliver to wholesale customers in Kumasi, Sunyani and Tamale. The provider's draft contains this commitment: "The provider will deliver orders on time at competitive rates and manage the warehouse to a high standard." The distributor's contract manager rewrites it before signature. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: Read the clause as a stranger would. When is "on time"? Competitive against what? High by which standard? Every phrase requires a judgement nobody has defined, so no failure could ever be demonstrated — the gap between promised and written, captured in one sentence.

Step 2: Decompose the promise into service elements. Delivery reliability; order correctness; speed of putting received goods away; trustworthiness of stock records. Four elements, four commitments.

Step 3: Give each element an indicator and formula. On-time in-full delivery: orders delivered both on time and in full, divided by total deliveries, multiplied by 100 — strict, so an order that is early but short still fails. Order accuracy: orders delivered without item or quantity error, divided by total orders, multiplied by 100. Dock-to-stock time: hours from vehicle arrival at the receiving dock until goods are available to pick. Inventory record accuracy: counted locations matching the record within tolerance, divided by locations counted, multiplied by 100.

Step 4: Set targets from the baseline. A three-month transition period is measured first. Illustrative results are on-time in-full of 88 per cent, order accuracy of 97 per cent, dock-to-stock of 30 hours and record accuracy of 96 per cent. Targets are set just beyond the baseline — illustratively, 92 per cent, 98 per cent, 24 hours and 98 per cent for the first contract year — not copied from an industry table nobody can verify.

Step 5: Name the data source, the measurer and the cadence. Dispatch and delivery confirmations come from the provider's warehouse system; proof-of-delivery timestamps fix delivery times; the provider measures and reports monthly to the distributor, and the operational meeting reviews the results.

Step 6: Attach the remedy. If monthly on-time in-full falls below target for two consecutive months, the SLA's service-credit schedule applies and a corrective-action plan is required — the ladder treated in the key concept on remedies and service credits.

What does this example demonstrate?
The before-and-after of drafting discipline. Key lesson: the difference between a promise and a commitment is a number, a method, a source and a consequence.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: The escalation ladder applied: an electronics distributor in Singapore
BODY:
A consumer-electronics distributor in Singapore contracts a regional provider for warehousing and next-day island-wide delivery. The SLA sets on-time in-full delivery at a target of 95 per cent, measured monthly from the provider's system records and proof-of-delivery data, with a service-credit schedule and an escalation ladder agreed in advance. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: Month one — a miss. On-time in-full is reported at 92 per cent. The miss appears at the monthly operational meeting, as the ladder's first step requires: the provider explains a driver shortage, the miss is logged, and corrective action is agreed for the rosters. No credit yet — one month below target is review territory under the agreed ladder.

Step 2: Month two — the pattern. On-time in-full is 93 per cent. Two consecutive months below target is the threshold written in the SLA, so the ladder is followed as written. The provider submits a corrective-action plan within the agreed period: root cause identified as picking backlogs after promotional volume spikes, the fix is extra picking capacity in promotion weeks, and the owner and dates are named. The service credit for the month is applied to the next invoice per the schedule — without argument, because the schedule was agreed when nobody was failing.

Step 3: Month three — the trend. On-time in-full is 94 per cent: still below target, so the credit applies again under the schedule, but the weekly reviews show the plan working, with promotion-week misses falling. The ladder distinguishes persistence from recovery: the exit trigger requires sustained failure with no effective plan, not a recovery in progress.

Step 4: Month four — recovery verified. On-time in-full reaches 96 per cent. The issue is closed at the operational meeting; the next quarterly business review records the episode, the amended picking process and the updated issue log. Nothing was renegotiated mid-failure; the document did the work.

Step 5: The road not taken. Consider the same two months managed without a ladder: an angry telephone call in month one, all payment withheld in month two, a threat of termination in month three — while the picking backlog quietly continued. Improvisation converts a fixable backlog into a relationship crisis.

What does this example demonstrate?
Consequences agreed in advance let both parties walk a failure back to health. Key lesson: an SLA's value is proven not in the month the targets are met but in the month they are missed — followed as written, the ladder turns failure into procedure instead of conflict.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: The annual review that mattered: a frozen-fish processor in Tema
BODY:
A frozen-fish processor in Tema holds a three-year contract with a cold-chain provider: store frozen fish at defined temperatures in the provider's cold store, and distribute to retail depots in Accra, Kumasi and Takoradi. The SLA's targets and the rate schedule were built on agreed baseline volumes — illustratively, 400 pallets stored and three dispatch runs a week. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: Growth arrives. The processor wins two supermarket chains, and volumes rise by roughly 40 per cent within a year, with some weeks running double dispatches. Nobody reneges on anything; the contract simply no longer describes the operation.

Step 2: The assumptions break quietly. The cold store fills towards capacity, so receiving slows and dock-to-stock time stretches beyond its target. Dispatch density rises above the volumes the rate bands assumed. On-time in-full begins to slip as the provider juggles space. Both parties are quietly losing: the provider absorbs costs the rates never priced, while the customer's measured service decays.

Step 3: The scheduled annual review happens. It would have been easy to skip — the relationship is friendly and both sides are busy. Instead, the review goes ahead with evidence tabled: twelve months of volumes, capacity use and SLA results.

Step 4: The contract is re-scoped through its variation process. The parties sign a documented amendment. Volume commitments and guaranteed cold-store capacity are restated for the new baseline. Additional dispatch capacity is added. The dock-to-stock target is reset to the new reality and then stepped down as process changes land. Rate bands are re-cut for the higher volumes — the processor pays a lower rate per pallet at the new scale while the provider commits the capacity. The exit provisions are refreshed too, because stock holdings and data records have grown with the business.

Step 5: The counterfactual. Had the review been skipped, each side would have kept silently repricing the relationship — the provider in thinner service, the customer in quieter resentment — until the failure surfaced as a crisis, with exit negotiated in anger at the worst possible moment.

What does this example demonstrate?
Review as maintenance, not ceremony. Key lesson: the review cycle is the contract's heartbeat — a contract kept current costs a meeting; a contract gone stale costs the relationship.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: An SLA without consequences is a wish list; without a data source it is an argument
BODY:
Two defects kill service-level agreements from opposite ends, and both are common. The first is the SLA without consequences: a page of targets — delivery on time, accuracy high, response swift — with no remedy attached to any of them. It reads well and changes nothing, because nothing happens when a target is missed; it is a wish list, not an agreement. The second is the SLA without a defined data source: targets exist and remedies exist, but nobody specified which system supplies the numbers or who measures. The first missed month becomes a dispute about arithmetic instead of a fact — one side's system says 93 per cent, the other's says 96, and the difference is the argument.

The remedy is structural, and it is the one this lesson has built: every commitment needs its indicator, its formula, its source, its measurer and its consequence, or it does not belong in the agreement. A related warning completes the set: the signed-but-shelved contract. Some organisations manage their contracts with a single act — the signature — and a file copy. Measuring is not managing: the reports must be read, the meetings must happen, the ladder must be followed, or the machinery exists only on paper. The test of contract management is not the quality of the document but the routine that operates it.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: A missed target is not the same as a broken obligation
BODY:
This lesson's most practical distinction bears consolidating, because practitioners blur it weekly. A missed performance target is a measured service failure, answered through the SLA's machinery: review, corrective action, service credit. A broken contractual obligation — insurance allowed to lapse, reports never sent, confidential prices disclosed — is a failure of contract compliance, answered through escalation and, if it persists, the dispute and termination provisions. The two can move in opposite directions: a provider can hit every monthly indicator while quietly breaching obligations, which is why contract compliance reviews cover duties as well as scores; and a missed target handled properly through the ladder is not automatically a legal rupture — the ladder exists precisely so that ordinary failures stay management matters rather than becoming disputes.

One boundary must also be drawn honestly. This lesson teaches contract management as a commercial and operational discipline: what the provisions are for, how commitments are made measurable, how performance is governed. It teaches no country's law, and its structural descriptions are not legal advice. Contracts are drafted and interpreted under applicable law by qualified advisers; the logistics professional's contribution is to know what the agreement requires operationally, to keep evidence, and to recognise when a question has left the operations meeting for the lawyers.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Exit clauses are written at the start because no one will discuss them at the end
BODY:
Exit and termination provisions look morbid at signature, which is exactly why they must be agreed then. At the start of a relationship there is goodwill, time and negotiating balance; at the end there are usually none of the three. A transition worked out calmly at signing — data and records handed over, stock counted and released, reasonable assistance given to a successor — becomes an improvised standoff when first discussed during a failure. The same logic keeps exit terms under maintenance: stock holdings grow, systems change, records multiply, so the exit plan agreed three years ago may no longer describe what would actually have to be handed over.

The wider habit these provisions teach is that contracts are living documents. The review cycle keeps assumptions current; the variation process keeps scope and rates true; the exit plan stays usable. A contract allowed to go stale does not merely underperform — it becomes a continuity risk, because a business depending on an outdated agreement has no tested answer for the day the agreement and the reality finally part company. That continuity question — how a logistics operation keeps running through disruption, including the disruption of a failing provider relationship — is the subject of the next lesson, Business Continuity and Supply Chain Resilience.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A manager of a Kumasi-based distribution company receives a draft logistics contract containing the commitment: "The provider will deliver orders promptly and keep storage charges competitive." She asks her team how the commitment should be strengthened before signature.

Which option best describes the change that would make this commitment manageable?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The commitment should be shortened, because shorter clauses leave less room for disagreement about their meaning.
2. The commitment should state the indicator, the measurement formula, the system the data comes from, and the remedy that applies when the target is missed.
3. The commitment should be reviewed by a lawyer, who will define "promptly" and "competitive" in legally precise language.
4. The commitment should be moved into a cover letter, so the main contract stays simple and easy to read.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

A commitment becomes manageable when a stranger, given the same data, would score it identically every time. That requires the full drafting discipline of this lesson: a named indicator with its formula — for example, on-time in-full delivery computed from delivery confirmations — a defined data source and measurer, a target set from the parties' own baseline, and a pre-agreed remedy when the target is missed. A promise carrying a number, a method, a source and a consequence can be tracked, enforced and improved.

Answer 1 fails because brevity is the problem, not the cure: a short vague clause is still unmeasurable. Answer 3 mistakes the discipline: legal review serves legal effect, but no amount of legal polish makes "promptly" countable — what is missing is measurement, not wording. Answer 4 weakens the commitment further by moving it outside the enforceable agreement.

The takeaway: if a commitment cannot be measured from a defined source, it is a preference, not a contract term.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
A frozen-food importer in Tema holds a warehousing and distribution contract whose service-level agreement sets an on-time in-full target, a service-credit schedule, and an escalation ladder: review, corrective-action plan, credits for consecutive misses, and an exit trigger reserved for persistent failure with no effective corrective plan. The provider has now fallen below the on-time in-full target for two consecutive months, and a corrective-action plan is in place with evidence of recovery under way.

Which statement best describes how the customer should respond?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Terminate the contract immediately for cause, because two missed months prove the provider cannot recover.
2. Apply the escalation ladder as written: apply the service credit for the consecutive misses, track the corrective-action plan to closure, and reserve termination for persistent failure with no effective plan.
3. Waive the credit this month to preserve goodwill, and ask the provider to try harder next quarter.
4. Renegotiate the on-time in-full target downwards so the provider can meet it while the recovery continues.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The ladder's value lies in being followed as written. Two consecutive months below target is exactly the threshold the SLA anticipated, so the agreed consequences follow: the service credit is applied per the schedule, the corrective-action plan is tracked at the meetings until it closes, and recovery is verified with the measured data. The exit trigger stays reserved for its defined condition — sustained failure that survives correction — which this scenario, with a plan in place and recovery evident, does not meet.

Answer 1 bypasses the agreed machinery and contradicts the contract's own definition of cause. Answer 3 improvises: waiving credits teaches both sides that the document is decorative, and goodwill is preserved by procedure, not by exceptions. Answer 4 quietly rewrites the deal in the provider's favour, destroying the discipline that targets be set from evidence.

The takeaway: consequences agreed in advance only work if they are applied as agreed — in failure as reliably as in success.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A pharmaceutical distributor in Accra reviews its cold-chain distribution contract at year end. The provider's on-time in-full and temperature-excursion indicators have met their targets every month. However, during the year the provider stopped submitting the agreed monthly temperature-report summaries, and allowed a required insurance certificate to lapse for part of the year.

Which statement best describes this situation?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The contract is in full compliance, because performance against the indicators is the only measure of compliance in a service contract.
2. The reporting and insurance duties are courtesies outside the contract, so only the missed indicators could ever carry consequences.
3. The performance targets were met, but contractual obligations were not: compliance covers duties as well as scores, so the reporting and insurance failures require escalation even though the indicators are green.
4. The situation shows the SLA is worthless and should be replaced with a purely legal agreement enforced through the courts.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

This lesson's central distinction is between contractual obligations — duties to do or not do something, such as submitting agreed reports and maintaining required insurance — and performance standards, the measured levels the SLA tracks. The provider met the standards but failed the duties. Contract compliance is adherence to all the obligations and requirements of the agreement, so a green scorecard does not certify a compliant contract: obligation failures are established by records and escalation, not by the indicator report.

Answer 1 fails because it collapses compliance into measurement, ignoring exactly the duties that would protect the distributor if a cold-chain claim ever arose. Answer 2 is wrong because reporting and insurance duties sit inside the agreement as obligations, with their own consequences. Answer 4 overreacts: the SLA and the obligations machinery work together, and the response to obligation failure is escalation under the contract, not abolition of measurement.

The takeaway: judge a contract relationship on duties kept as well as targets hit.
