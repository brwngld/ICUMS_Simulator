=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: What a logistics strategy is
BODY:
Every operational detail in this programme — the delivery standards of the lesson on the Seven Rights of Logistics, the service-level targets of the lesson on Inventory Optimisation and Service Levels, the cost components of the lesson on Components of Total Logistics Cost — was decided somewhere, by someone, on purpose. Strategy is the level where those decisions are made.

A logistics strategy is a long-term approach to organising logistics resources and activities to support defined business objectives. In plainer terms, it is the deliberate answer to three questions. What service do we promise our customers? Through what structure — our own people and assets, contracted providers, or a blend — will we deliver that promise? And at what cost, so that the promise remains profitable?

The word strategy places the activity inside strategic planning, the process of establishing long-term objectives and determining the actions and resources required to achieve them. Logistics strategy is that planning applied to the movement and storage of goods: the business decides where it is going, and the strategy decides what logistics must be to get it there.

The test of a good strategy is strategic alignment — the consistency between operational decisions and broader organisational objectives. A business whose promise is speed cannot run a structure built to minimise cost per load above all else; a bulk trader whose customers accept weekly deliveries should not pay for a same-day machine. The lesson on the Seven Rights of Logistics listed what every delivery must get right; the strategy decides which of those rights this particular business will guarantee most firmly, and what it will pay to guarantee them.

A logistics strategy is therefore not a slogan on a shelf. It is a position, visible in concrete commitments: which vehicles are owned, where warehouses stand, which providers hold contracts, and which performance measures those contracts carry. This lesson builds that position — first its decisions, then the discipline of selecting the providers who will carry much of it.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Customer requirements shape the strategy
BODY:
Logistics strategy starts outside the logistics department. It starts with what the customer has been promised, because the promise dictates the structure.

Consider two businesses. A retailer promising same-day delivery of consumer orders needs dense local coverage, small vehicles moving constantly, tight order cut-off times, and systems that show every order's status hour by hour. A trader selling cement in bulk, delivered in full loads to construction sites with agreed lead times, needs a completely different machine: large vehicles, port or plant loading capacity, and disciplined scheduling of fewer, bigger deliveries. Neither structure is better: each is right for its promise, and absurd for the other.

Customer requirements reach the strategy through several channels: the speed customers expect, the sizes and frequency of their orders, the condition the goods must arrive in (frozen fish, medicines and machine parts demand very different care), the geography that must be covered, and the information customers expect — proof of delivery, shipment tracking, accurate documents.

The lesson on Inventory Optimisation and Service Levels showed how service-level targets translate customer expectations into measurable commitments, such as dependable product availability. The logistics strategy does the same for the whole operation: it takes the service promise, quantifies it, and then designs the structure — network, assets, providers — that can hit those targets at a cost the business can afford.

Two disciplines follow. First, the promise must drive the structure, not the other way round: a strategy copied from another company fails quietly because the promises differ. Second, the promise must be affordable: the strongest strategies align promise, structure and cost so the business can keep the promise profitably, year after year.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The major strategic decisions in logistics
BODY:
A logistics strategy becomes real through a small set of large decisions, taken together. The principal ones are these.

1. Which activities to own and which to outsource
Transport, warehousing, forwarding, fleet maintenance — for each, the business asks whether to perform it with its own people and assets or buy it from the market. This make-or-buy decision carries the whole weight of outsourcing, and deserves its own treatment below.

2. Centralise or decentralise the network
How many warehouses and distribution points, and where? More, deeper networks place stock closer to customers and shorten final deliveries, but add facilities, inventory and fixed cost. A centralised network does the reverse: cheaper to run, but goods travel further to reach the customer. The right answer depends on the service promise — the same-day retailer and the bulk trader of the previous section would centralise at opposite ends.

3. Asset-heavy or asset-light
Should the business own vehicles and warehouses, or rent access to other people's capacity? Owning brings control and committed capacity, but locks capital into assets that must be kept busy. Asset-light operation keeps capital free and flexes with demand, but depends on the market's willingness to sell that capacity when it is needed.

4. Single provider or several
Concentrating volumes with one provider buys scale, leverage and simplicity; spreading them across several buys resilience and choice. This trade-off — and the risks of concentrating too far — is examined at the end of the lesson.

5. Where to position cost and service quality
Cost management and service quality are strategic positions, not accidents: the strategy decides which service elements the business competes on and which costs it manages hardest, because maximising both everywhere produces an operation that is expensive without being excellent.

These decisions interlock: an outsourcing choice changes the network options; a network choice changes what providers are needed. Together they form the structure half of the strategy. The first — own or outsource — shapes all the others, so it comes next.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: In-house or outsourced: the make-or-buy logic
BODY:
Logistics outsourcing is the use of external service providers to perform logistics functions. Its most common vehicle is third-party logistics — 3PL — the provision of outsourced logistics services such as transport, warehousing and forwarding by an external organisation, introduced in the lesson on Types of Logistics: Inbound, Outbound, Reverse, and Third-Party. Above the 3PL sits fourth-party logistics — 4PL — an arrangement in which a provider coordinates or integrates multiple logistics resources and service providers on behalf of the customer, typically owning few or no transport and warehouse assets: a 3PL operates logistics for you, while a 4PL manages the whole network, including the 3PLs.

Whether to outsource at all is the make-or-buy decision, and it should be walked through four factors rather than decided by habit.

1. Capability
Can we perform this activity as well as a specialist would? Providers move many customers' goods through shared networks, spreading fixed costs, employing trained specialists and running professional systems; a manufacturer's core excellence is manufacturing, and transport is the provider's core.

2. Volume
Is our volume large and steady enough to keep owned resources busy? Own vehicles and warehouses earn nothing when they stand idle, and idle capacity is paid for either way. Seasonal peaks and troughs argue for buying capacity only when it is needed.

3. Control
How much direct control does this activity genuinely need? The more strategic or sensitive the flow — customer-critical deliveries, tightly regulated goods — the stronger the case for keeping it close, or at least for holding control through strict performance measures rather than through ownership.

4. Capital
What would ownership tie up? Vehicles, warehouses and equipment consume capital that could fund the core business, and bring maintenance, insurance and replacement obligations; outsourcing converts that commitment into a purchasable service.

Rarely does one factor decide alone: the discipline lies in answering all four honestly for each activity, then respecting the answer — as the first worked example of this lesson does, end to end.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: What to outsource, what to keep, and the hidden costs
BODY:
The make-or-buy factors compress into a working rule. Outsource what the market can do better or cheaper at scale, and what is not your strategic core. Keep what differentiates you from competitors, and what you cannot safely surrender control of. A cocoa exporter's differentiating capability is its buying and quality system, not its truck fleet; a pharmacy chain's differentiating capability may include the cold-chain handling its medicines depend on.

But outsourcing is not free, and a strategy that counts only the invoice has missed three hidden costs.

1. Coordination
Every outsourced activity adds a party whose work must be specified, scheduled and checked. The lesson on Coordinating Carriers, Agents, and Regulatory Authorities showed how much communication a single international movement already demands; each new provider multiplies the contracts, data exchanges and meetings that hold the operation together.

2. Reduced visibility
Work done in-house can be watched directly. Work done by a provider is seen through reports and systems — or not at all. Outsourcing without visibility arrangements is outsourcing blind.

3. Dependency
Depend on a provider long enough and the internal skills to specify, judge and replace that provider quietly fade. The knowledge needed to run the activity migrates out of the business, and with it the power to leave the relationship.

Most businesses therefore live in the hybrid middle: keeping in-house the activities that are core, sensitive or short-notice, and contracting out the predictable, scale-dependent work. The same blending applies to how they buy capacity — core lanes under contract rates for committed volumes, with peaks covered by spot purchases at current market prices. Ownership versus outsourcing is not a yes-or-no vote taken once. It is a position, maintained activity by activity.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Specification first, then the stages of selection
BODY:
Once the strategy says "buy", the discipline of service-provider selection begins — the process of evaluating and choosing external organisations to deliver specified services. It rests on one uncomfortable truth: you cannot evaluate what you have not specified.

Specification means writing down, before any provider is contacted, what is actually being bought: the scope of services (which activities, which stages); the lanes and routes (origins, destinations, frequencies); the volumes (now and expected over the contract term); the service promise (delivery windows, condition requirements such as cold chain, information needs); and the commercial horizon (contract length, expected growth). A weak specification lets every bidder price a different imaginary service, produces bids that cannot be compared, and saves its surprises for the first month of operation, when they are most expensive.

With the specification written, selection follows recognisable stages.

1. Specify needs
The scope, volumes, service promise and evaluation priorities, in writing.

2. Scan the market and build a long list
Identify every plausible provider from industry contacts, associations and existing relationships — a broad first list, with judgement applied only for basic plausibility.

3. Short-list and invite proposals
Screen the long list against minimum requirements — capability, coverage, licences — and issue the surviving short list a formal request. A request for quotation, or RFQ, invites suppliers to submit prices and commercial terms for specified requirements, and suits standardised, fully specified services. A request for proposal, or RFP, invites suppliers to propose solutions, service approaches and commercial arrangements, and suits complex services where the how matters as much as the price. Either way, every bidder receives the same scope and the same questions — the quotation discipline taught in the lesson on Preparing and Evaluating Logistics Quotations, applied at contract scale.

4. Evaluate the proposals
Scored against criteria agreed in advance — the subject of the next section.

5. Verify what the paper claims
Reference checks and site visits, before commitment rather than after.

6. Contract, implement, and monitor
The award is not the finish: the measures and review rhythm that follow are where selection succeeds or quietly fails.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Evaluation criteria: price comes last, not first
BODY:
Supplier evaluation is the systematic assessment of a supplier's capabilities, performance, costs and suitability. Its quality depends almost entirely on the criteria chosen and the order they are considered in — and five areas the syllabus names outright, cost, reliability, capacity, compliance and service quality, each appear below. The professional criterion set covers seven areas.

1. Capability and coverage
Does the provider actually perform the services in the specification — the right vehicles, warehouses, handling equipment and skilled people — across the required geography? Include capacity: enough equipment and staff not only for today's volumes but for seasonal peaks and expected growth. A provider that cannot scale will serve the first year well and disappoint the third.

2. Reliability and track record
What has this provider actually delivered before, for customers like us, on lanes like ours? References, contract history and performance evidence matter more than promises: past behaviour on comparable work is the best predictor of future behaviour.

3. Systems and visibility
Can the provider show where shipments are, exchange data with our systems, and produce the reports the contract will require? The lessons on Transport and Warehouse Management Systems and on electronic data exchange established what good systems look like; a provider without them drags the customer's own visibility down to the provider's level.

4. Compliance and safety record
Licences, insurance, customs compliance, dangerous-goods competence where relevant, and safety history. Compliance failures do not stay with the provider — they land on the customer's shipment, as this lesson's important notes emphasise.

5. Financial health
A provider that fails commercially mid-contract halts the customer's operation with it. Solvency and staying power are operational criteria, not merely financial ones.

6. Cultural fit
Responsiveness, honesty when things go wrong, willingness to solve problems jointly. Contracts are operated by people, and mismatched ways of working erode even well-drafted agreements.

7. Price
Last, deliberately: price is meaningful only once the service being priced is genuinely comparable, which is why the specification came first. The cautionary tale later in this lesson shows what forgetting this costs.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The weighted framework, reference checks, and site visits
BODY:
Criteria alone still leave a problem: different evaluators weigh reliability, price and compliance differently in their heads, and the loudest voice wins. The remedy is a written evaluation framework — a scored comparison agreed before any proposal is opened.

The framework has three parts. First, criteria drawn from the specification, each given a weight reflecting the service promise: a cold-chain pharmaceutical contract weights compliance and reliability far above price; a bulk construction-materials lane might weight capacity and cost more heavily. Second, a common scoring scale applied identically to every bidder on every criterion. Third, a documented calculation — each score multiplied by its weight, summed to a total — together with the reasoning behind the scores. The documentation matters as much as the arithmetic: it keeps the evaluation honest under pressure, lets the loser be told fairly why they lost, and protects the business if the decision is later questioned.

Two verification steps complete the discipline, and neither can be done from a desk.

Reference checks mean contacting the short-listed provider's existing customers — ideally of similar size and lanes — and asking specific questions: what was actually delivered against the promise, what happened when a delivery failed, how the provider behaved when things went wrong. Vague praise is worth little; a concrete account of failure and recovery is worth a great deal.

Site visits mean seeing the operation as it really is: the condition and age of the vehicles and equipment, the state of the warehouse, whether the tracking system demonstrated in the pitch is the system actually used in daily work, and how the management team answers unwelcome questions. Proposals are written by salespeople; sites are run by operations.

Framework, references and visit together convert selection from an impression into an evidence-based decision — exactly what a strategic commitment of several years deserves.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Total cost of ownership
BODY:
The final evaluation discipline corrects the most common error in provider selection: comparing quotations as if the quoted price were the cost.

Total cost of ownership, or TCO, is the total cost associated with acquiring, using, managing and maintaining a product or service over a defined period. Applied to a logistics contract, the quoted rate is only the visible layer. Beneath it sit the costs the contract generates in operation: the coordination effort of managing the provider; the cost of claims when goods are damaged; the cost of expediting when deliveries miss their windows; the compensation and lost custom when the service fails the end customer; and eventually the cost of switching away.

This is the total-cost thinking of the lesson on Components of Total Logistics Cost — transport, warehousing, inventory holding, handling, administration and insurance seen together, direct and indirect costs both counted — turned onto a single purchasing decision. A provider whose price sits somewhat above the lowest bid but whose reliability prevents claims, expediting and lost sales is frequently the cheaper choice over the contract term, because the avoided failures are real money even though they never appear on a rate card.

TCO therefore changes how bids should be read: estimate, for each bidder, not only what the rate will cost but what the bidder's performance record is likely to cost in failures and management attention, over the full contract period. The decision rule is simple to state: compare bids on total cost over the term, not on the quotation. The third worked example of this lesson shows, in the negative, what happens when a business forgets it — an award made on price alone that repaid the saving several times over in claims and missed deliveries.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: From selection to partnership: monitoring and governance
BODY:
Selecting a provider creates a relationship; the strategy must then say what kind of relationship it intends to run.

The instrument of that relationship is the service-level agreement, or SLA — an agreement that states what service is bought, how it is measured, what happens when it is missed, and how either side exits. Its standard components form a checklist every logistics manager should carry: the scope of services; the KPIs with agreed targets — drawn from the measures of the lesson on Logistics Performance Indicators and KPIs, such as on-time in-full delivery per lane or damage rates; the measurement and reporting arrangements; the remedies and service credits that apply when targets are missed; the governance and review cycle; and the exit and transition terms. The next lesson, Contract Management and Service-Level Agreements, treats each component in depth; the point here is that the SLA is where the strategy's service promise becomes enforceable.

Monitoring turns those measures into vendor performance — the measured effectiveness of a provider in meeting agreed requirements. A working rhythm has three layers: regular performance reports against the KPIs; scheduled reviews — commonly quarterly — where trends, recurring misses and root causes are discussed; and joint improvement actions, so both parties work from the same data on making the next period better. This rhythm is also the early-warning system for everything this lesson has warned about: failing capability, growing dependency, eroding financial health.

The mindset must be chosen per relationship. A transactional mindset — buy, receive, pay — suits spot-market commodity purchases, where switching costs nothing and loyalty earns nothing. A partnership mindset suits the strategic lanes and multi-year contracts at the centre of the strategy: it shares information and forecasts, plans jointly, and gives both sides reason to invest in the relationship. Treating strategic partners as disposable suppliers forfeits the commitment the strategy paid for; treating spot purchases as partnerships overpays for them.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Dependence and diversification
BODY:
The last strategic decision — single provider or several — deserves its own examination, because its risks arrive late and all at once.

Excessive dependence on a single service provider is a quiet concentration of risk. Operationally, the provider's failure — insolvency, capacity shortage, a damaged fleet, a lost licence — becomes the customer's failure overnight, with no ready alternative. Commercially, dependence reverses the bargaining position: the provider who knows the customer has nowhere else to go is the provider who can raise rates and relax effort. Strategically, dependence deepens itself: each year of single-provider operation transfers more knowledge out of the customer's business, until even evaluating an alternative becomes difficult.

The strategic answer is supplier diversification — the use of multiple suppliers or service providers to reduce dependence and improve flexibility. It takes several forms: splitting critical lanes between two qualified providers, so each keeps the other honest and both together carry the volume; keeping a second provider qualified and lightly used, so that shifting volume in a crisis is a decision rather than a project; and blending contract rates for core volumes with spot purchases for peaks, so no single provider holds all the capacity.

Diversification has a price, and honesty requires naming it. Volume split between providers is volume concentrated with neither: rates rise, committed capacity shrinks, and the scale that made the relationship attractive thins out. The strategy therefore balances concentration — for leverage, scale and depth of partnership where the flows are stable and the provider proven — against diversification — where the flows are critical and the cost of failure high. How much dependence is acceptable is itself a strategy decision, reviewed as the business and the market change.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Make or buy: the fleet decision at a Tema juice manufacturer
BODY:
A manufacturer of packaged fruit juice in Tema distributes to wholesalers in Accra, Kumasi, Takoradi and Cape Coast. Its delivery volumes rise sharply before festive seasons and fall away for much of the rest of the year. Its own trucks are ageing, and the transport manager has proposed replacing them with a new fleet. The board applies the make-or-buy logic instead. The scenario and its figures are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: Capability
The company's own drivers know the routes and the returnable-crate handling, but the fleet is maintained reactively, delivery planning lives in one person's head, and no tracking exists. Specialist distributors in the region run scheduled maintenance, trained drivers and shipment tracking as standard products. On capability alone, the market would perform the core line-haul better.

Step 2: Volume
The analysis shows the fleet fully stretched for roughly a quarter of the year and substantially idle for the rest — yet drivers are paid and vehicles depreciate all year. Ownership would buy capacity for the peak and pay for it in the trough. Volume argues for buying transport as a service.

Step 3: Control
The goods are robust, customer deliveries are to wholesale depots with agreed windows, and nothing in the flow requires licences or special handling. What the customers actually need is reliability, not ownership — and reliability can be held through a contract with delivery-window measures and remedies rather than through painted company names on doors.

Step 4: Capital
Quotations show a new fleet plus a workshop, spare-parts stock and driver payroll would absorb capital the production side has been requesting for two expansion projects. Trucks would be the company's largest single asset, serving its least differentiating activity.

Step 5: The decision
The company contracts a regional 3PL for line-haul under agreed rates for committed base volumes, with peak capacity bought additionally in high season, and keeps two small company vehicles for city top-ups and short-notice orders. Control is exercised through a service-level agreement carrying delivery and condition measures, reviewed each quarter.

What does this example demonstrate?
The four factors — capability, volume, control, capital — walked honestly to a position rather than a reflex. Key lesson: ownership is only one form of control; in this case the contract delivered more reliability than the fleet ever had, released the capital, and stopped paying for idle trucks.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Selection done right: a pharmaceutical distributor awards a regional contract
BODY:
A pharmaceutical distributor based in Accra supplies wholesalers in Kumasi, Takoradi and Tamale with temperature-sensitive medicines and decides to outsource regional distribution. The scenario, weights and scores in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: Specify
Before contacting anyone, the distributor writes the specification: collect palletised stock from the Accra warehouse under cold-chain conditions; deliver next-day to Kumasi and Takoradi wholesalers and within two days to Tamale; maintain temperature records for every consignment; provide electronic proof of delivery the same day; handle current volumes with headroom for growth. The specification states the priorities outright: reliability and compliance first, price later.

Step 2: Scan and short-list
A market scan through industry contacts and professional associations produces a long list of eight; screening against minimum requirements — cold-chain capability, national coverage, valid licences and insurance — shortens it to five.

Step 3: Invite proposals
All five receive the same specification document and the same structured questions — the same scope for every bidder, so answers are comparable. Because the service is complex and multi-year, this is a request for proposal, inviting each provider to describe its service approach as well as its price.

Step 4: Evaluate with weights
The panel had agreed criteria and weights before opening any proposal: service capability and cold-chain competence twenty-five per cent, reliability and track record twenty per cent, compliance and insurance twenty per cent, systems and visibility fifteen per cent, cultural fit ten per cent, price ten per cent. Each bidder is scored on every criterion. The weighted totals place first overall a provider ranking only second on price — not the cheapest bid, but the strongest on reliability, compliance and systems.

Step 5: Verify
Reference checks call three of the winner's customers of similar profile, asking specifically about temperature excursions, missed deliveries and behaviour after failures; the answers confirm the record. A site visit inspects the depot — refrigerated vehicles and maintenance records, cold rooms, and the monitoring system working in live operation, not in slides.

Step 6: Contract and monitor
The contract annexes the specification and a service-level agreement carrying the measures of the lesson on Logistics Performance Indicators and KPIs — on-time in-full delivery per lane, delivery cycle time, temperature excursion rate, proof-of-delivery timeliness — with monthly reporting, remedies for misses, quarterly reviews and exit terms. The next lesson, Contract Management and Service-Level Agreements, examines that agreement in depth.

What does this example demonstrate?
The full selection discipline: specify, scan, short-list, invite, score with agreed weights, verify, contract with measurable commitments. Key lesson: because the specification came first, every bid was comparable, the evaluation was defensible, and the contract inherited the promise instead of inventing it afterwards.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: The price-first award: a cautionary tale from Mumbai
BODY:
A furniture retailer in Mumbai distributes imported flat-pack furniture from a central warehouse to its stores across the surrounding region. When its distribution contract comes up for renewal, the retailer invites proposals from five providers. The scenario is illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: The award
There is no written service specification — only a rough description of the work and a request for prices. The bids come back structured differently and describing different services, so they cannot be compared on anything but number. The award goes to the lowest quote, which sits noticeably below the rest of the field. The evaluation takes an afternoon.

Step 2: The first months
Problems arrive on a predictable schedule, each tracing back to the service the low price never included. Vehicles are older than promised, so breakdowns push deliveries past agreed store windows. Handlers are casual and untrained, so furniture arrives scratched and with corner damage; claims from the stores mount. There is no shipment tracking, so store managers spend their mornings telephoning the depot for whereabouts and their afternoons arranging premium transport for the shortfalls.

Step 3: The full bill
At the quarterly review the retailer finally assembles the whole picture. Against the saving made on the quotation, it sets: the claims paid for damaged furniture; the premium transport bought to patch missed deliveries; the compensation owed to stores left understocked in trading weeks; the management hours consumed by chasing; and the customers who bought elsewhere when shelves were bare. Measured this way, the cheapest contract costs more than the next-best bid would have — the price gap was an illusion created by comparing quotations instead of services.

Step 4: The re-award
The contract is retendered properly: a written specification, the same scope to all bidders, evaluation on weighted criteria, and bids compared on total cost of ownership over the contract term — the rate plus a realistic estimate of each bidder's failure costs and management burden. The new winner is a mid-priced provider with strong references, and the failure costs it avoids repay its price premium within the first year.

What does this example demonstrate?
The difference between price and cost, and the failure chain that runs from a missing specification through an unscorable comparison to an award on the only number available. Key lesson: the cheapest bid is cheap only if the service arrives; every avoided claim, expedite and lost sale is money the rate card never mentioned.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Outsourcing transfers the task, not the accountability
BODY:
The most dangerous sentence in outsourcing is "that is the provider's problem." The task is transferred; the accountability is not.

When a manufacturer's contracted carrier delivers late, the retailer does not ask about the carrier — it stops ordering from the manufacturer. When a distributor's provider breaks the cold chain, the pharmacy blames the distributor, and regulators may do the same. The customer experience belongs, always, to the business that made the promise. This is the same principle the lesson on the Role and Responsibilities of Freight Forwarders established from the other side: a forwarder acts on the customer's behalf, yet the shipment remains the customer's — and the same holds when the business itself is the customer of a 3PL.

Accountability has practical consequences. A provider must be managed actively, not merely engaged: specification, KPIs, reporting and reviews exist precisely because the business remains answerable for work it no longer performs. Enough internal knowledge must be retained to specify the service, judge the performance and, when necessary, leave the relationship — the antidote to the dependency described in this lesson. And failures should be treated as the business's own failures to fix: the first response to a provider's miss is not to quote the contract at them but to establish what happened, hold the provider to the agreed remedies, and repair the customer relationship, which is the only one the end customer can see.

Outsourcing, done well, is delegation with control. Done carelessly, it is abdication with paperwork.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: A contract without measurable KPIs is an aspiration
BODY:
Provider contracts frequently contain sentences like "the provider will deliver in a timely and professional manner." Such sentences are aspirations, not commitments: no one can say what "timely" means, so no one can ever be proved to have missed it. Where nothing is measurable, nothing is enforceable — no remedy can be invoked, no trend can be tracked, no improvement can be demonstrated, and disputes decay into whose-word-stands arguments.

The remedy is to give every promise three companions: a number, a measurement method and a consequence. "Next-day delivery" becomes on-time in-full delivery per lane, counted as the lesson on Logistics Performance Indicators and KPIs prescribes — an order counts only if it arrives both on time and complete — measured from a defined data source, reported monthly, with an agreed remedy when the target is missed. Every service element in the specification should pass the same test: if it cannot be measured, either define the measurement or admit it is a preference, not a commitment.

Two cautions keep measurement honest. First, few measures well: a contract carrying dozens of KPIs is as blind as one carrying none, because nobody manages what nobody reads. Second, people manage what is measured, so a badly chosen measure gets gamed — count only on-time deliveries and depots will be emptied of stock to make the window. Measures must be chosen to reflect the promise the customer actually bought.

The next lesson, Contract Management and Service-Level Agreements, builds the full architecture; the working rule to carry into it: a contract that cannot be measured cannot be managed, and a promise without a number is a hope.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Strategy is a position, not a portrait
BODY:
A logistics strategy is a position taken about service, structure and cost — and positions depend on conditions. When the conditions move and the position does not, yesterday's strategy becomes today's habit, defended by habit rather than by results.

Three kinds of change should trigger review. The business changes: new products, new markets, new channels — a strategy built for wholesale distribution creaks when the company opens an online shop promising direct delivery. The volumes change: growth can break a centralised network that once served brilliantly, while decline strands owned assets and starves contracted volumes below the levels the rates were built on. The market changes: providers merge or fail, capacity tightens or floods, and rate levels move — including after the kind of freight-market disruption the industry has repeatedly seen in recent years. Each shift alters what is available, what it costs, and how much dependence on any single provider is safe.

Review should therefore run on two clocks. The scheduled clock — the governance reviews of the provider contracts, and a periodic look at the whole strategy — catches slow drift. The trigger clock — an explicit decision to re-examine the strategy whenever the business, the volumes or the market shift materially — catches the sudden moves before the operation spends a year compensating for a position that no longer exists.

None of this means strategy changes casually: a position worth taking is worth holding while its conditions last — held conditionally, and examined. The professional test is simple to state and demanding to apply: could we currently explain, with present facts, why our logistics is structured the way it is? When the honest answer becomes "because it always has been", the review is overdue.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A producer of shea butter in Tamale ships palletised consignments to distributors in Accra and Tema. Volumes rise sharply during harvest season and fall away for much of the rest of the year. The production manager proposes buying a fleet of trucks, arguing that ownership would give the company control of its deliveries.

Which option best describes the sound strategic analysis of this make-or-buy decision?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The decision should weigh capability, volume, control and capital: seasonal volumes would leave owned vehicles idle for much of the year while capital sits locked in them, so contracting transport — with reliability held in place through agreed performance measures — is likely the sounder position.
2. The company should buy the fleet, because owning vehicles is always cheaper in the long run than paying a service provider for the same journeys.
3. The company should outsource all transport and stop monitoring deliveries afterwards, because a contract transfers accountability for delivery performance to the provider.
4. The company should decide purely by choosing whichever transport provider submits the lowest quotation, since price is the only factor that matters.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

The make-or-buy decision weighs four factors. Capability: specialists run transport as their core business, with systems and maintenance an individual manufacturer struggles to match. Volume: this producer's demand is strongly seasonal, and owned vehicles earn nothing while standing idle for much of the year — the business would be buying peak capacity and paying for it all year. Control: what the customers need is reliability, which a contract can secure through KPIs and remedies; ownership is only one form of control. Capital: a fleet would absorb capital that serves the company's core business better. All four point the same way, which is what makes the position sound rather than merely defensible.

Answer 2 ignores utilisation and capital: ownership is cheaper only when volume keeps the assets busy. Answer 3 repeats the misconception that outsourcing transfers accountability — the task moves, but the customer still experiences the failure, so monitoring must continue. Answer 4 is the price-first error this lesson dismantles.

The takeaway: own or outsource is a four-factor analysis, not a reflex — and a contract can hold control better than a parked truck.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
A distributor in Accra is selecting a provider for regional distribution. Two proposals remain. The lowest-priced bid comes from a provider with a weak delivery record, ageing vehicles and no shipment tracking. A higher bid comes from a provider with strong references from similar customers, complete compliance documentation and live tracking.

Which statement best describes sound provider selection in this situation?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Price is the first criterion in provider selection, so the lowest bid should win unless a competitor matches it exactly.
2. Selection should weigh all the specified criteria — reliability, capacity, compliance, systems and service quality — and compare the bids on total cost of ownership, because service failures carry costs that can exceed the price gap.
3. The largest and best-known provider available should be appointed, because the size of a company guarantees its performance.
4. The contract should be split equally between the two providers so that neither is disappointed and no difficult choice has to be made.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Provider selection evaluates capability and coverage, reliability and track record, systems and visibility, compliance and safety, financial health and cultural fit — with price considered last, after the service being priced is genuinely comparable. Here the bids describe very different services: the cheap bid buys an operation whose record predicts claims, missed windows and management time, and total cost of ownership counts those failure costs alongside the rate, as this lesson's Mumbai example showed. The dearer bid's references, compliance and tracking are precisely the criteria that prevent such failures.

Answer 1 is the price-first fallacy the lesson warns against: comparing quotations as if they priced the same service, when the specification and the criteria say otherwise. Answer 3 substitutes size for fit — large providers also fail when capability, coverage and cultural fit are wrong. Answer 4 misuses diversification: splitting volume is a deliberate risk decision with its own cost, not a way to avoid choosing between a sound bid and a weak one.

The takeaway: evaluate against the specification, price last, and decide on total cost over the contract term.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A manufacturer depends on a single logistics provider for all of its transport and warehousing. Concerned about this concentration, the board asks management which actions would genuinely reduce the risks of excessive dependence on one provider.

Which option is NOT a sound response to excessive dependence on a single service provider?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Qualifying a second provider on the critical lanes, so that volume can be shifted deliberately if the incumbent fails.
2. Blending contracted rates for core volumes with spot purchases for peaks, so that no single provider holds all the capacity the business needs.
3. Cancelling the incumbent's contract immediately and moving all volume to a new provider found at short notice, without evaluation, specification or transition arrangements.
4. Holding regular performance reviews with the incumbent, so that warning signs of failure surface while options to respond still exist.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

Dependence on one provider concentrates risk: the provider's failure becomes the customer's failure overnight, the bargaining position reverses, and the internal knowledge to switch fades. The sound responses reduce that exposure with discipline — qualifying a second provider before a crisis makes switching a decision rather than a project; blending contract and spot cover so no one provider holds all the capacity; and using the governance rhythm of reviews and KPIs as the early-warning system that surfaces problems in time. Answer 3 does the opposite of all three: it replaces a managed position with an unmanaged leap, repeating the entire selection process in its worst form — no specification, no evaluation, no references, no transition — and would likely deepen dependence on the new provider within months.

Dependence is reduced by prepared alternatives and early warning, not by panic. Diversification is a strategy, and strategies are specified and evaluated like everything else in this lesson.

The takeaway: the opposite of excessive dependence is not haste — it is qualified alternatives, measurable performance, and a review rhythm that sees trouble coming.
