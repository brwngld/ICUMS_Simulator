# DRAFT — Lesson 11.1: Cargo Insurance and Risk Allocation

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Risk allocation comes before insurance
BODY:
This lesson opens Module 11, Logistics Risk and Compliance, with the discipline that holds the rest of the module together: managing the possibility that cargo is lost, damaged, or delayed, and knowing in advance who carries the financial consequence. Two different questions are hiding inside that sentence. The first is a question of allocation: if something goes wrong on the journey, which trading party bears the loss? The second is a question of compensation: having borne it, can that party recover the money? Insurance answers only the second question. Insurance compensates; it never assigns responsibility. Responsibility is allocated by the contract of sale, above all by the Incoterms 2020 rule the parties have chosen, which Lesson 4.3, Incoterms and Allocation of Responsibilities, taught in full.

Risk transfer is the point or process through which responsibility for specified risks passes from one party to another. Risk mitigation is the set of actions taken to reduce the likelihood or consequences of an identified risk. With those two ideas in place, the professional sequence for managing cargo risk runs in three steps.

1. Identify the risk. Ask what can happen to these particular goods on this particular route: physical loss, damage, the consequences of delay, even a share in another party's maritime sacrifice.

2. Allocate the risk. Ask which party carries each risk, and from which precise point. The trade term answers this before anything goes wrong.

3. Insure the risk. Ask whether the party carrying the risk can afford to carry it unprotected, and buy cover accordingly.

Prevention and insurance are partners, not rivals: mitigation lowers the chance of a claim, and insurance stands behind the losses that mitigation could not prevent. The blocks that follow walk the sequence in order.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: What can happen to goods on the move
BODY:
Before anyone buys cover, the risk must be identified, and the risks are not the same for every consignment. The common families are these.

1. Physical loss. Goods may disappear entirely: a container lost overboard, a whole consignment never delivered, part of a delivery missing, or goods stolen in transit or from storage.

2. Physical damage. Goods may arrive but arrive ruined or impaired: crushed by bad handling, soaked by water entering a container, burnt in a fire, damaged when a vehicle collides or overturns, or spoiled by the weather on an exposed quay.

3. Delay and its consequences. A consignment may arrive late with consequences that are financial rather than physical: a perishable cargo past its market, a factory line stopped for want of parts, a seasonal order missed. Delay deserves separate treatment because, as a later block explains, the financial losses that delay causes are generally not what cargo insurance is written to pay.

4. A share in a maritime sacrifice. Under the seafaring principle called general average, a cargo owner can owe money for a rescue that never touched its own goods, because all cargo interests share the cost of a deliberate sacrifice made to save the voyage. The full mechanics belong to their own block below, but the possibility belongs on the identification list, because it surprises traders more than any other.

5. Storage-phase risks. Goods do not cease to be exposed when they stop moving: warehouse fire, water damage, and handling accidents in storage claim their share of losses.

The identification step is done route by route and cargo by cargo: the risks facing frozen fish in a reefer container are not the risks facing bagged cement.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Cargo insurance, the parties, and the policy's core numbers
BODY:
Cargo insurance is insurance designed to provide financial protection against specified loss of or damage to goods during insured transportation or related activities. Its importance in logistics is structural: international trade would be a parade of intolerable gambles if every loss fell in full on whoever happened to hold the goods at the moment of the accident. Insurance converts a ruinous, random loss into a known, budgetable cost, which is what allows businesses to ship at a scale no single loss could fund alone.

The parties are named plainly. The insured is the person or organisation covered under an insurance policy. The insurer is the company or organisation providing insurance coverage under an agreed policy. The deal between them is recorded in the policy, and three numbers in it matter most at this stage.

1. The insurance premium is the amount paid for insurance coverage. It is the price of transferring the financial consequence of the risk.

2. The sum insured is the monetary amount for which an insured interest is covered under the policy. It is the ceiling of the cover, and choosing it wisely is a discipline of its own, treated at the end of this lesson.

3. The deductible, met properly in the block on exclusions and conditions, is the portion of an insured loss borne by the insured.

The subject matter of cargo insurance is the goods themselves while they are in transit, together with the incidental exposures around the transit: loading, discharge, and interim storage connected with the journey. Cover can be arranged for the legs the parties actually face, including the whole door-to-door journey where the shipment is arranged so; a trader who ships constantly may cover movement after movement under a continuing arrangement rather than buying a separate policy for every consignment. What the policy protects is money: compensation to the insured within the agreed terms.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Insurable interest: who may insure
BODY:
Not everyone with an opinion about a shipment may insure it. The governing principle is insurable interest: the insured must stand to gain from the cargo's safe arrival or suffer from its loss. The principle ties insurance cover to a genuine stake in the goods, and it exists to keep insurance a protection against real exposure rather than a wager on other people's misfortune.

Insurable interest follows the risk. The party who bears the risk of transit under the trade term has the clearest stake, because that party pays if the goods vanish. A seller usually holds the interest while the goods remain at its risk before dispatch; a buyer holds it once risk has passed; and where the term obliges one party to insure for the benefit of the other, as some Incoterms rules do, the arrangement exists precisely so the cover is in force at the moment the risk crosses over, with the policy document made transferable to the party who will hold the interest during the voyage.

Two practical consequences deserve attention. First, because insurable interest follows the risk, the allocation exercise of Lesson 4.3 is also the insurance exercise: work out where risk passes, and you have worked out who can and should insure each leg. Second, the interest must be real. A business cannot sensibly insure goods it will never own and never lose; a buyer who has not yet taken the risk has nothing to insure, and a seller who has passed the risk has, in general, little left to protect beyond its prospect of payment.

This is why the professional question is never simply "is this shipment insured?" but "who holds the risk at this leg, and is the cover in that party's interest?" The next block meets a party whose responsibilities are real but which is not the answer to that question.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Carrier liability is not cargo insurance
BODY:
Carriers also answer for cargo, and the two channels must never be confused. Carrier liability is the legal or contractual responsibility of a carrier for specified cargo loss, damage, or delay. When goods are damaged in a carrier's custody, the carrier may indeed be answerable. But that responsibility is neither automatic nor unlimited.

Carriage is governed by applicable transport rules and by the carrier's own contract of carriage, and across the international carriage regimes two features recur. First, carriers enjoy defences: there are circumstances, such as damage arising from certain inherent characteristics of the goods or from events beyond the carrier's control, in which the carrier is not answerable at all. Second, liability is limited: where a carrier is answerable, the rules and the contract commonly cap the compensation well below the commercial value of the goods, and they attach their own notice requirements and time limits to claims. The specific figures differ by regime and are not taught in this course; the structural point is what matters.

Cargo insurance behaves differently in every particular. It is a separate contract, bought in advance by the cargo interest, that responds to covered losses within its own terms, and it does not depend on proving the carrier at fault. The two channels also answer different questions: the carrier's responsibility asks what the carrier owes for what happened on board; the policy asks what the insurer owes the insured under the agreed cover.

The practical consequences are uncomfortable but clarifying. An uninsured cargo owner whose goods are destroyed may finish the episode with a fraction of the value recovered even where the carrier was plainly implicated, because defences and limits stand in the way. Conversely, holding insurance does not erase the carrier's responsibility. A professional treats them as two distinct recoveries, each with its own basis, its own documents, and its own clock.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The coverage ladder: Institute Cargo Clauses (A), (B), and (C)
BODY:
Cargo cover is not one product but a ladder of three standard levels, the Institute Cargo Clauses, the marine-cargo policy wordings in general use across the international market, in the version current since 2009. The levels are known by the letters (A), (B), and (C), and the alphabet climbs with the protection.

At the bottom stands Institute Cargo Clauses (C), which covers major casualties only: the listed catastrophes of the journey, among them fire and explosion, the vessel being stranded, grounded, sunk, or capsized, collision, derailment, or overturning of the conveyance, discharge of cargo at a port of distress, and the sacrifice of cargo under general average. At the middle level, Institute Cargo Clauses (B) is a named-perils cover that adds further listed events relative to (C), such as cargo washed overboard, entry of water into the conveyance or container, and loss or damage during loading and discharge. Named perils coverage is insurance covering losses caused by specifically identified risks or events: if the event is not on the list, the loss is not covered, however real it was.

At the top stands Institute Cargo Clauses (A), the all-risks level. All risks coverage is insurance covering fortuitous physical loss or damage except where excluded by the policy terms. The wording reverses the logic: instead of listing what is covered, it covers everything fortuitous and physical, and pushes the list in the other direction, to what is not covered. Even so it is not a blank cheque, and the exclusions get a block of their own below.

The premium rises with the ladder: (C) is the cheapest, (A) the dearest, which is why the choice is an economic decision and not an automatic one. The common market pattern follows the cargo: bulk, low-risk commodities, whose dominant dangers are the vessel's own casualties, often travel at the (C) or (B) level, while finished, high-value, damage-prone goods typically justify the wider protection of (A). Match the ladder to the exposure, and pay for breadth only where the cargo needs it.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Exclusions, policy conditions, and the deductible
BODY:
An insurance exclusion is a circumstance, cause, or category of loss that is not covered by an insurance policy. Exclusions are not fine-print spite; they are the boundary lines that keep the cover insurable, and a professional reads them before the loss, not after.

Even the widest level, Institute Cargo Clauses (A), carries exclusions. In general terms, the standard clauses do not respond to loss caused by the wilful misconduct of the insured, to ordinary wear and tear and leakage, to inherent vice, to delay, and to war and strikes unless that cover is separately bought. Three of these deserve unpacking.

1. Inherent vice means deterioration arising from the goods' own nature: grain that heats in a hold, fruit that ripens and rots, moisture-laden goods that sweat and mildew. The insurer accepts the voyage; it does not accept the cargo's chemistry.

2. Delay excludes the financial consequences of lateness: the lost market, the stopped production line. This is the boundary line behind the delay family identified at the start of the lesson.

3. War and strikes risks sit outside the standard cover but can generally be bought back as separate extensions where the route requires them.

Policy conditions are the insured's own obligations under the agreement: to observe the duties the policy spells out, to notify claims promptly, and to allow the insurer's examination of the loss. A policy is a two-sided contract, and conditions breached at the wrong moment can prejudice a claim however genuine the loss.

The deductible is the portion of an insured loss borne by the insured under the applicable policy. Deductibles keep small, routine losses inside the trader's own accounts, and they mean that a claim is measured net of the agreed first slice. Their size varies by policy; this course teaches the concept, never amounts. Read the whole document: cover, exclusions, conditions, deductible.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Incoterms 2020: where risk passes and why that decides who insures
BODY:
Incoterms 2020 is the International Chamber of Commerce's set of eleven three-letter rules that say who does what, who pays what, and where risk passes in an international sale; they do not decide who owns the goods or how payment is made. Lesson 4.3, Incoterms and Allocation of Responsibilities, taught the full allocation; this lesson needs the risk-transfer points, because they decide where insurable interest settles.

The risk passes from seller to buyer as follows. Under EXW, when the goods are placed at the buyer's disposal at the seller's premises. Under FCA, when the goods are handed to the buyer's carrier at the named place. Under FAS, when the goods are placed alongside the ship at the port of shipment. Under FOB, CFR, and CIF, when the goods are on board the vessel at the port of shipment. Under CPT and CIP, on handover to the first carrier. Under DAP, when the goods are placed at the buyer's disposal, ready for unloading, at the named destination; under DPU, the same but after unloading; and under DDP, as DAP with import clearance added for the seller's account.

One structural fact dominates the insurance question. Under the four C rules, CFR, CIF, CPT, and CIP, the seller pays for carriage to the destination, but the risk passes to the buyer much earlier, already at shipment. Paying for carriage does not mean bearing the risk to destination. A buyer purchasing on CFR or CIF therefore owns the sea voyage's risk while the seller owns the freight bill, and the two must never be confused.

The insurance conclusion follows mechanically from the sequence taught at the start of this lesson: identify the risk, allocate it by the term, then ask who insures. The party holding the risk between the transfer points is the party whose money is exposed, and that party is the natural buyer of cover for the leg. The next block applies this to the terms one by one.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Who arranges the policy under each trade term
BODY:
Apply the sequence and the insurance map of Incoterms 2020 falls out quickly. Only two of the eleven rules contain a duty to insure. Under CIF, cost, insurance and freight, the seller must insure at minimum Institute Cargo Clauses (C) cover for the buyer's benefit; under CIP, carriage and insurance paid to, the seller must insure at the higher Institute Cargo Clauses (A) level, reflecting that CIP is used across all modes and commonly for higher-value, damage-prone consignments. Under CIF the insurance covers, at minimum, the shipment-leg risk from the port of shipment to the named destination port; under CIP, from the place of shipment to the named destination. A seller arranging either should make sure the insurance period runs over the full transit risk the buyer takes over, not merely the port-to-port sliver of it.

Under every other rule there is no obligation to insure at all, though the risk may make it wise. The buyer choosing EXW, FCA, FAS, or FOB carries the main carriage risk and insures, or gambles, as it sees fit. The seller choosing DAP, DPU, or DDP carries the risk to destination and is the natural insurer of the journey it owns. Under CFR the seller pays the freight but the buyer owns the voyage risk, so the buyer is the party who should hold a policy.

Three refinements matter in practice. First, the Incoterms insurance duty is a minimum, not a maximum: parties may agree more, a wider ladder level or a higher sum, and many do. Second, the customary valuation basis in the market, reflected in the clauses themselves, is to insure for 110 per cent of the contract value; the figure is a long-standing benchmark, and the parties' agreement governs. Third, the buyer under CIF or CIP needs proof of the cover promised: the certificate of insurance, the short document evidencing that a shipment is covered under the seller's marine policy, commonly required under CIF and CIP so the buyer receives transferable evidence of cover. It belongs to the certificate family met in Lesson 6.3, Certificates of Origin, Permits, and Supporting Documents: another document that proves, on its face, that something required is in place.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: General average: the sacrifice every cargo shares
BODY:
General average is the oldest sharing rule in shipping, and the one no trader should first meet in a demand for money. Codified for the modern era in the York-Antwerp Rules, first settled in 1890 and revised since, the principle is this: where a voluntary sacrifice, such as cargo jettisoned to lighten a stranded vessel, or an extraordinary expense, is deliberately made to preserve the common adventure from a common peril, the sacrifice is shared proportionally among the ship, the cargo, and the freight interests that the rescue saved.

Notice what the principle does to ordinary intuition. The sacrifice was made for everyone: the water that drowned one hold's cargo saved the hull, the remaining cargo, and the voyage's earnings. The law therefore spreads the cost over all who benefited, not merely over the unlucky. Every cargo interest contributes roughly in proportion to the value its cargo brought safe to the adventure, and that includes cargo owners whose goods were never touched, never wetted, and never threatened directly. Cargo owners ordinarily post security for their contribution, typically through their insurers, before the goods are released for delivery.

Two connections bind this block to the rest of the lesson. First, general average sacrifice is such a recognised maritime peril that it appears among the major casualties covered even at the narrowest level, Institute Cargo Clauses (C); the ladder protects the contribution as well as the loss. Second, general average is why cargo insurance matters even to the trader whose container always arrives fine: an insured cargo interest hands the problem of security and contribution to its insurer, while an uninsured one must find its share from its own pocket before its goods move.

Real declarations of general average in recent years have included the Ever Given (2021) and Maersk Honam (2018) cases, and the first worked example of this lesson walks through such an event step by step, in words, from a cargo owner's chair.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: When loss happens: the claim, the survey, and the documents
BODY:
An insurance claim is a formal request for compensation under an insurance policy, and a claim well run is a procedure, not an outburst. The broad sequence runs as follows.

1. Notify promptly. The insured tells its insurer, and the carrier where the goods travelled in its custody, as soon as loss or damage is discovered. Prompt notice protects evidence, preserves the insured's rights against the carrier, whose liability rules attach their own time requirements, and satisfies the policy's conditions, which demand timely notice.

2. Limit the further loss. The insured takes sensible steps to prevent the damage spreading: segregating wet cartons, moving cargo out of the rain, salvaging what can be salvaged. The policy expects mitigation, not passivity.

3. Survey. The insurer arranges or requires an examination of the damaged cargo. The output is the survey report: a document recording the findings of an examination of cargo condition, loss, or damage. It establishes what happened, how extensive the loss is, and what caused it, and it anchors the claim's quantum.

4. Assemble the documents. A cargo claim is proved on paper. The documents commonly required include the insurance policy or certificate of insurance, the commercial invoice, the packing list, the transport document such as the bill of lading or air waybill, the survey report, and the correspondence with the carrier asserting its responsibility. They are the same document families whose verification Lesson 6.3, Certificates of Origin, Permits, and Supporting Documents, and the documentation lessons around it trained; here they serve a second master, the claim file.

5. Claim and settle. With notice given, survey done, and documents assembled, the insurer assesses the loss within the policy's terms, applies the deductible and any conditions, and compensates within the sum insured.

The habit to carry forward is simple: the claim begins on the day the damage is discovered, and its strength is decided by the promptness of notice and the quality of the file.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Insuring value: invoice value, landed cost, and under-insurance
BODY:
One decision remains in the sequence this lesson opened with: insure, yes, but for how much? The tempting answer is the invoice value of the goods, because that is the number on the paper in front of everyone. It is usually the wrong answer, for a reason this course has already priced.

Lesson 10.2, Freight Costing and Landed-Cost Calculations, built the landed cost: goods value, freight, duty and taxes, destination charges, inland transport, and the tail. Every line of that build is money the buyer has genuinely at stake in the goods arriving. A consignment lost is not merely an invoice evaporating; it is the freight paid or payable, the duties and taxes incurred, the destination charges and inland carriage that will be re-incurred, and the selling position that depended on the goods. Under-insurance is the gap that opens when the sum insured is set on the invoice value while the true financial exposure runs to the full landed cost: whatever the loss, the compensation is bounded by the sum insured and by the policy's terms, so the uninsured slice of the exposure falls on the business that underinsured. Whether any particular cost line beyond the invoice is recoverable depends on the policy's wording, which is one more reason to read it before buying.

The professional disciplines follow. First, value the cover on the full exposure, using the landed-cost build as the map of what is at stake, and remember the market's customary benchmark of 110 per cent of contract value in CIF and CIP sales. Second, revisit the basis when costs move; a sum insured set two seasons ago may no longer bound today's exposure. Third, treat prevention as the first line of defence. Risk mitigation, the actions taken to reduce the likelihood or consequences of an identified risk, is cheaper than any claim: sound packaging, careful stowage, vetted carriers, sensible routes and seasons, secure storage, and accurate documentation all shrink the loss that no policy fully restores. Insurance stands behind what prevention cannot stop; prevention keeps the claim, the deductible, the delay, and the lost customer off the books in the first place.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: General average aboard a container vessel
BODY:
The following scenario is illustrative and told in words; it follows the general average principle as taught in this lesson. Step 1: the voyage. A container vessel is mid-Atlantic on a rotation from Shanghai toward West Africa. Among some thousands of containers are two belonging to Adomah Foods Ltd, a processor in Accra, carrying food-processing machinery invoiced at 180,000 US dollars, sold on FCA Shanghai terms. All figures are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 2: the peril. A fire breaks out in a lower hold. The master faces a choice: lose the ship, or fight the fire in a way that destroys some cargo to save the rest. The master orders the hold sealed and flooded with seawater. The fire dies. Some containers in the hold are burnt beyond saving, dozens more are soaked, and the great majority of cargo on board, including both of Adomah Foods' containers, is untouched.

Step 3: the diversion. The vessel diverts to a nearby port for emergency repairs and re-inspection, an extraordinary expense incurred to preserve the voyage.

Step 4: the declaration. The vessel's owners declare general average. Specialists appointed to adjust it measure the values at stake and work out each interest's share of the sacrifice and the expense, roughly in proportion to the saved value each interest contributed to the adventure. Adomah Foods reads the notice with disbelief: its containers were never touched, yet before its machinery can be delivered, it must post security for its contribution to a rescue its goods did not need.

Step 5: the insurance difference. Because Adomah Foods had arranged all-risks cover as the FCA buyer, it hands the notice and the security demand to its insurer, which provides the security and absorbs the contribution within the policy terms. A neighbouring consignee on the same vessel, uninsured, must fund its share from working capital and wait, uninsured, through the adjustment.

What does this example demonstrate? General average spreads a voluntary sacrifice over every cargo interest that benefited, including the untouched, and security is ordinarily required before delivery. Insurance is the difference between forwarding the problem and funding it.

Key lesson: even a perfectly safe cargo is exposed on a shared voyage; insure the interest, not just the container.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Choosing cover: cocoa butter on CIP against clinker on CFR
BODY:
Step 1: the two desks. In Tema, an exporter of cocoa products is booking 800 cartons of cocoa butter, a finished, high-value food product, to a buyer in Rotterdam, sold on CIP Rotterdam terms. Across the harbour office, a second trader is fixing a full bulk cargo of clinker, the low-value mineral input for cement, to a buyer overseas, sold on CFR terms. Both desks must now answer the same question: who insures, and at which level of the ladder? All figures and volumes are illustrative.

Step 2: the CIP desk applies the rule. CIP carries a duty to insure: the seller must arrange cover for the buyer's benefit at Institute Cargo Clauses (A), the all-risks level, with the insurance period running over the transit risk that passed to the buyer when the goods were handed to the first carrier. The desk checks that the cover is real, obtains the certificate of insurance for the buyer, and notes that the customary market basis is 110 per cent of the contract value.

Step 3: the CIP desk reasons about the level. Even without the obligation, the level would choose itself: cocoa butter is finished, expensive, and damage-prone, exposed to heat, taint, and water, and the losses that matter to it, water entry and handling damage, sit above the narrow (C) rung. The mandatory (A) matches the cargo.

Step 4: the CFR desk applies the rule. CFR carries a freight obligation and no insurance duty. Risk passed to the buyer when the clinker went on board at the load port, so the buyer owns the sea voyage; the seller's stake in the transit is gone. Nobody is obliged to insure, but the buyer would be wise to, in its own interest.

Step 5: the CFR desk reasons about the level. If the buyer insures, the reasoning runs the other way: bulk clinker is low-value, robust, and its dominant dangers are the vessel's own casualties, fire, grounding, sinking, the very events of the (C) rung. A (C) or (B) cover is the natural consideration, and the premium economy is exactly why the ladder exists. The buyer that insures nothing is gambling the whole cargo value on the ship's luck.

What does this example demonstrate? The trade term allocates the risk, and the allocation decides who insures; the cargo's character decides the level. Same ladder, different seats.

Key lesson: first ask where risk passes, then ask who holds the exposure, and only then choose the rung.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: The under-insurance gap: insured at invoice, exposed at landed cost
BODY:
Step 1: the purchase. Sika Health Ltd, a pharmaceutical distributor in Accra, imports a consignment of medicines from a supplier overseas. The invoice value of the goods is GHS 400,000. Sika's clerk, filling in the proposal form, writes the sum insured as GHS 400,000, the number on the invoice. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 2: what the consignment really cost. Recall the landed-cost build from Lesson 10.2. For this shipment, illustratively: ocean freight GHS 30,000; import duty, VAT, and levies computed by rule at the time of assessment, GHS 70,000; terminal and destination charges GHS 10,000; clearance fees and inland transport GHS 18,000. The full exposure bound up in this consignment is therefore about GHS 528,000, not the GHS 400,000 on the invoice. The duty and taxes alone represent money already spent or committed because of these goods.

Step 3: the loss. Mid-voyage, fire breaks out in the hold and Sika's consignment is destroyed. Fire is a major casualty, covered even at the narrowest level of the ladder, so the level of cover is not the problem here. The problem is the ceiling.

Step 4: the shortfall. The claim is measured within the policy's terms, and the sum insured the policy records is GHS 400,000. The gap between what the consignment cost Sika in total and what the sum insured can respond with, about GHS 128,000 in this illustration, falls on Sika. Whether particular cost lines beyond the invoice, such as the duty already paid, would have been recoverable had the cover been written more widely depends on the policy wording; the sum insured at invoice value guarantees only that the answer starts too low.

Step 5: the correction. On the next import, Sika values the cover from the landed-cost build: goods, freight, duties and taxes, destination charges, and inland carriage, with the market's customary 110 per cent of contract value in mind where the sale terms carry an insurance obligation. The premium is modestly higher; the exposure is finally matched.

What does this example demonstrate? The invoice is the price of the goods; the exposure is the landed cost. Insuring the first while risking the second is how businesses fund their own shortfalls.

Key lesson: set the sum insured from the build, not from the invoice.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Insurance pays money; it does not deliver the goods
BODY:
The deepest misconception this lesson dismantles is the belief that insurance puts the transaction right. Insurance compensates: it moves money, within the policy's terms, after the loss. It does not deliver the goods, and it does not repair the damage the loss does to the business around the goods.

Consider what actually follows a serious loss. The customer who was to receive the consignment is still unserved, and the relationship is strained whether or not a claim is eventually paid. The production line waiting on the lost spare parts is still stopped while the survey, the file, and the settlement take their course. The trader must still arrange replacement supply, renegotiate delivery, and manage the commercial consequences, none of which the policy performs. And some of the damage is financial in a form the standard cover was never written to answer: the losses caused by delay, the missed market, the expired season, are excluded even under the widest level of the clauses.

Insurance is therefore a balance-sheet protection, not an operations plan. It preserves the money, and preserving the money is essential; but the service reputation, the customer's schedule, and the trading relationship are protected by the things insurance is not: prevention, buffer stock, reliable carriers, and honest communication when a shipment goes wrong. A professional buys the cover for the loss that would otherwise be unbearable, and still works as if the cover were not there.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: "All risks" is not all events, and the minimum is not a maximum
BODY:
Two boundaries close this lesson, and both are lines people cross in the wrong direction in real claims.

First, all-risks cover does not mean all events. Institute Cargo Clauses (A) covers fortuitous physical loss or damage except where the policy excludes it, and the exclusions are real: inherent vice, the goods' own nature working their deterioration; delay and the financial losses it causes; ordinary wear and leakage; wilful misconduct; war and strikes unless separately bought. A consignment of timber that seasons and splits in a humid hold has not suffered an insured event merely because the policy was titled all risks. The professional discipline is to read the exclusion list against the cargo's actual vulnerabilities before the voyage, and to buy back what the route genuinely needs as separate cover where that is available.

Second, the Incoterms insurance duty is a floor, not a ceiling. Under CIF the seller's obligation is minimum cover at Institute Cargo Clauses (C); under CIP, at the (A) level. Parties may agree more, and the minimum binds only where the contract is silent. Equally, under every other rule nobody is obliged to insure at all, which is not the same as saying nobody should. The term tells you the floor and the risk tells you the need; neither substitutes for reading the two documents that actually govern the money: the policy, with its sum insured, conditions, and exclusions, and the contract of sale, with the trade term and any agreed extensions written into it.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A Ghanaian importer buys a consignment of machine parts on CFR Tema terms. During the ocean voyage, heavy weather damages several of the importer's containers. The importer demands that the seller compensate the loss, arguing that the seller paid the freight all the way to Tema.

Which statement best describes the position?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The seller must compensate, because paying the freight to the destination port means bearing the risk of the voyage to that port.
2. Under CFR the seller pays carriage to the destination, but the risk passed to the buyer when the goods were on board the vessel at the port of shipment, so the buyer bears the voyage loss and any insurance was the buyer's own arrangement.
3. The shipping line automatically compensates the buyer for the full invoice value of the cargo, so neither trading party bears the loss.
4. The seller was obliged to insure the goods for the voyage under CFR, so the seller's insurer must pay the buyer's claim.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

This is the classic trap of the C rules. Under CFR, CIF, CPT, and CIP the seller pays for carriage to the destination, but the risk passes to the buyer already at shipment, when the goods are on board the vessel at the port of shipment. Paying for carriage does not mean bearing the risk to destination. The lesson's sequence applies: the trade term allocated the voyage risk to the buyer at shipment, so the buyer was the party with the insurable interest at sea and the party whose decision it was to insure or gamble. Only CIF and CIP oblige the seller to insure, at minimum ICC(C) and ICC(A) respectively; CFR contains no insurance duty at all.

Answer 1 restates the exact misconception: freight paid is a cost, not a risk allocation. Answer 3 overstates carrier liability, which is subject to the carriage rules' defences and limits and is never an automatic full-value guarantee. Answer 4 invents an insurance obligation that CFR does not contain.

The takeaway: read the term for two separate allocations, cost and risk; they pass at different points, and insurance follows the risk.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
A container vessel suffers a serious fire mid-voyage. The master orders the burning hold flooded with seawater, saving the ship and most of the cargo. On arrival, an importer whose containers were completely untouched by the fire and the water is required to post security for a general average contribution before its goods can be delivered.

Which statement best explains the demand?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The demand is an error, because only the cargo owners whose goods were actually sacrificed or damaged can be asked to contribute.
2. The carrier has absorbed the loss as an ordinary operating cost of the voyage, and the contribution is simply the recovery of that cost.
3. A voluntary sacrifice made to preserve the common adventure from a common peril is shared proportionally among ship, cargo, and freight interests, including cargo owners whose goods were untouched, and insurers commonly provide the security before delivery.
4. General average contributions arise only when the vessel is totally lost, so a demand after a successful salvage must be a scam.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

General average is the maritime principle, codified in the York-Antwerp Rules, under which a voluntary sacrifice or extraordinary expense deliberately made to preserve the common adventure from a common peril is shared proportionally among the ship, the cargo, and the freight interests that benefited. The flooding of the hold saved every cargo owner's voyage, so every cargo interest contributes roughly in proportion to the value its cargo brought safe to the adventure, and cargo owners ordinarily post security for their contribution, typically through their insurers, before delivery. This is exactly why the lesson teaches that cargo insurance matters even when the goods arrive fine: the insured hands the security and contribution problem to its insurer.

Answer 1 reflects the intuitive but wrong view; the untouched cargo owner owes a share precisely because the rescue was made for all. Answer 2 mischaracterises the sharing rule as an ordinary carrier cost. Answer 4 confuses general average with total loss; the principle operates precisely when the adventure is saved, not when it is lost.

The takeaway: on a shared voyage, a rescue for one is a charge on all, and insurance is how an untouched cargo owner pays without bleeding.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
After a storm, a trainee finds cartons of imported stationery soaked and damaged. The carrier indicates that its responsibility for the goods is limited under the applicable carriage rules. The trainee's manager replies that the company holds an all-risks cargo policy, "so none of the carrier's position matters and everything will be paid regardless."

Which option best describes the correct view of the two channels?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The manager is right: an all-risks policy covers every loss from every cause, so the carrier's position is irrelevant once cover exists.
2. The carrier is automatically liable in full for all cargo damage occurring in its custody, so insurance is redundant whenever a carrier can be identified.
3. The insured must choose one channel only, and abandoning any claim against the carrier increases the amount the insurer must pay.
4. Carrier liability and cargo insurance are separate channels with different bases: the carrier's responsibility depends on the carriage rules and contract, with their defences and limits, while the policy responds only within its own terms, which exclude some causes of loss.
CORRECT: 4
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 4.

Carrier liability is the legal or contractual responsibility of a carrier for specified cargo loss, damage, or delay, and it operates under the applicable carriage rules and the contract of carriage, which attach defences, limits, and time requirements to it. Cargo insurance is a separate contract that responds to covered losses within its own terms, without requiring the insured to prove the carrier at fault. The two channels are distinct in basis, documents, and timing, and a professional pursues each on its own merits rather than treating one as a substitute for the other.

Answer 1 is the manager's error: all-risks cover means all risks of fortuitous physical loss or damage except the exclusions, so causes such as inherent vice or delay sit outside it, and no policy makes the carrier's position irrelevant. Answer 2 overstates the carrier's responsibility, which is neither automatic nor unlimited. Answer 3 invents a forced election; nothing in the lesson's claim procedure requires abandoning one channel to use the other, and the claim procedure requires notifying both insurer and carrier promptly.

The takeaway: know what each channel can and cannot answer, and never let the comfort of a policy replace the discipline of reading its terms.
