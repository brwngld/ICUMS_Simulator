# DRAFT — Lesson 11.2: Dangerous Goods and Special Cargo Handling

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Two kinds of cargo that change the rules
BODY:
Earlier courses treated cargo largely as a neutral quantity: cartons, bags, drums, and containers whose management questions were questions of cost, space, and time. Module 11 turns to risk and compliance, and this lesson deals with the cargo that changes the rules completely, because of what it can do when something goes wrong.

Dangerous goods are substances or articles presenting hazards during transportation and regulated under applicable transport safety requirements. Flammable liquids, compressed gases, corrosive chemicals, and lithium batteries are ordinary commercial examples. Two features of the definition deserve attention. First, the test is hazard in transport: a product may be safe on a shop shelf, yet present a genuine hazard inside a closed ship's hold, an aircraft hold at altitude, or a steel container heating up on a tropical road. Second, the regulation is not optional: if goods meet the definition, the requirements apply whoever ships them, and however small the consignment.

Special cargo is cargo requiring additional handling, documentation, packaging, or transportation precautions because of its characteristics. Frozen fish, temperature-sensitive medicines, machinery wider than a container opening, glass products, live animals, and high-value consignments all sit in this family. Dangerous goods are one branch of special cargo, and the most strictly regulated one, which is why they take the larger half of this lesson.

Why is compliance treated as absolute, when so much else in logistics is negotiated? Because the scale of failure is different. An ordinary cargo failure is a claim: goods are damaged or lost, an insurer pays, and the loss is measured in money. A dangerous-goods failure is a catastrophe: a fire in a vessel's hold far from land, a toxic release in a terminal, an event in an aircraft hold from which there is no recovery. Catastrophes are not compensated afterwards; they are prevented beforehand, which is why dangerous-goods rules are written as duties rather than choices, and why the first of those duties, a true declaration of what the goods are, sits with the shipper.

This lesson builds in four stages: the classification system, the compliance chain from packaging to acceptance, the modal rulebooks and the people who operate them, and the wider families of special cargo.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Classification is the foundation: the safety data sheet and the shipper's duty
BODY:
Every control in dangerous-goods compliance depends on getting one prior step right: classification, the process of deciding what the goods are under the system the rulebooks share. If classification is wrong, everything built on it is wrong: the packaging is selected for the wrong hazard, the label announces the wrong danger, the segregation decision is made on false information, and the emergency services plan for an event that will not be the one that happens. Classification is therefore not clerical work at the end of an export job. It is the foundation on which the whole consignment's compliance stands.

Where does a shipper start? With the safety data sheet (SDS), a document providing information about a chemical's hazards, safe handling, storage, and emergency measures. Manufacturers issue the SDS in a standard sixteen-section format, and it is the starting point for deciding whether goods are regulated for transport at all, and under what entry. Lesson 7.2, Air Cargo Operations and Cargo Acceptance, worked through reading an SDS for an air shipment; the same discipline opens every dangerous-goods movement in every mode.

Classification produces three outputs, and each becomes a permanent feature of the shipment.

1. The hazard class. A hazard class is a category used to classify dangerous goods according to their primary transport hazards. Classification answers which of the nine classes described in this lesson the goods belong to.

2. The UN number. A UN number is a four-digit identification number assigned to specified dangerous goods or groups of dangerous goods under the United Nations system, written with the prefix UN. UN 1950 identifies aerosols and UN 3480 identifies lithium-ion batteries, to take two widely met examples.

3. The proper shipping name. The proper shipping name is the designated transport name used to identify a dangerous substance or article under the applicable dangerous goods regulations. Trade names and marketing descriptions are not transport names; the proper shipping name is the one the rulebooks recognise.

The same chemical carries the same UN number in every country and every rulebook; what changes between modes is not the classification but the procedures built around it. And the duty is personal: the shipper owns classification. A supplier's brochure or a customer's assurance does not discharge it. The shipper reads the SDS, applies the rulebook, and stands behind the result.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The nine hazard classes
BODY:
The United Nations Model Regulations, on which all the modal rulebooks are built, divide dangerous goods into nine classes. Lesson 3.3, Cargo Types, Packaging, and Handling, introduced them briefly within its cargo map; this lesson treats them as a working system, because a logistics professional must be able to place real products into real classes.

1. Class 1, Explosives, divided into divisions 1.1 to 1.6 according to the nature of the explosive risk.

2. Class 2, Gases, with three divisions: 2.1 flammable gases, 2.2 non-flammable non-toxic gases, and 2.3 toxic gases.

3. Class 3, Flammable liquids, the class behind countless everyday products, from solvents and paints to fuels.

4. Class 4, Flammable solids, with three divisions: 4.1 flammable solids and self-reactive substances, 4.2 substances liable to spontaneous combustion, and 4.3 substances which emit flammable gas in contact with water.

5. Class 5, Oxidising substances and organic peroxides, with 5.1 oxidising substances and 5.2 organic peroxides.

6. Class 6, Toxic and infectious substances, with 6.1 toxic substances and 6.2 infectious substances.

7. Class 7, Radioactive material, a class of its own with no divisions.

8. Class 8, Corrosive substances, which attack living tissue and metal.

9. Class 9, Miscellaneous dangerous substances and articles, the class that gathers hazards not fully covered elsewhere. It matters increasingly in ordinary logistics, because it includes lithium batteries, dry ice, and environmentally hazardous substances.

Three observations complete the picture. Divisions within a class distinguish different forms of one primary hazard, which is why gases divide by flammability and toxicity. Class 9 shows that the system moves with commerce: a consignment of electronics containing lithium batteries, or a frozen consignment chilled with dry ice, is a dangerous-goods consignment even though it looks harmless. And classification turns on the primary hazard in transport, which is exactly the judgement the safety data sheet supports.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Packing groups: degree of danger, not size of box
BODY:
Within most classes, the rulebooks grade how dangerous one substance is compared with another of the same class. The instrument is the packing group. Packing group I denotes great danger, packing group II medium danger, and packing group III minor danger. Two flammable liquids in the same class can present very different levels of danger, and the packing group records that difference on the documents and drives the strictness of everything downstream.

Two boundaries matter. First, packing groups do not apply to every class: they are assigned in most classes but not in classes 1, 2 and 7. Explosives, gases, and radioactive material are governed by their own class-specific requirements instead. Second, and this is the error to dismantle now: a packing group is a degree of danger, not a packaging size. It does not mean a large drum, a medium box, or a small bottle. It measures how dangerous the substance is, and that measurement then determines the performance the packaging must deliver. The link between the two is performance packaging, treated in the next block, but the group itself is a statement about the substance, never about the box.

Reading packing groups correctly matters operationally: the group appears on the dangerous goods declaration, drives packaging selection, and informs stowage and segregation decisions. Misreading it as a size instruction produces absurd documents; ignoring it removes the grading that tells handlers which of two flammable liquids deserves the greater care.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Compatibility and segregation
BODY:
Some dangerous goods must not travel together. Segregation is the separation of incompatible dangerous goods or cargo according to applicable safety requirements, and it exists because some hazards interact: combined, they produce a risk that neither poses alone. The rulebooks carry the detailed requirements in tables that trained professionals consult; at the level of this lesson, the professional needs the concept and the habit.

The concept is clearest at the classic pairing. A flammable liquid supplies the fuel for a fire; an oxidising substance supplies the oxidiser that feeds it. Held apart, each is a managed hazard. Stowed together in an enclosed space, each makes the other's failure worse, which is why such combinations are kept apart by the segregation rules. Between that extreme and ordinary compatible stowage lies a spectrum of restrictions, and the rulebook table, not intuition, decides which applies to any given pairing.

The habit is procedural, and it connects directly to operations this programme has already taught. Lesson 5.3, Shipping Instructions and Cargo Consolidation, taught the checks a forwarder performs before consolidating several shippers' goods into one container. Lesson 7.3, Containerisation: FCL, LCL, and Container Types, taught how containers are packed. Wherever cargo from different sources comes together, in a container freight station or a consolidation, segregation becomes a live question, and the check is documentary: what classes are present, are they compatible, and does the rulebook allow this combination in this configuration? The check costs minutes. The failure it prevents can cost a vessel.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Packaging, marking, and labelling: making the package speak
BODY:
Classification identifies the hazard; the next links in the compliance chain contain it and communicate it. Each link exists for a purpose the professional should be able to state, not merely recite.

1. Performance packaging. Dangerous goods travel in UN-performance packaging: packaging designed, tested, and marked to perform to a standard matched to the hazard, rather than ordinary trade packaging chosen for appearance or price. The purpose is containment that survives the drops, stacking, vibration, and moisture of a real journey, and the packing group grades how demanding that standard must be.

2. Marking. Packages are marked with the proper shipping name and the UN number. The purpose is identification: anyone handling the package can know what it is without opening a file. A package marked only with a trade name is anonymous to the stevedore, the inspector, and the firefighter alike.

3. Hazard labels. A hazard label is a label communicating the hazard classification of dangerous goods, applied to the package in the standardised form the rulebooks prescribe. The purpose is instant recognition of the danger: the label speaks to a handler who has no time and no documents, and it informs the stowage, segregation, and emergency-response decisions taken at every interchange.

4. Placards. Where the mode requires them, placards, larger signs serving the same purpose as labels, are fixed to transport units and vehicles, so that the hazard of a whole load is visible from outside, to inspectors and, if the worst happens, to emergency services deciding how to respond.

The common idea is that the cargo announces itself. A dangerous-goods consignment correctly packaged, marked, and labelled carries its own safety instructions on its face, and every participant in the chain, including ones the shipper will never meet, can act on what they see.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The dangerous goods declaration and the shipment's document set
BODY:
A dangerous goods declaration is a document or electronic declaration providing required information about dangerous goods offered for transportation. It is the shipper's declaration: made in the form the applicable rulebook prescribes, given under the shipper's own authority, and stating the facts the rulebook requires. For a typical consignment those facts are the ones classification produced: the UN number, the proper shipping name, the hazard class, the packing group where one applies, the quantities and type of packaging, and confirmation that the goods are correctly packaged, marked, and labelled as the requirements demand.

The declaration's significance is easiest to see by imagining its absence. The carrier would be accepting cargo whose nature it does not know; the vessel or aircraft would be stowed without regard to the hazard; the crew would respond to an incident blind; and every downstream control would be built on silence. The declaration is the moment the shipper's knowledge becomes the whole chain's knowledge. That is why it is the shipper who gives it, and why a false or careless declaration is treated as one of the most serious failures in logistics.

Around the declaration sits the shipment's wider document set. The transport document itself, the bill of lading for a sea consignment or the air waybill for an air one, carries the dangerous-goods entries, so that the cargo's nature appears on the documents the whole trade runs on. The safety data sheet remains available through the information chain, so that handlers and responders can consult the full hazard picture. And the carrier's booking and manifest carry the dangerous-goods details, so that the vessel's or aircraft's stowage can be planned before the cargo moves. A dangerous-goods consignment is therefore documented into the declaration, into the transport documents, and into the carrier's records, and the entries must agree.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: One classification, three rulebooks: IMDG, IATA DGR, and the road question
BODY:
The United Nations system supplies the classification, and each transport mode applies it through its own rulebook. The professional must know which book governs which journey.

1. Sea: the IMDG Code. The IMDG Code, the International Maritime Dangerous Goods Code, is the rulebook governing the maritime transport of dangerous goods, mandatory under Chapter VII of the SOLAS convention and updated on a two-year amendment cycle. A container shipped from Tema towards Rotterdam travels under the IMDG Code for the sea leg.

2. Air: the ICAO Technical Instructions and the IATA DGR. The ICAO Technical Instructions are the international technical instructions governing the safe transport of dangerous goods by air. The IATA Dangerous Goods Regulations (DGR) are the industry regulations published by IATA for the air transport of dangerous goods, based on the ICAO Technical Instructions and updated annually. Lesson 7.2 taught acceptance under the DGR; the discipline is at its strictest in this mode, for the reason this lesson opened with: an aircraft cannot stop.

3. Road: the special case of ADR. ADR is a European agreement on the road transport of dangerous goods. It is the source of a persistent error, so the boundary must be stated plainly: ADR does not apply in Ghana or elsewhere in West Africa. What travels globally is the classification system, the nine classes and the UN numbers, which ADR shares with the IMDG Code and the DGR. The road leg of a West African journey follows the applicable national rules, and a professional who writes European references onto a West African road consignment has decorated the file rather than complied with anything.

For a multimodal movement the classification is constant while the regime switches leg by leg: the same drum of paint is a class 3 consignment under the IMDG Code aboard the vessel, under national road rules on the truck, and under the IATA DGR if it flies. Decide the mode first, then prepare the documents from that mode's rulebook.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Trained people, acceptance checks, and the price of a wrong declaration
BODY:
Every rulebook described so far assumes one thing the documents cannot supply: competent people. Dangerous-goods rules work only in the hands of personnel trained for their role, which is why requirements for training and authorisation are built into the rulebooks themselves, and why Lesson 7.2's air acceptance discipline, dangerous goods accepted only by trained and authorised staff checking the consignment against the regulatory checklist, is the visible tip of a rule that runs through every mode. The sea terminal's checker, the vessel's officer planning stowage, the driver of a tank truck, and the warehouse clerk signing off a consolidation each hold a role the system depends on.

The acceptance check is where the compliance chain closes. Before a carrier, a terminal, or a consolidator takes a dangerous-goods consignment, trained staff verify that the classification is plausible, the packaging and marks are correct, the labels are present, and the declaration is complete. Lesson 7.2 taught this as air cargo acceptance; the same gate exists on the sea side, to catch failures that slip past even conscientious shippers.

When the declaration is wrong, whether carelessly or deliberately, the protections do not degrade gracefully; they disappear. Packaging selected for the wrong hazard fails, stowage ignores the real danger, segregation is computed from false premises, and the crew that faces a fire or a leak reads documents describing a different cargo. Beyond the physical risk sits the commercial and legal one: consignments refused or quarantined, penalties imposed, liability for the consequences of the misdeclaration, and the loss of the standing that allows a shipper to move cargo at all. An incorrect declaration is not a paperwork error with a paperwork consequence. It is the removal of every protection the system built, committed to paper by the one participant who knew the truth.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Refrigerated cargo and the documented cold chain
BODY:
The second half of this lesson's title belongs to cargo that is not dangerous but still punishes negligence. Refrigerated cargo is goods requiring controlled temperatures during transportation or storage, and its compliance discipline is documentary: the cold chain must be provable, not merely maintained.

Lesson 7.3 treated the refrigerated container as equipment: insulated, fitted with its own cooling unit, and demanding electrical power wherever it stands. The compliance view adds four questions.

1. What is the set point, and who says so? The required temperature comes from the product's storage requirements, confirmed in writing at booking, not from the driver's habit or the previous shipment's setting. A wrong set point, faithfully maintained, still delivers spoiled cargo.

2. Was everything pre-cooled? The reefer container is pre-cooled before loading, and cargo whose condition depends on temperature is brought to the required temperature before it enters the box. Loading warm goods into a cold container shocks the goods and spends the equipment's capacity recovering instead of holding.

3. Does power continue at every leg? The container needs electricity aboard the vessel, in the terminal, and on the road, where a generator set supplies it. Every change of leg is a handover of responsibility for power, and a silent break in supply can ruin cargo that still looks normal on arrival.

4. Where are the records? Temperature recorders travel with the cargo, and the equipment logs its own performance. Downloaded at destination, these records are the journey's evidence.

That evidence is what decides disputes. When a temperature excursion, a period spent outside the required range, occurs and a claim follows, the temperature record locates the failure in time: which leg, whose custody, whose responsibility. A party with records can show where the chain broke; a party without records argues. Pharmaceutical shipments make the discipline strictest: medicines require documented temperature control from end to end, with the documentation treated as part of the product's quality, because a medicine that has travelled outside its range may be worthless even when it looks perfect.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Out-of-gauge, fragile, and high-value cargo: when specialist advice comes first
BODY:
Out-of-gauge cargo (OOG) is cargo whose dimensions exceed the standard internal or external limits of the intended transport equipment. Lesson 7.3 met its equipment: open-top containers for over-height pieces, flat racks for machinery that no closed box can swallow. The compliance questions ride on top of the equipment choice. An out-of-gauge lift is engineered before it is performed: a lifting plan sets the method, the equipment, and the rigging before a crane ever takes the load, because improvising with a piece weighing many tonnes is how terminals have accidents. An out-of-gauge road move is surveyed before it is driven: a route survey confirms that the roads, bridges, bends, and overhead clearances on the chosen path can actually pass the load. Both are concepts every planner should recognise, and both are work for specialists.

Fragile cargo is goods requiring protection against damage from shock, vibration, pressure, or improper handling. Its handling considerations are ordinary in kind and unforgiving in degree: packaging that cushions and supports, orientation marks that are respected, loading plans that keep weight off delicate pieces, and handling by people and equipment briefed on what is inside the crates.

Two further families complete the map, treated here lightly. Live animals travel under welfare requirements set by regulation: containers designed for the animals' needs, care arrangements for the journey, and the permissions the movement requires, all arranged before departure. High-value cargo attracts security requirements: sealed and controlled handling, restricted access, and sometimes authorised or escorted procedures, so that the cargo's value does not become its vulnerability.

The unifying rule of the special-cargo half of this lesson is this: the moment a consignment's characteristics exceed the ordinary operator's competence or equipment, specialist advice or authorisation must come before transportation, not after a failure. A chemical never shipped before, a lift beyond the terminal's usual capacity, a route nobody has surveyed, a document set that does not clearly fit any pattern: each is a signal to stop and consult. The cheap failure is the question asked early.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Classifying and shipping a cleaning compound from Tema
BODY:
A trading company in Tema supplies industrial cleaning products to an overseas buyer, shipping by sea from Tema Port. A new formulation is ordered in drums, and the company's export clerk must take it through the full dangerous-goods path for the first time. The product and details in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: Read the safety data sheet. The clerk obtains the manufacturer's SDS. Its transport-relevant sections identify the product as a flammable liquid graded packing group II. Under this lesson's classification, that makes it a class 3 consignment: the classification the SDS supports, never the one the trade name suggests.

Step 2: Identify the UN entry. From the SDS and the IMDG Code's index, the clerk confirms the proper shipping name and the UN number under which the product travels. The trade name remains on the commercial invoice; the proper shipping name goes onto the marks and the declaration.

Step 3: Select performance packaging. The product is packed in UN-performance packaging appropriate to a packing group II flammable liquid: drums tested and marked to the standard, not reconditioned containers of unknown history.

Step 4: Mark and label. Each drum is marked with the proper shipping name and the UN number, and carries the class 3 hazard label, applied so that it survives the journey and stays legible.

Step 5: Declare. The clerk completes the dangerous goods declaration for the sea leg under the IMDG Code, stating the UN number, proper shipping name, class, packing group, quantities, and packaging, and books the consignment with the shipping line as dangerous goods, so that the vessel's stowage can be planned for it.

Step 6: Acceptance. At the terminal, trained staff check the consignment before loading: marks, labels, declaration, packaging. The check passes, the drums are stowed in line with the vessel's dangerous-goods arrangements, and the shipment sails.

What does this example demonstrate? The compliance chain is a sequence in which each step consumes the previous one: classification feeds the packaging decision, the packaging feeds the marking, the marks feed the declaration, and the declaration feeds the acceptance check. Skipping a step does not speed the shipment; it unbuilds every step after it.

Key lesson: the shipper who walks the chain in order owns a compliant consignment. The declaration is credible precisely because every step behind it was actually performed.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: The consolidation check that caught an incompatible pair
BODY:
A freight forwarder consolidating groupage cargo at a container freight station near Tema Port is filling a forty-foot container for a regional sea service. Two consignments are on the receiving list: household goods, and a consignment of swimming-pool treatment chemicals documented as class 5.1, oxidising substances. A third booking arrives late: a consignment of paint documented as class 3, flammable liquids. Space is tight, and the warehouse supervisor's first plan slots the paint into the same container to fill the last gap. This example is illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: The check. Lesson 5.3 taught that consolidation is a documented checking process, not a loading exercise. Among the checks is compatibility: what classes are present in this container, and are they permitted together? The forwarder's DG-trained clerk pulls both declarations before approving the plan.

Step 2: The finding. An oxidising substance and a flammable liquid are the classic incompatible pairing: one supplies the fuel for a fire, the other supplies the oxidiser that feeds it. Sealed together in a container at sea, each makes the other's failure worse. This is precisely the combination the segregation concept exists to question, and the clerk will not approve it on personal judgment alone.

Step 3: The decision. The paint is booked onto the next sailing instead, into a container carrying no oxidising cargo. The rework costs the forwarder an illustrative additional handling charge, a day of replanning, and a difficult telephone call to the paint consignor, whose vessel connection slips by a week.

Step 4: The counterfactual. Had the check been skipped, the container would have sailed with the two consignments stowed together. In all probability nothing would have happened: most voyages pass without incident, which is exactly why incompatible consolidations accumulate, because the absence of disaster reads as proof of safety. But given a leaking paint drum, a failed valve on a chemical drum, and heat in the hold, the vessel's crew would have been fighting a fire fed by its own cargo, with documents that listed each consignment separately and no combination anyone had approved.

What does this example demonstrate? Segregation is a check with a place in the process, and the place is before the container is packed, while the plan is still on paper and the fix costs a telephone call rather than a salvage operation.

Key lesson: the consolidation check that feels redundant on a quiet afternoon is the one standing between routine cargo and a stowage decision nobody would defend afterwards.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A cold chain documented end to end: pharmaceuticals from Rotterdam to Accra
BODY:
A pharmaceutical distributor in Accra imports a temperature-sensitive medicine from a manufacturer whose regional distribution centre stands in Rotterdam. The product must be held within a specified temperature range until it is delivered in Accra, and both parties treat the documentation of that range as part of the product itself. The product, route, and details in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: The set point is fixed in writing. At booking, the required temperature range is stated on the booking and confirmed against the product's storage requirements. The reefer container is specified, and the set point is recorded on the equipment handover documents.

Step 2: Pre-cooling and loading. At Rotterdam, the container is pre-cooled to its set point before stuffing, and the medicine, already held at the required temperature, is loaded. An independent temperature recorder is placed with the cargo, logging the journey separately from the container's own equipment.

Step 3: Power continuity at every leg. The container travels to the port on the terminal's power, crosses to Tema aboard the vessel on the ship's power supply, and completes the road leg to Accra on a generator set. At each interchange, the power source and the set point are verified and recorded, because each is a change of custody as well as a change of plug.

Step 4: The excursion. At Accra, the distributor downloads the temperature records before releasing the medicine for sale. The record shows one excursion: a rise above the required range lasting some hours during the road leg, after the container left the terminal.

Step 5: The claim, decided on records. The distributor rejects the affected stock and claims. The records decide the question cleanly: the container's log and the cargo recorder agree that temperature held correctly through the sea voyage and the terminal stay, and the excursion began only on the road leg, in the haulier's custody. The haulier's generator log for the same period confirms a power interruption. Responsibility locates itself in the documents: the claim is pursued against the party whose custody matches the failure, and the earlier, unaffected legs are not dragged into it.

What does this example demonstrate? The cold chain was not merely maintained; it was evidenced. Records converted what could have been a three-way argument into an evidence question with one answer.

Key lesson: in temperature-controlled logistics, the documentation is not paperwork about the cargo. It is the proof of what the cargo experienced, and it is worth exactly as much as the discipline with which each leg was recorded.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: "It is only a small quantity" is how dangerous-goods incidents begin
BODY:
Every dangerous-goods trainer meets the sentence: it is only a small quantity, a few bottles, one carton, a sample for the customer. The sentence is the most dangerous in this subject, and this lesson ends its authority here.

The rules do not regulate danger by the pallet. They regulate substances and articles that present hazards in transport, and a single leaking bottle of flammable liquid in a closed container, an undeclared aerosol in an aircraft hold, or one damaged lithium battery in a carton of mixed goods can begin an event that no quantity of paperwork afterwards will stop. Quantity changes the value of the goods; it does not change the nature of the hazard they can start.

Undeclared dangerous goods are the gravest compliance failure in this subject, graver than a late declaration, a wrong label, or a packaging defect, because they strip every protection silently. Lesson 7.2 taught why air acceptance checks consignments rather than trusting them: the system assumes that some shippers will not declare, and builds a last line of defence at acceptance. A professional who finds regulated goods travelling as general cargo stops the consignment, whatever the cost of stopping it.

The working rule is symmetrical. If the goods are regulated, declare them, whatever the quantity. If you are not willing to declare them, do not ship them.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Packing groups grade danger, not packaging size
BODY:
The most common beginner's misreading of the classification system is to hear packing group and picture packaging: group I, a big strong crate; group III, a small light carton. The picture is wrong, and documents built on it are wrong with it.

A packing group is a statement about the substance: how much danger it presents within its class. Packing group I denotes great danger, II medium danger, III minor danger. What the group does downstream is select the performance standard the packaging must meet, so that a greater danger travels in packaging tested to a more demanding standard. The size and shape of that packaging follow the product and the journey, not the group.

Remember also the boundary taught earlier: packing groups are not assigned at all in classes 1, 2 and 7. Explosives, gases, and radioactive material carry no packing group on their documents, and their packaging requirements come from their own class rules. A document showing a packing group against a gas cylinder is not a variant reading. It is an error to correct before the consignment moves.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: The SDS starts the process but never replaces the declaration
BODY:
Two errors live at the boundary between the safety data sheet and the dangerous goods declaration, and this note closes both.

The first is omission: a shipper reads the SDS, decides the product looks harmless, and ships it as general cargo. The rule this lesson teaches is that if the SDS says regulated, the goods are regulated. The shipper does not get a second opinion on the hazard. The shipper owns the classification, and optimism is not a classification method.

The second is substitution: a shipper attaches the SDS to the consignment and considers the dangerous-goods obligation discharged. It is not. The SDS is the starting point for deciding whether goods are regulated for transport, and under what entry; it is not the transport declaration. The declaration is a separate document, in the form the modal rulebook prescribes, given by the shipper, carrying the UN number, proper shipping name, class, packing group, and packaging confirmation. The SDS informs the classification; the declaration declares it. A consignment with an SDS and no declaration is a consignment whose hazards are documented and whose compliance does not exist.

The professional habit that joins the three stages: SDS first, classification second, declaration third, and nothing shipped until all three agree.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A trading company in Accra is shipping twelve bottles of a flammable cleaning solvent by sea to an overseas buyer. The product's safety data sheet identifies it as a flammable liquid. The sales manager proposes declaring it as general cargo, arguing that twelve bottles is too small a quantity to matter.

Which statement best describes the correct course of action?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The consignment may ship as general cargo, because quantities this small fall outside the dangerous-goods classification system.
2. The consignment must be classified, packaged, marked, labelled, and declared as dangerous goods, because regulation follows the hazard identified in the safety data sheet, not the quantity offered for shipment.
3. The consignment may ship as general cargo by sea, although a declaration would be required if the same goods travelled by air.
4. The freight forwarder may decide whether a declaration is required, since the forwarder accepts the consignment on the carrier's behalf.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Dangerous goods are regulated because of the hazard they present in transport, and the safety data sheet is the starting point for deciding whether goods are regulated. A flammable liquid does not become unregulated because the consignment is small: quantity changes the value of the cargo, not the nature of the hazard. The compliant path is the full chain taught in this lesson: classify from the SDS, use performance packaging, mark and label the packages, and make the dangerous goods declaration under the rulebook governing the mode, which for this sea shipment is the IMDG Code.

Answer 1 is the exact misconception this lesson warns against: undeclared dangerous goods are the gravest compliance failure in the subject, because they silently remove packaging, stowage, segregation, and emergency-response protections. Answer 3 invents a sea exemption that does not exist; the sea leg has its own rulebook and its own declaration. Answer 4 misplaces the duty: the declaration is the shipper's own responsibility and cannot be delegated to the forwarder's discretion.

The takeaway: if the goods are regulated, declare them whatever the quantity; if you are not willing to declare them, do not ship them.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
While preparing documentation for a consolidated shipment, a clerk enters a packing group against a consignment of compressed gas cylinders and explains to a colleague that packing group II means the cylinders must travel in a medium-sized package.

Which statement best describes the clerk's work?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Both the entry and the explanation are in order, because gases carry packing groups and packing group II fixes the size of package required.
2. The packing group entry is wrong, because packing groups are not assigned in classes 1, 2 and 7, and the explanation is wrong, because a packing group grades the degree of danger a substance presents, not the size of its packaging.
3. The packing group entry is wrong because gases always carry packing group I, but the explanation about package size is correct.
4. Both are acceptable in practice, because packing groups are optional entries that shippers may apply as they prefer.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The clerk has made both errors this lesson isolates. Compressed gas cylinders belong to class 2, and packing groups are assigned in most classes but not in classes 1, 2 and 7; explosives, gases, and radioactive material are governed by their own class-specific requirements, so no packing group belongs on the cylinders' documents. Even where a packing group does apply, it is a statement about the substance: packing group I denotes great danger, II medium danger, and III minor danger. The group determines the performance standard the packaging must meet; it says nothing about the size or shape of the package, which follows the product and the journey.

Answer 1 accepts both errors. Answer 3 corrects the entry with a fiction, since gases carry no packing group at all, while keeping the size misconception. Answer 4 treats a grading system as a free-form field, which it is not: the packing group appears on the dangerous goods declaration and drives packaging selection and segregation decisions.

The takeaway: the packing group answers how dangerous, never how big, and for classes 1, 2 and 7 the correct answer is that no packing group exists.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A freight forwarder in Accra arranges the final leg of a dangerous-goods consignment: a container discharged at Tema Port is trucked to a warehouse in Kumasi. A colleague preparing the file states that the European ADR agreement governs this road leg, because ADR is the world rulebook for dangerous goods by road.

Which statement best describes the applicable position?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The colleague is right, because ADR applies wherever dangerous goods travel by road, including West Africa.
2. The colleague is wrong, because no rules govern dangerous goods on West African roads, so the consignment may be documented as ordinary cargo for the truck leg.
3. The colleague is wrong, because ADR is a European agreement that does not apply in West Africa; the nine classes and UN numbers travel globally, and the road leg follows the applicable national rules.
4. The colleague is wrong, because the IMDG Code that governed the sea voyage continues to govern the goods until delivery, including the road leg.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The classification system is global: the same substance carries the same hazard class and UN number in every rulebook, which is why the consignment classified for the sea voyage under the IMDG Code needs no reclassification for the road leg. But the rulebooks themselves are modal and regional. ADR is a European agreement on the road transport of dangerous goods, and it does not apply in Ghana or elsewhere in West Africa. The road leg of this journey follows the applicable national rules. The professional error to avoid is decorating a West African road file with European references and mistaking that for compliance.

Answer 1 states the exact misconception this lesson dismantles. Answer 2 overcorrects into lawlessness: that ADR does not apply does not mean nothing applies, and documenting regulated goods as ordinary cargo is the gravest failure in the subject. Answer 4 confuses the classification, which does continue with the goods, with the rulebook, which changes with the mode: the IMDG Code governs the sea leg, not the truck.

The takeaway: the classification travels; the rulebook is chosen by the mode, and on West African roads the applicable rules are national.
