=== ASSESSMENT Q1 | tests lesson 11.1 ===
QUESTION:
A factory in Kumasi buys a shrink-wrapping line from an overseas machinery manufacturer on EXW terms. The factory engages a regional trucking firm, whose truck and crew arrive at the manufacturer's works to collect. The machine is placed at the buyer's disposal in the yard, and while the buyer's own crew is rigging it onto the buyer's truck, the machine slips from its lifting sling and is damaged. The factory demands that the manufacturer compensate the loss, arguing that the damage happened on the seller's own premises before the journey even began.

Which statement best describes the position?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The manufacturer must compensate, because the machine was still standing on the seller's premises when it was damaged, and responsibility stays with the seller until the goods physically leave the seller's ground.
2. The trucking firm must compensate the factory in full, because a carrier that has taken goods into its custody is automatically liable for the full commercial value of anything damaged in its hands.
3. Under EXW the risk passed to the buyer when the goods were placed at the buyer's disposal at the seller's premises, so the collection and the loading were at the buyer's risk; EXW imposes no insurance duty on the seller, and covering the collection leg was the buyer's own arrangement to make.
4. The manufacturer must compensate, because a seller always insures the goods until collection is complete, so the seller's insurers must answer for damage occurring before the truck departs.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The lesson's three-step sequence runs: identify the risk, allocate it by the trade term, then ask who insures. Under EXW the risk passes to the buyer when the goods are placed at the buyer's disposal at the seller's premises. From that moment the machine's exposure — the collection, the loading, and the journey — belongs to the buyer, whose own crew and contractor were performing the lift. The party holding the risk on each leg is the party whose money is exposed and the natural buyer of cover for it, so arranging insurance for the collection was the buyer's own decision to take or skip; EXW, like every rule except CIF and CIP, contains no duty on the seller to insure at all.

Answer 1 moves the risk onto the physical ground: responsibility under a trade term follows the agreed transfer point, not the location where the loss happened, and the machine stood at the buyer's disposal before the lift began. Answer 2 overstates carrier liability, which is neither automatic nor unlimited — the carriage rules and the contract attach defences and limits — and the trucking firm here is the buyer's own contractor, engaged on the buyer's terms. Answer 4 invents an insurance obligation running until collection is complete; only CIF and CIP oblige a seller to insure, and neither the seller's premises nor the incomplete loading creates one.

The takeaway: fix the risk-transfer point before asking who pays and who insures; on an EXW collection the buyer owns the risk from the moment the goods stand at its disposal.

=== ASSESSMENT Q2 | tests lesson 11.1 ===
QUESTION:
An importing company in Tema buys two consignments from two overseas suppliers. The first, finished ceramic sanitary ware, is sold on CIP Tema terms. The second, a bulk consignment of a robust raw commodity, is sold on CIF Tema terms. Both sellers arrange insurance and send certificates of insurance, and on checking the two documents the company's clerk finds that both policies were written at Institute Cargo Clauses (C). The clerk asks whether the two covers comply with the sales' terms.

Which statement best describes the position of the two policies?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Both policies comply, because the seller under either term may select whichever ladder level best matches the cargo's character, since the Incoterms 2020 insurance duties name no particular level.
2. The CIF policy complies and the CIP policy falls short: under CIF the seller must insure at minimum Institute Cargo Clauses (C) for the buyer's benefit, while under CIP the required minimum is the higher (A) level, whatever the cargo's character.
3. Both policies fall short, because CIF and CIP both impose Institute Cargo Clauses (A) as an absolute maximum that no policy may exceed without breaching the trade term.
4. The CIP policy complies and the CIF policy falls short, because sea-only CIF demands the widest cover while multimodal CIP, whose risks are spread across more carriers, demands only (C).
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Only two of the eleven Incoterms 2020 rules contain a duty to insure: CIF and CIP. The duty fixes a minimum level for each. Under CIF the seller must insure at minimum Institute Cargo Clauses (C) for the buyer's benefit; under CIP the seller must insure at the higher (A) level, reflecting CIP's use across all modes and commonly for higher-value, damage-prone consignments. The bulk consignment sold on CIF is therefore correctly covered at (C), and the sanitary ware sold on CIP is not: whatever one thinks of the cargo's character, (C) sits below the minimum the CIP rule itself imposes. The duty is a floor and not a ceiling — the parties may agree more, and the cargo's character may justify more — but the term's minimum binds where the contract is silent.

Answer 1 dissolves the minimums by pretending the rules name no level; both rules name theirs, and the ladder exists precisely so the level can be stated. Answer 3 reverses the logic: the stated levels are minimums the parties may exceed, never maximums they may not. Answer 4 swaps the two duties, reasoning from mode breadth to cover breadth; the allocation is fixed by the rules themselves, and no sea-versus-multimodal argument lowers the CIP requirement.

The takeaway: under CIF check the certificate for (C) or better, under CIP for (A) or better — and the buyer's proof is the certificate of insurance the term entitles it to receive.

=== ASSESSMENT Q3 | tests lesson 11.1 ===
QUESTION:
A general cargo vessel touches bottom on a shoal while leaving a river port fully laden. To refloat her before the tide turns, the master orders part of the deck cargo jettisoned into the river. The vessel comes free, and both she and the remainder of the cargo complete the voyage safely. Aboard are consignments for two importers: Importer M, whose containers were lifted overboard in the jettison, and Importer N, whose containers below deck were never touched. On arrival, the vessel's owners declare general average, and Importer N is asked to post security for a contribution to the sacrifice.

Which statement best describes the outcome for the two importers?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Importer M bears its loss alone, because cargo deliberately jettisoned sits outside every sharing rule, while Importer N, whose goods were untouched, owes nothing to anyone.
2. The jettison was the master's unilateral act, so the vessel's owners alone must compensate Importer M in full, and the declaration cannot bind Importer N without its prior written consent.
3. The declaration is invalid because the voyage ended safely; general average can arise only where the vessel itself is lost.
4. Importer M claims its sacrifice through the general average adjustment, and Importer N contributes in proportion to the value its cargo brought safe to the adventure, because the sacrifice preserved the common adventure; cargo owners ordinarily post security for their contributions, typically through their insurers, before delivery.
CORRECT: 4
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 4.

General average is the maritime principle, codified in the York-Antwerp Rules, under which a voluntary sacrifice, such as cargo jettisoned to lighten a vessel, or an extraordinary expense, deliberately made to preserve the common adventure from a common peril, is shared proportionally among the ship, the cargo, and the freight interests that the rescue saved. The jettison freed a vessel whose hull, remaining cargo, and earnings the sacrifice preserved, so every cargo interest contributes roughly in proportion to the value its cargo brought safe to the adventure. Importer M's claim for the containers lost overboard and Importer N's contribution are settled inside the same adjustment, and security is ordinarily required before the goods move. The lesson adds the ladder connection: general average sacrifice is a recognised peril covered even at the narrowest level, Institute Cargo Clauses (C).

Answer 1 applies ordinary own-loss intuition on both sides at once; the point of the sharing rule is that the rescue was made for everyone who benefited, the touched and untouched alike. Answer 2 demands a consent the principle never asks for; the master's authority to sacrifice in the common peril is exactly what general average exists to price. Answer 3 confuses general average with total loss; the rule operates when the adventure is saved, not when it is lost.

The takeaway: a rescue for one is a charge on all, and an untouched cargo owner's policy, even at (C), turns the demand into a filed problem instead of a funded one.

=== ASSESSMENT Q4 | tests lesson 11.2 ===
QUESTION:
An export clerk at a trading company near Tema Port prepares the company's first sea shipment of a newly reformulated industrial coating, packed in new UN-performance drums. Under time pressure, the clerk never obtains the product's safety data sheet. Working from the supplier's marketing flyer, which describes the coating as low-odour and user-friendly, the clerk marks each drum with the product's trade name, applies a flammable-liquid hazard label copied from the previous formulation's consignment, and completes a declaration giving the trade name only — no UN number, proper shipping name, hazard class, or packing group.

Which statement best identifies the first point at which the compliance chain failed, and the consequence?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The first failure is classification: the shipper's duty is to read the safety data sheet and classify the new formulation before anything else, because the packaging standard, the marks, the labels and the declaration are all built on that classification, and a declaration naming a trade name instead of a UN number and proper shipping name presents the chain's outputs without their foundation.
2. The first failure is documentary: the consignment would be compliant if the supplier's marketing flyer were attached to the file, since the flyer can stand in for the missing safety data sheet.
3. The first failure is physical: new UN-performance drums make the consignment compliant whatever the papers say, because containment is what actually protects the handlers.
4. The first failure is at the terminal: responsibility passed to the carrier's trained acceptance staff the moment the container left the gate, so the shipper's file cannot affect the consignment's compliance afterwards.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

Classification is the foundation of dangerous-goods compliance, and the shipper owns it personally: the safety data sheet is read, the rulebook applied, and the results — the hazard class, the UN number, the proper shipping name, and the packing group where one applies — stand behind everything that follows. The performance standard of the packaging is selected for the classified hazard; the marks state the UN number and proper shipping name the classification produced; the hazard label announces the class; and the declaration declares those facts in the form the modal rulebook prescribes. A consignment classified from a marketing flyer has skipped the only step that generates the facts, so the marks, the label and the declaration present outputs without a foundation — and the declaration, the shipper's own statement, names no UN number or proper shipping name at all.

Answer 2 repeats the substitution error the lesson's closing note dismantles: the safety data sheet is the starting point for deciding whether goods are regulated, never a substitute for the declaration, and a marketing flyer is not a safety data sheet at all. Answer 3 separates containment from communication; packaging alone makes no consignment compliant, because the chain exists so that every handler and responder knows what is inside. Answer 4 misplaces the duty: the trained acceptance check is a gate that catches failures, and it presupposes — it does not replace — the shipper's work before the gate.

The takeaway: SDS first, classification second, declaration third, and nothing shipped until all three agree.

=== ASSESSMENT Q5 | tests lesson 11.2 ===
QUESTION:
A chemical distributor in Ghana imports two class 3 flammable liquids from the same overseas supplier. On the two dangerous goods declarations, solvent X is assigned packing group I and solvent Y is assigned packing group III. Reviewing the file, a new member of staff asks what the difference between the two entries means for the operation.

Which statement best describes what the packing groups record and change?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The entries record package size: solvent X travels in larger drums and solvent Y in smaller ones, and the distributor should order its packaging to match the group number.
2. The entries are the supplier's commercial gradings with no operational meaning; only the hazard class, class 3 in both cases, affects packaging and stowage decisions.
3. Solvent X presents greater danger within its class than solvent Y: the packing group grades the degree of danger the substance presents — I denoting great danger, II medium danger and III minor danger — and drives the performance standard the packaging must meet, together with the stowage and segregation care around the consignment.
4. Solvent Y presents the greater danger, because the higher numeral denotes the higher danger, in the same way that the hazard class numbers climb from 1 to 9.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

A packing group is a statement about the substance: within its class, packing group I denotes great danger, II medium danger, and III minor danger. Solvent X is therefore graded the more dangerous of the two class 3 liquids, and the grading does real work downstream: it selects the performance standard the UN-performance packaging must meet, it appears on the dangerous goods declaration, and it informs the stowage and segregation decisions taken around both consignments. It grades how dangerous, never how big.

Answer 1 is the misreading the lesson isolates early: the group determines the performance the packaging must deliver, not a drum size, which follows the product and the journey. Answer 2 strips the entries of meaning; the packing group is precisely the instrument that distinguishes two substances sharing one class, which is why it travels on the documents. Answer 4 inverts the scale: the ladder's letters climb with protection, but the packing-group numerals run the other way, I above II above III, and nothing in the rulebooks reads group III as more dangerous than group I.

The takeaway: read the packing group as the substance's danger rating within its class — I the greatest, III the least — and let it set the packaging standard and the care around the consignment; and remember that for classes 1, 2 and 7 no packing group exists at all.

=== ASSESSMENT Q6 | tests lesson 11.2 ===
QUESTION:
A freight forwarder is consolidating a forty-foot container at a container freight station for a regional sea service. Already booked inside is a documented consignment of a class 5.1 oxidising substance. A late booking arrives: drums of solvent-based printing ink documented as class 3, flammable liquids, which would exactly fill the container's last gap. The warehouse supervisor, with many years of experience at the station, proposes to load the ink: both consignments are sealed and sound, the pairing has been carried many times over the years without incident, and the space would otherwise sail empty.

Which statement best describes the position?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The supervisor's experience is the appropriate authority here: segregation decisions rest on personal professional judgment, and years without incidents are practical evidence that this pairing is safe.
2. The consolidation check is documentary and comes before packing: the classes present are checked for compatibility under the applicable requirements before the container is packed, because a flammable liquid and an oxidising substance each make the other's failure worse; many quiet voyages in the past are not evidence about the next one.
3. Segregation requirements govern cargo on open vehicles and decks; inside a closed container any pairing may be stowed as space allows, because the container walls separate the goods.
4. The pairing would be acceptable if the two consignments travelled on separate pallets, since segregation requires only that incompatible goods do not touch.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Segregation is the separation of incompatible dangerous goods according to applicable safety requirements, and the flammable-liquid-and-oxidiser pairing is the classic incompatibility the lesson teaches: one supplies the fuel for a fire, the other the oxidiser that feeds it, and sealed together in a container at sea each makes the other's failure worse. The check is procedural and documentary, and it has a place in the process: before the container is packed, while the plan is still on paper, the classes present are checked for compatibility under the applicable requirements — the same consolidation discipline that treats groupage as a documented checking process rather than a loading exercise. Caught at that moment, the fix costs a re-booking and a telephone call instead of a stowage decision nobody would defend afterwards.

Answer 1 substitutes experience for the check. Long experience of quiet voyages is exactly the trap the lesson's counterfactual dismantles: most voyages pass without incident, which is why incompatible consolidations accumulate — the absence of disaster reads as proof of safety until the day a leaking drum and heat in the hold meet an oxidiser. Answer 3 promotes the container walls into a segregation solution; the closed container is precisely the enclosed space in which the combination is worst. Answer 4 reduces segregation to touching, which the applicable requirements do not do.

The takeaway: the consolidation check is documentary, it happens before packing, and it is never satisfied by years of nothing having happened yet.

=== ASSESSMENT Q7 | tests lesson 11.3 ===
QUESTION:
At a delivery to a warehouse in Kumasi, a driver comparing the container's seal number against the delivery documents finds that the high-security seal on the doors does not match the number recorded on the bill of lading. A colleague recalls that the container was opened for a customs examination while in transit — and the journey file contains a dated record of that examination, showing the authority for the opening, the reason, the time and place, and the number of the replacement seal applied, all recorded at the time.

Which statement best describes how the driver should treat this position?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The mismatch is explained by a documented story: the record shows who broke the original seal, on whose authority, why, and which replacement seal was fitted, so the consignment may proceed to its normal checks and stripping, since a seal broken with a record is not tampering.
2. Any difference between the seal on the doors and the bill of lading makes the container evidence in every case, and even a documented customs examination cannot legitimise it, so the delivery must be refused outright.
3. The colleague's recollection is sufficient: a verbal account of a customs stop witnessed by the crew settles the matter, and the container may be opened and stripped on that understanding.
4. The driver should cut off the replacement seal, fit a new seal of his own, note the new number on the delivery documents, and complete the delivery so the vehicle is not delayed further.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

The seal discipline treats a seal that disagrees with the documents as a stop event — but the lesson's second boundary matters here: the discipline does not forbid breaking a seal, it forbids breaking one without a story. Whenever a seal must be broken and replaced before delivery, the event is documented at the time: who broke it, on whose authority, why, when and where, who witnessed it, and the numbers of both the old seal and the new. The transit examination's record carries exactly those elements, so the mismatch on the doors is explained continuity rather than an unexplained story, and the delivery proceeds to its normal checks.

Answer 2 overreaches in the opposite direction: a documented, authoritative break is not tampering, and treating every recorded examination as a refusal would break legitimate trade. Answer 3 accepts memory in place of record; a recollection is a story told after the fact, and the lesson's rule is that records written at the time locate the break in the chain while a story told later locates nothing — to the next stranger who checks the seal, an undocumented replacement is indistinguishable from tampering. Answer 4 performs the silent break-and-replace the discipline condemns: it destroys physical evidence and fabricates documentary continuity.

The takeaway: a seal may be broken, but never silently; the story must be written down at the time, with both numbers, so that continuity — not contradiction — faces the next checker.

=== ASSESSMENT Q8 | tests lesson 11.3 ===
QUESTION:
Two exporters ship consignments on the same vessel. After discharge overseas, prohibited items are found inside both containers, inserted after packing by other parties in the chain. Exporter P supervised its packing, verified the packed goods against the packing list before sealing, applied and recorded a high-security seal at packing, and handed the container onward through signed handovers to vetted carriers. Exporter Q's container was packed by an unsupervised casual crew, and Q cannot show what was checked, when the container was sealed, or who handled it afterwards.

Which statement best describes the two exporters' positions?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The two positions are identical, because a shipper answers for everything found in its container whatever it did, and nothing it can show makes any difference.
2. Exporter P is exempt from all scrutiny, because a recorded seal is a guarantee that the container was never opened, while Exporter Q is treated as the importer of the prohibited items.
3. Neither exporter can be questioned, because responsibility for goods inserted after packing lies with parties outside the trade chain altogether.
4. Exporter P can use its records — supervised packing, the pack verified against the list before sealing, the seal applied and recorded at packing, and signed handovers to known parties — to show what it packed and where its control ended, so an insertion can be demonstrated to have happened to it; Exporter Q, unable to account for its own load, cannot draw that line.
CORRECT: 4
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 4.

The container carries the shipper's name and the shipper's declaration, and if prohibited goods inserted after packing are found inside, the discovery happens against that shipper first — to customs, to the carrier, and to the buyer. Innocence is therefore an outcome the shipper must be able to demonstrate, not a status anyone grants on request. Exporter P's controls are the demonstration: supervised packing means the shipper knew what entered the container; the pack verified against the packing list fixed the record at the moment of greatest knowledge; the high-security seal applied and recorded at packing froze that moment into evidence; and signed handovers to vetted parties protected the evidence along the chain. Those records let P show what it packed and where its control ended, so the insertion can be demonstrated to have happened to it rather than because of it.

Answer 1 is the fatalism the lesson exists to prevent: identical discoveries are not identical positions, because one shipper can produce the trail and the other cannot. Answer 2 overstates the seal into an absolute guarantee; any seal can be broken, and its number is evidence, not a certificate of inviolability. Answer 3 invents strangers to the transaction; the parties who must be answered are the ones whose systems carry the consignment.

The takeaway: integrity discipline is self-protection — it is how the honest operator proves it was honest when someone else was not.

=== ASSESSMENT Q9 | tests lesson 11.3 ===
QUESTION:
Late on a Friday, the operations desk of a logistics company in Accra receives an email appearing to come from a long-standing overseas customer, instructing that a container due for delivery next week be redirected to a different warehouse, with new collection contact details. The email is written in the customer's usual style and signs off with a name the desk recognises.

Which response best reflects the discipline the lesson teaches for instructions and data?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Act on the instruction without delay: the writing style and a familiar signature are sufficient identification, and holding up a confirmed customer's instruction would damage the service relationship.
2. Verify the instruction by calling the customer on a number taken from the company's own records before anything is acted on, and let the goods move only when the instruction, the record, and the physical reality agree; an emailed change of delivery details is verified, never assumed.
3. Reply to the email asking the sender to confirm the change, and act once a reply arrives, since the email exchange itself establishes who sent the instruction.
4. Decline every change of instruction once a consignment is moving, since delivery details can never be altered after dispatch under any circumstances.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Documentation and data fraud is mostly impersonation: falsified release or delivery instructions, manipulated consignment details, documents altered after issuance. The controls are procedural. Instruction verification requires that a change of delivery details received by email be verified by calling the counterparty on a known number — one taken from the company's own records, not from the suspect message — before it is acted on. The three-way match states the same logic generally: instruction, physical reality, and record must agree before goods move. Call-back verification costs minutes; a diverted load costs the load.

Answer 1 authenticates by style and a familiar signature, which is exactly what a competent impersonation copies; nothing inside the email itself proves who sent it. Answer 3 verifies the instruction through the instruction's own channel: a reply from the same address, or the same compromise, confirms nothing, and an email exchange is not the independent second source the discipline requires. Answer 4 overcorrects into a rule no operation can run on; changes of instruction are legitimate and often urgent, and the discipline governs how they are verified, not whether they may exist.

The takeaway: an emailed change of delivery details is verified on a known number before a wheel turns — and instruction, record, and reality must agree before goods move.

=== ASSESSMENT Q10 | tests lesson 11.4 ===
QUESTION:
At a warehouse yard near Tema, a delivery of assorted industrial goods is being unloaded when a drum on one pallet is found leaking a flammable liquid, and several cartons beneath it are soaked. The yard supervisor must direct the team's first actions.

Which sequence best reflects the incident-response framework the module teaches?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Notify the insurer and the carrier first and hold the scene exactly as it is until their instructions arrive, since commercial notification must precede every operational step.
2. Photograph the drum, the pallet positions and the soaked cartons in detail first, then stabilise the leak, since evidence must be preserved before anything else is disturbed.
3. Make safe first — protect people and stabilise the leaking drum — then preserve the evidence as found: photographs before anything is moved or cleaned, the damaged goods and packaging retained and segregated, nothing repaired or repacked; then notify the parties each within its own process — the forwarder and carrier, the insurer by claim notification, and the authorities a dangerous-goods incident brings in, with safety-related notification preceding commercial steps.
4. Repack the undamaged stock into sound cartons to protect its saleability, clean the yard before the spill spreads, and file the incident report once the outcome of any claim is known.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The incident-response framework has six movements in order: detect early, contain and make safe, preserve evidence, notify the right parties, resolve and record, and learn and improve. The leaking flammable drum invokes the second movement's absolute priority — safety precedes commerce, always — and the dangerous-goods handling taught in Lesson 11.2 governs the stabilisation before any claim is discussed. Only then is evidence preserved: photographs before anything is moved or cleaned, the damaged goods and their packaging retained and segregated, nothing repaired or repacked — because the surveyor surveys what is preserved, and a scene tidied is evidence destroyed. Notification follows, each party within its own process: the forwarder and carrier, the insurer by claim notification while the evidence is fresh, and the authorities a dangerous-goods incident brings in — with safety-related notification preceding commercial notification.

Answer 1 holds a live flammable leak hostage to claims instructions and inverts the ordering: commercial notification is one movement among several, and none precedes containment. Answer 2 photographs first, treating evidence as more urgent than the people in the yard; a stabilised scene can still be photographed, and a protected scene is itself the stronger evidence. Answer 4 performs the exact destruction the lesson's Important Note condemns — repacking, cleaning, and withholding the report until the claim's outcome — converting an investigable incident into an unexplained loss.

The takeaway: make safe, preserve as found, then notify every channel it concerns — the order is the discipline.

=== ASSESSMENT Q11 | tests lesson 11.4 ===
QUESTION:
A warehouse team in Tamale strips an import container of packaged sanitary fittings and counts 104 cartons against 100 on the packing list and the commercial invoice. A colleague proposes to book the four extra cartons into stock at once as a windfall, so that the record balances today, arguing that no one has lost anything and the matter needs no further work.

Which statement best describes the correct handling of this finding?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The surplus is a discrepancy like any other: it is traced through the records — the invoice, the packing list, the purchase order, the supplier's dispatch papers — and regularised before any stock figure moves, because undeclared extra goods create record and customs discrepancies that must be declared and corrected, not quietly absorbed.
2. The colleague is right: an over shipment is a gain with no injured party, so increasing the stock record by four closes the matter, and discrepancies matter only where goods are missing.
3. The container should be resealed at once and returned to the carrier unopened, since any surplus is necessarily a carrier's mis-delivery and must go back before it is counted.
4. The surplus should be left to surface in the annual stock count's variances at year end, since customs requirements apply only to shortages, never to extra goods.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

A discrepancy is any disagreement between what the records say and what the goods show, and the lesson is explicit that the definition runs both ways: an over shipment is not a windfall but a shipment containing more goods than the records specify, creating record and customs discrepancies that must be declared and regularised, not quietly absorbed. The reconciliation reflex — records before adjustments — treats the surplus as a question the documents must answer: checked across the invoice, the packing list, the purchase order, and the supplier's dispatch papers, and traced before any stock figure moves. Only on the strength of that investigation does the record change — perhaps an invoice correction follows, perhaps cartons belonging to another order are found mixed into the load — and the incident then closes, as every incident should, with a root cause and corrective and preventive action on the process that let the count disagree.

Answer 2 books the surplus as gain: it treats discrepancies as losses only, which the over-shipment teaching refutes, and it hides an unexplained variance inside a stock figure. Answer 3 destroys the investigation before it begins, returning the goods uncounted and unexplained. Answer 4 postpones the question into the annual count's tolerances and rests on a falsehood — customs requirements do not apply only to shortages.

The takeaway: investigate before you adjust, in either direction; a record that balances today by absorbing a question has stored the question, not answered it.
