=== ASSESSMENT Q1 | tests lesson 9.1 ===
QUESTION:
A logistics officer at an importing company has three matters to resolve at a port during one week. First, her company's vessel is waiting at the anchorage and needs to know when and where it will be berthed. Second, a discharged container has been placed in the wrong stack inside the container terminal's yard and cannot be found for collection. Third, at a break-bulk berth, bags of cocoa are being loaded and stowed aboard a vessel slowly by workers specially engaged for that cargo work.

Which option correctly attributes each matter to the responsible party?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Berth assignment rests with the port authority, the misplaced yard container with the terminal operator, and the slow loading and stowing with the stevedores engaged for the vessel's cargo work.
2. Berth assignment rests with the terminal operator, the misplaced yard container with the customs authority, and the slow loading and stowing with the shipping line's agent.
3. All three matters rest with the port authority, because it manages the whole port estate and therefore performs every cargo operation inside it.
4. Berth assignment rests with the stevedores, the misplaced yard container with the shipping line's agent, and the slow loading and stowing with the port authority.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

The lesson separates three roles that beginners merge, and this question applies the separation to operational situations rather than definitions. The port authority administers, regulates and manages the port facilities within its mandate; among its stated responsibilities are managing navigation and vessel movements and controlling where vessels berth, so the question of when and where the waiting vessel berths belongs to it. The terminal operator is the company responsible for managing cargo-handling and related activities at its designated terminal, which includes running the yard, planning the stacks and keeping the records that say where every discharged box stands; a container misplaced in those stacks is the operator's matter. Stevedoring is the work of loading, unloading, stowing and handling cargo on or around vessels, and in break-bulk trades specialist stevedoring companies are commonly engaged for a loading or discharge, so slow cocoa stowing belongs to the stevedores.

Answer 3 is the attractive misconception: under the concession model the authority acts as landlord and regulator while commercial companies run the cargo work, so managing the estate is not performing every operation. Answer 2 assigns berthing and yard custody to parties that control neither; customs controls legality, not berth allocation or yard stacks. Answer 4 scrambles all three roles.

The takeaway: knowing who is responsible for each activity tells a logistics professional whom a delay belongs to.

=== ASSESSMENT Q2 | tests lesson 9.1 ===
QUESTION:
At a container terminal, a backlog of unreleased customs declarations builds up over several days. The physical operations keep working: cranes lift, tractors shuttle, gates open across shifts. But containers discharged days ago stand in the yard because their paperwork has not released them, and trucks are arriving to collect boxes that are not yet available.

Which statement best describes how this backlog will show up across the rest of the port?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. It will remain confined to the paperwork, because documents and cargo belong to separate worlds, so the berths, cranes and gates continue entirely unaffected.
2. The unreleased boxes occupy yard positions and dwell time grows, the yard fills faster than trucks empty it, re-handling multiplies, quay cranes begin waiting for yard space to clear, and vessel turnaround stretches, because a delay at one node of the port's 24-hour chain propagates to the others.
3. It will slow only the vessels that brought the unreleased containers, and only while those vessels remain alongside; later arrivals are unaffected.
4. It will force the port authority to suspend gate operations completely until every declaration is released, because a port cannot run with unreleased cargo inside it.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The lesson's important note teaches that a port is a continuous 24-hour chain in which a delay at one node propagates to all the others, and its Rotterdam congestion example shows the exact mechanism this question describes. Documentation delays are port delays in the fullest sense: an unreleased container cannot leave, so it occupies scarce yard space, ties up the carrier's equipment and runs down its free time. As unreleased boxes accumulate, the yard fills faster than trucks empty it, planners run out of good positions, boxes must be shifted to reach buried ones, and the re-handling consumes crane and tractor time without earning a productive move. Eventually the quay cranes themselves wait for yard space, vessel work stretches, turnaround grows, and the backlog rolls forward into the next day's plan. Congestion is a system condition, not a single failure, and nothing in the story is broken.

Answer 1 is precisely the misconception the note dismantles: the belief that the berth, the yard, the release and the gate belong to separate worlds. Answer 3 treats the propagation as ending with one vessel call, when the pressure in fact rolls forward to later arrivals. Answer 4 invents a suspension; operations continue, but they run slower and partly blind.

The takeaway: when something slips anywhere in a port, ask where the delay will surface next, and remember that the paperwork must travel at least as fast as the cargo.

=== ASSESSMENT Q3 | tests lesson 9.1 ===
QUESTION:
One large container vessel makes a single call at a terminal. During the call it removes its import containers onto the quay, lifts export containers aboard into their planned positions, and passes a number of transhipment containers across the terminal from its own hold to another vessel that will carry them onward.

Which option correctly labels the three movements?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Unloading removes the import containers from the vessel onto the quay and hands them into the port's custody, loading places the export containers on board secured for the voyage, and the transhipment containers move from one vessel to another while remaining inside the port estate.
2. Loading removes the import containers onto the quay, unloading places the export containers aboard, and the transhipment containers must be trucked out through the gate and re-enter before they may join the second vessel.
3. Loading, unloading and transhipment are three names for the same operation, because every movement performed at a berth is simply cargo handling.
4. Unloading transfers the import containers directly into the vessel's custody, loading hands the export containers into the port's custody, and transhipment describes the road leg between the two vessels.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

The lesson distinguishes the three words by the direction and effect of the movement. Unloading, also called discharge, removes cargo from the vessel onto the quay; the discharged cargo becomes the terminal's responsibility, and for import containers this is the step that starts the release clock. Loading places cargo on board a vessel, together with stowing and securing it in its planned position so that it survives the voyage; export cargo is loaded, and loading hands it into the vessel's custody. Cargo transfer moves cargo between transport units or vessels while it remains within the port, and transhipment is its container case: the box moves from one vessel to another to reach its final destination and never leaves the port estate between the two ships. A large vessel often performs loading and unloading in the same call, as this one does.

Answer 2 gets the labels backwards and invents a gate loop for transhipment boxes that would defeat the purpose of the transfer. Answer 3 merges the terms; the direction matters because each movement has its own planning, documentation and risks, and confusing them becomes a confusion about who is waiting for whom. Answer 4 reverses the custody handover for both directions.

The takeaway: the direction of a movement decides its name, its documentation, its custody, and its risks.

=== ASSESSMENT Q4 | tests lesson 9.2 ===
QUESTION:
A training group visits one inland facility far from the coast. On one side, full sealed containers stand stacked in blocks awaiting collection or onward carriage, and nothing is opened there. In a shed, loose less-than-container-load cargo is being combined into containers for export and separated from a shared container for delivery. A customs post on site examines and releases containers where the authority has authorised it. In a corner, empty boxes are stored, inspected and repaired for the shipping lines.

Which statement best identifies the facility?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. It is an inland container depot: an inland facility used for handling and storing containers and, where authorised, providing customs-related services, containing both a container-yard role for the sealed boxes and container-freight-station services for the loose cargo, with empty-container handling alongside.
2. It is a container freight station, because cargo is handled unpacked there, and the stacked sealed boxes are only a waiting area attached to the station.
3. It is an empty container depot that has added cargo storage, because the management of empty boxes is the facility's defining purpose.
4. It is a second container terminal, so a container passing its gate counts as gate-out from the seaport's own custody records.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

The lesson's reliable test asks three questions of a facility: what work happens there, where does it stand, and what is it authorised to do? The stacked full sealed boxes are the container-yard role: received, stacked, tracked and handed over intact, nothing opened. The loose cargo being combined and separated is the container freight station's work, the one container facility where cargo is regularly handled unpacked. The defining frame, however, is location plus authority: the facility stands inland and provides customs services where authorised, which is exactly the definition of an inland container depot. The lesson is explicit that an ICD is a facility in its own right and can contain both a CY and a CFS, and that depots inland commonly perform the empty-container role as well. Large facilities combine these roles under one management.

Answer 2 lets one function swallow the facility: the CFS is defined by its consolidation and deconsolidation work, but this site as a whole is defined by its inland location and its customs authorisation. Answer 3 makes the same mistake with the empties, which are one function among several. Answer 4 invents an equivalence with the coastal terminal; the depot's gate-in and gate-out are its own custody records, made at the facility.

The takeaway: sealed full boxes in stacks mean a CY, loose cargo being combined means a CFS, inland location with customs authorisation means an ICD, and the professional asks what happens at a facility rather than trusting its sign.

=== ASSESSMENT Q5 | tests lesson 9.2 ===
QUESTION:
An importer whose warehouse stands only a few kilometres from the seaport expects an urgent container of promotional stock that must reach shops within days. A colleague recommends clearing it at an inland container depot several hundred kilometres away, arguing that inland clearance always avoids port storage charges and is therefore the cheaper routing for every trader.

Which statement best evaluates the recommendation?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The colleague is right: inland clearance removes the port from the journey, so the depot routing must be cheaper and faster for every trader who uses it.
2. The recommendation inverts the trade-off: the depot routing adds an extra handling leg with its own time, cost and risk, so a trader whose premises stand near the port and whose cargo is wanted urgently may rationally prefer port-centred clearance, and the depot trade pays only when its gains in space and proximity outweigh the extra leg.
3. The choice makes no difference, because a container cleared at either place moves under identical arrangements at identical cost and speed.
4. The importer should route through the depot because goods cleared inland are exempt from the customs requirements that coastal cargo must satisfy.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The lesson's important note presents inland clearance as a trade, never an automatic answer. The cost side is concrete: the ICD routing adds a handling leg, because the box is discharged at the port, carried inland under customs control, lifted and stacked at the depot, and handled again if it is stripped there, and each lift and leg takes time, costs money and carries a small risk of damage. The gain side is equally concrete: space, because the port yard is relieved of a dwelling box; proximity, because the trader clears and collects near its premises; and function, because empties, stuffing, stripping and staging become available inland. For a trader far from the coast, or for transit cargo, or for a crowded port, the trade pays clearly. For this importer, premises a few kilometres from the port and stock needed in days, the extra leg works directly against urgency, and port-centred clearance is the rational reading.

Answer 1 repeats the always-mistake the note warns against, judged from one side of the journey. Answer 3 is false: the routings differ measurably in handling, time and port space. Answer 4 invents an exemption; inland clearance satisfies the same customs requirements, performed at the authorised depot.

The takeaway: judge the whole door-to-door movement in time and money, and choose the routing whose gains outweigh its extra leg.

=== ASSESSMENT Q6 | tests lesson 9.2 ===
QUESTION:
An urgently needed import container lies at the bottom of a stack in a terminal yard, beneath boxes that are not needed for days. A supervisor argues that the delay will be trivial, because the yard is only half full by area, so the terminal plainly has plenty of spare capacity and the dig-out will cost nothing that matters.

Which statement best evaluates the supervisor's reasoning?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The reasoning is sound, because a yard's capacity is its area, and a half-empty yard can never generate delay.
2. The reasoning fails twice: retrieval is not free, because boxes stand in tiers and digging out the wanted box delays the boxes dug aside as well; and a yard's capacity is the number of well-planned slots it holds and the speed at which cranes can serve them, which is why a half-empty yard can still congest.
3. The reasoning fails only because the terminal's truck drivers should re-stack the surrounding boxes themselves while they wait, which needs no planning or record.
4. The reasoning fails because import containers may never be stacked in tiers; every box must stand on the ground until it is collected.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The lesson's important note dismantles the car-park picture of the yard, and the supervisor's argument is that picture speaking. Containers stack in tiers, so the box wanted now may sit beneath boxes wanted next week; fetching it means digging, and digging means delaying the boxes dug aside, consuming crane time and adding a small risk of damage with every extra lift. The yard planner's whole craft is to minimise such re-handling, so that each box is lifted out of the stacks exactly once, in the right order, at the right moment. And capacity is not area: it is the number of well-planned slots the area holds and the speed at which cranes can serve them, which is why a yard that is half empty can still congest when the remaining boxes stand in the wrong places, and why congestion is usually a dwell problem before it is a space problem.

Answer 1 accepts the area-equals-capacity error outright. Answer 3 contradicts the terminal's core discipline: cranes lift only under the yard system's instruction, and an unrecorded, unplanned move breaks the plan and corrupts the live inventory that the terminal and customs rely on. Answer 4 invents a prohibition; stacking in tiers is exactly how yards achieve their density.

The takeaway: the yard is a precision instrument for storing boxes in motion, run by plan and record, where nothing is just parked and no retrieval is free.

=== ASSESSMENT Q7 | tests lesson 9.3 ===
QUESTION:
A transporter's truck arrives at a container terminal in its booked interchange slot. The driver holds a delivery order naming his company, the shipping line's release is confirmed in the terminal's system, and the terminal's own charge position is settled. But customs selected the consignment for physical examination, and no customs release has been granted.

Which option best explains what will happen?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The terminal releases the container, because a delivery order and the line's release together are sufficient authority for a container to pass the gate.
2. The terminal refuses gate-out, because shipping line release, customs release and the terminal's own requirements are separate authorisations and all must be in place; the examination is a customs hold that only customs can lift, so the fastest route is to satisfy the condition it names.
3. The gate staff contact the examination officers, have the examination waived on the strength of the delivery order, and release the container as a courtesy.
4. The terminal releases the container but records a note that customs release is pending, because a commercial document issued by the line outranks a customs hold.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The lesson's organising idea is that a container moves only when every key turns: shipping line release, customs release and terminal release are separate authorisations, from three different organisations, against three different sets of requirements, and no container leaves on two keys out of three. Here the commercial key is complete, the delivery order is in order, and the terminal's own requirements are settled, but the legal key is missing: goods may not enter a country's commerce until declared, assessed, paid and any required control completed. A customs hold is a status, not a place, a flag in the system that stops the cargo moving until its named condition is satisfied, and only the authority that set it can lift it. No delivery order, truck or goodwill overrides it; the examination must conclude before release, so the practical course is to present the cargo and complete the control quickly.

Answer 1 is the attractive error, treating two keys as the whole set, and it fails exactly as the gate would fail it. Answer 3 describes a completion that cannot happen at the window: gate staff read what their systems show. Answer 4 invents commercial supremacy over the state's control.

The takeaway: track the three keys as three separate checklists from the day the vessel sails, and never assume one authorisation implies the others.

=== ASSESSMENT Q8 | tests lesson 9.3 ===
QUESTION:
An importer awaits a container of machinery that moved on a full set of original bills of lading. Before the vessel sailed, the exporter surrendered the complete set at origin, and the carrier authorised electronic release at destination, a telex release. A colleague insists that nothing can happen until the paper originals arrive from overseas by courier.

Which statement best describes the position?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The colleague is right, because every sea shipment requires physical surrender of an original bill of lading at destination before any release can be authorised.
2. Under a telex release the carrier authorises electronic release at destination without presentation of an original, because bill-of-lading control was preserved when the shipper surrendered the full set at origin; the line's other release conditions still apply, and customs clearance remains a separate key.
3. A telex release takes effect only once the originals reach destination, because electronic messages merely reserve the release until paper arrives.
4. A telex release discharges the consignee's customs obligations as well, because the electronic authorisation covers both the commercial and the legal keys.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The lesson teaches that the line releases only against an established release basis, and telex release, also called express release, is one of the three bases. The shipper surrenders the full set of originals at origin, and the carrier authorises electronic release at destination, so the cargo moves with waybill-like speed while bill-of-lading control is preserved up to that surrender. The colleague's error is treating physical surrender at destination as the only possible basis; here the release basis already exists, without any paper travelling. What the telex release does not do is finish the whole chain: the line's charges due at destination remain payable before the agent completes the commercial release, and customs release, the legal key, is a separate authorisation with its own requirements, whether the shipment moves on originals, a telex release or a sea waybill.

Answer 1 states the classic basis as the only basis, which is the misconception the telex release exists to answer. Answer 3 inverts the mechanism: the electronic authorisation is the release, not a reservation of it. Answer 4 conflates the keys, the exact error the lesson's three-key discipline forbids; no commercial message, electronic or paper, satisfies the legal key.

The takeaway: know the release basis early, because it decides who must do what before the delivery order can exist, and remember that the other keys still stand.

=== ASSESSMENT Q9 | tests lesson 9.3 ===
QUESTION:
A transporter delivers an import container to an importer's warehouse. Two days later, unpacking reveals water-stained cartons. The equipment interchange receipt signed at the terminal's gate-out recorded no exceptions: the joint inspection had found the container clean, with the seal matching the paperwork. The importer now claims the water damage against the transporter.

Which statement best describes what the signed record means?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The claim must be directed to the shipping line, because the equipment interchange receipt is the document that records the ocean voyage.
2. The clean receipt records that the container left the terminal in apparent good condition, so responsibility for damage found afterwards points to the leg after gate-out; this is exactly why exceptions must be recorded at handover, because a signed record is very hard to unwind later.
3. The receipt has no bearing on the claim, because it is a receipt for the goods packed inside the container rather than for the container itself.
4. The record can be rewritten by agreement a week later, because an equipment interchange receipt remains a draft until every party's invoices are settled.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The equipment interchange receipt is the record that acknowledges the transfer of the container and fixes its condition at the moment responsibility changes hands. The driver checked the entry and signed it, so the documentary position is that the box passed gate-out clean, seal matching, with no exceptions noted. When water-stained cartons appear at the warehouse, the record therefore points away from the terminal and the ocean carriage and squarely at the road leg, the party responsible after gate-out. That is the condition-at-handover principle in both directions: the few minutes of joint inspection exist so that each party signs only a record that is true, because afterwards the signature, not anyone's memory, decides who answers for the damage. A clean signature on an unchecked box quietly accepts a claim that may not be yours.

Answer 1 misreads the document: the EIR records the container's condition at the interchange, not the voyage itself, and a claim arising after a clean handover runs against the road leg. Answer 3 mistakes its object; the EIR is about the equipment and its condition, not a receipt for the contents. Answer 4 invents a correction window that does not exist; the moment for the exception was at the inspection.

The takeaway: the signature is the moment responsibility changes hands, so record the truth first and sign second.

=== ASSESSMENT Q10 | tests lesson 9.4 ===
QUESTION:
An import container is discharged and made available for collection on a Monday. The booking's free-time allowance, given for illustration only, runs five days, and the container is still standing inside the terminal on day eight because its customs examination has not concluded. All figures in this question are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Which statement correctly identifies what has begun to accrue, and from whom?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Demurrage only, charged by the shipping line, because the terminal imposes no charge of its own, the space having already been paid for by the ocean freight.
2. Detention, charged by the shipping line, because the container has passed its last free day wherever it happens to stand.
3. Demurrage from the shipping line for the container remaining inside the terminal beyond free time, and storage from the terminal operator for the space it occupies, two charges from two different parties that can run over the same days.
4. Nothing accrues while the cause of the delay is a customs examination, because the terminal clock pauses for regulatory delays.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The question turns on the container's position. The box still stands inside the terminal past its last free day, which is the exact situation demurrage prices: the ocean carrier's charge, under the carrier arrangement, for an import container remaining within the terminal beyond the free-time allowance. But the line is not the only creditor. Storage is the terminal operator's own charge for cargo or containers occupying terminal land, separate from and additional to the carrier's demurrage, because two different organisations are billing for two different resources, the container's time and the space it occupies. That is why the two charges can run over the same days, and why delays at the port are usually more expensive than beginners expect.

Answer 1 gets the demurrage right but invents a free space; the freight pays for carriage, and time-based charges are a separate family from the freight bill, so occupied space is billed on its own. Answer 2 misplaces the clock: detention is the charge for a container retained outside the terminal beyond its return allowance, and it starts where demurrage ends, at gate-out; this box has not left. Answer 4 repeats the misconception that the clock cares about reasons; a consignment under examination dwells while the clock runs.

The takeaway: read the position before naming the charge, because a full box sitting in the port past expiry pays the line for its time and the terminal for its space at once.

=== ASSESSMENT Q11 | tests lesson 9.4 ===
QUESTION:
An importer has brought in dry containers for years on one line's service, routinely collecting them well beyond a week after discharge without ever paying a time-based charge, and it has always planned the in-terminal period and the empty-return period as two separate clocks. It now books a refrigerated container of frozen fish on a different line at a different terminal and plans the collection on the same assumptions. All details are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Which statement best evaluates the plan?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The plan is safe, because free-time allowances are fixed industry-wide customs that every line and terminal applies identically to every box.
2. The plan rests on assumptions the lesson dismantles: allowances, clock structures and rates are set by the tariff of each specific booking; specialised equipment such as reefers commonly carries a tighter allowance than dry boxes, and some lines run combined demurrage-and-detention arrangements rather than separate clocks, so the new booking confirmation and its tariff schedule must be read before collection is planned.
3. The plan is safe because the only factor that matters is the cargo inside the box, and the equipment type never affects the free-time allowance.
4. The plan is safe because refrigerated containers are exempt from time-based charges, since their cargo cannot wait and charging for them would be unreasonable.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The lesson's closing discipline is that free time is not an industry constant but a contractual term: the allowance, the event that starts each clock, the way days are counted and the rates are all set by the applicable tariff, which differs between shipping lines, terminals, trades and bookings. The lesson adds two factors this importer has ignored. Equipment type matters: refrigerated and specialised containers commonly carry shorter allowances than dry boxes, because plugged-in boxes occupy scarce power slots and their cargo cannot wait. Clock structure matters too: some lines run demurrage and detention as separate clocks, others publish combined arrangements with one clock from discharge to empty return, and the same line may differ between trades. Hence the four questions of any booking: which clocks apply, when each starts, how long each allowance runs, and how the rate behaves. The years of dry-container experience answer none of them for this reefer.

Answer 1 is the fixed-industry-allowance misconception the lesson exists to dismantle. Answer 3 denies a factor the lesson lists explicitly. Answer 4 invents an exemption; if anything, reefers carry tighter clocks, not none.

The takeaway: the previous shipment's figures are memory, not evidence; the current booking confirmation and its tariff schedule are the only safe answer.

=== ASSESSMENT Q12 | tests lesson 9.4 ===
QUESTION:
An importer's container has completed customs release but still stands inside the terminal, already four chargeable days into its stay. The booking's tariff, given for illustration only, prices the first three chargeable days at GHS 300 each and every day from the fourth onwards at GHS 600, with any part of a day billed as a full day. The container can gate out tomorrow, chargeable day five. The operations manager proposes waiting until day nine instead, reasoning that the daily rate is fixed, so the extra wait costs almost nothing. All figures are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Which statement best evaluates the proposal?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The proposal is sound, because under a progressive tariff every chargeable day is priced like the first, so postponement changes the total only slightly.
2. The proposal is costly: collecting tomorrow bills five chargeable days at about GHS 2,100, while waiting until day nine bills about GHS 4,500, because from the fourth day each day costs GHS 600 rather than GHS 300, and the terminal's storage and the standstill costs stack on top of the same days.
3. The proposal is safe, because demurrage stops accruing once customs release is complete, even if the container continues to stand in the yard.
4. The proposal is safe, because the invoice will not arrive until after collection, so the postponement costs the company nothing either way.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

A progressive tariff is one in which the daily rate increases over successive periods, so cost grows faster than time. The arithmetic: five chargeable days cost three days at GHS 300 plus two days at GHS 600, about GHS 2,100, while nine chargeable days cost three at GHS 300 plus six at GHS 600, about GHS 4,500. The four postponed days add roughly GHS 2,400 at the higher step, and because any part of a day bills as a full day, the day of collection is fully billable whenever the truck arrives. The tariff is not the whole bill either: the terminal's storage runs on the same days, and the standstill costs, the rebooked truck, the missed delivery slots, ride on the same delay. The lesson's economics are one-sided: the cheapest day to clear a container is the first one, and every day after it tends to cost more.

Answer 1 describes a flat tariff, which is exactly what a progressive one is not. Answer 3 confuses release with collection; released cargo still accrues until it gates out. Answer 4 mistakes silent accrual and after-the-fact invoicing for absence of cost; by the time the invoice lands, every chargeable day is history.

The takeaway: delay is itself a billable decision, and under a progressive tariff hesitation buys the most expensive days in the schedule.
