# DRAFT — Lesson 9.4: Demurrage, Detention, and Storage Charges

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Free time and the last free day: why ports run on a clock
BODY:
Lesson 7.4 dealt with the freight bill: the price of moving goods from one place to another. This lesson covers a different family of costs, ones that begin not when goods move but when they stop. They are the charges that arise when a container or its cargo stays too long at a port, terminal, or other facility, and they all rest on one idea: free time.

Free time is the period during which specified container or cargo usage or storage charges are not applied under the relevant tariff or agreement. For an imported container, free time is an allowance of days after the container has been discharged and made available for collection, during which it may wait without any time-based charge. The final day of that allowance is the last free day, and the moment the allowance ends is the free-time expiry. From expiry onwards, charges may begin to accrue day by day until the container is collected or returned.

Free time exists because a terminal must keep its inventory turning. Yard space is finite and valuable: vessels arrive on schedule, discharge their boxes, and the yard must empty to receive the next ship. The allowance balances two legitimate needs. The consignee needs a few working days to arrange release, clearance, and transport; the terminal needs its space back so that the whole port keeps flowing. The clock is therefore not a punishment. It is the discipline that keeps clearance and collection moving.

The length of time cargo remains at a port, terminal, or logistics facility before departing is called cargo dwell time. Free time is the tolerated slice of dwell time; beyond it, dwell becomes billable.

One caution before anything else: the number of free days, the event that starts the clock, and the way days are counted are all set by the applicable tariff, which differs between shipping lines, terminals, and trades. Nothing in this lesson should be read as a universal period. The figures used in the worked examples are illustrative for teaching purposes.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Demurrage: the charge for a container that stays inside the terminal
BODY:
Demurrage is a charge commonly applied when a shipping container remains within a terminal or other designated facility beyond the free-time allowance under the applicable carrier arrangement. In its most familiar form it is the import container's charge: the box has been discharged, it is standing in the terminal yard, and it has not been collected when the last free day passes. From that day the ocean carrier charges for each further day or part of a day the container remains inside.

It helps to see demurrage from the terminal's side of the fence. A full container occupying yard space is inventory the port cannot use: it crowds the stacks, slows handling, and delays the movement of everyone else's cargo. Demurrage is how that cost of occupied space is transferred to the party responsible for the delay, and the daily pressure of the charge is what pushes the consignee to complete release and collection. The charge is made by the ocean carrier under the carrier arrangement, while the underlying resource being consumed is terminal space. The memory rule is simple: demurrage is paying to keep a full box sitting in the port.

The clock stops when the container is collected and passes out through the terminal gate. Whatever happens after that belongs to a different family of charges.

A brief note on the other direction of trade: these families are not import-only. A container delivered to a port too early for its booked vessel stands in the export yard before loading, and export free time can run out before the box is loaded, so similar time-based charges can arise before departure. Practices on the export leg differ from the import leg, and between lines, so the same habit applies: check the applicable tariff rather than assuming.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Detention: the charge for holding the line's container outside the terminal
BODY:
Detention is a charge commonly applied when a shipping container is retained outside the terminal or designated facility beyond the permitted free-time allowance. In the standard import pattern, the ocean carrier charges detention when the container is out of the terminal, with the consignee or in transit, but has not been returned empty to the carrier's depot by the due date.

The reason detention exists is ownership of the equipment. The container belongs to the shipping line; it is lent to the consignee for the voyage and for unloading, not indefinitely. Once the cargo is out, the box must go back so the line can put it to work on another shipment. Every day the empty box sits idle in a warehouse yard is a day the line's equipment earns nothing, and the lines' fleet planning depends on boxes circulating. Container return is the delivery of an empty container to a location designated by the equipment provider, and the place where this happens is the empty container return depot: a designated facility where empty containers are returned after cargo unloading.

The detention clock therefore starts where demurrage ends, at the gate-out of the full container, and it stops only when the empty container is returned to the designated depot within the allowance. This is the beginner's blind spot. The charges do not stop because the cargo has left the port; the port is out of sight, but the line's clock keeps running wherever the box is. The memory rule: detention is paying to keep the line's box out of the port for too long.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Storage charges: the terminal's own charge for space
BODY:
A storage charge is a charge imposed for keeping cargo or containers at a terminal, warehouse, or other storage facility beyond or within specified chargeable periods. The wording is broader than demurrage or detention, and deliberately so. Storage is not tied to one trade or one document; it is the general price of occupying space, and it appears wherever goods or equipment sit in a facility under a tariff that charges for space.

At a container terminal the storage charge has a precise relationship to demurrage, and the distinction matters. Storage is the terminal operator's own charge for cargo or containers occupying terminal land, separate from, and additional to, the carrier's demurrage. Two different organisations are billing for two different things: the line bills the container's time under the carrier arrangement, and the terminal bills the space the cargo occupies. Because the two charges have different sources, they can run at the same time. A container that overstays inside the terminal can attract demurrage from the line and storage from the terminal for the same days, which is why delays at the port are usually more expensive than beginners expect.

Storage also reaches beyond containers. Loose cargo held in a transit shed, goods in a container freight station, or uncollected consignments in a warehouse can all attract storage charges under the applicable facility tariff. The memory rule that keeps the three families straight: demurrage is paying to keep a full box sitting in the port; detention is paying to keep the line's box out of the port too long; storage is what the terminal charges for the space regardless of who owns the box.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Combined arrangements and other variants of the three families
BODY:
The inside-the-terminal and outside-the-terminal pattern is the common teaching model, but lines publish variants, and one of the most important is the combined arrangement. Combined demurrage and detention is a charging arrangement in which a shared free-time allowance or tariff applies to specified periods of container use inside and outside the terminal. Instead of two clocks, one running in the yard and one running after gate-out, there is a single clock that starts when the container is discharged and runs until the empty box is returned to the depot. Combined arrangements are common in some trades and regions, while elsewhere the two clocks run separately, and the same line may apply different structures on different trades.

Two further variants are worth knowing. First, the import and export legs are treated differently: a line's import free time and its export free time are separate allowances, and each leg has its own conditions. Second, the equipment type matters. Refrigerated containers usually carry a shorter free-time allowance than dry boxes, because plugged-in boxes occupy scarce power slots and their cargo cannot wait; the general principle is that perishable and specialised equipment tends to come with tighter clocks. No day counts are offered here because none can be generalised.

The practical consequence of all this variation is a professional habit. Because definitions and practices vary between lines, ports, and regions, the safe question to ask of any shipment is: which clock applies to this box, on this booking, at this terminal? The booking confirmation and the tariff it points to answer that question. Assumptions carried from a previous shipment answer it wrongly often enough that checking is always worth the few minutes.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Chargeable periods, daily rates, and the tariff schedule
BODY:
To understand how lines and terminals actually apply their charges, three terms carry the weight. A tariff schedule is a published or agreed schedule specifying charges, rates, conditions, and applicable periods. The chargeable period is the period for which charges are calculated under the applicable tariff or contractual arrangement. The daily rate is a charge applied for each billable day or part of a day according to the applicable tariff.

Put together, the mechanics are straightforward. The tariff schedule states when the clock starts, how long free time lasts, how the days are counted, and what each billable day costs. For demurrage, the chargeable period typically runs from free-time expiry until the container is collected from the terminal. For detention, it typically runs from gate-out until the empty container is returned to the designated depot. For storage, it runs for as long as the cargo or container occupies the charged space beyond whatever allowance the facility's tariff grants. Under the part-of-a-day rule, a container collected at any time during a day can still make that whole day billable, which is one reason last-minute collections save less than learners expect.

Who applies all this? Both parties in the port: the shipping line sets and charges demurrage and detention under its tariff, and the terminal operator sets and charges storage under its own. The tariffs are contractual, published by each line and terminal, and they differ; there are no standard international amounts or periods.

The operational lesson follows directly. The applicable allowance and rates for a specific shipment appear on its booking confirmation and in the tariff it references. Reviewing those conditions before arranging collection, before the goods even arrive, is one of the cheapest forms of insurance in import logistics, and later blocks in this lesson build routines around it.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Progressive tariffs: when each extra day costs more than the one before
BODY:
Time-based port charges are usually daily, and they are often progressive. A progressive tariff is a charging structure in which the applicable daily rate increases over successive periods. Instead of one flat price for every late day, the tariff is built in steps: the first days beyond free time carry one rate, and each later band carries a higher one, so the longer the container stays, the more expensive each additional day becomes. The worked examples later in this lesson use round illustrative figures to show the shape of such a structure; the shape is the teaching point, not any particular number.

Progressive structures are not an arbitrary punishment. They reflect the operational reality of a terminal. Space pressure worsens the longer a box sits; a container that overruns free time by a day or two is an ordinary event, while a container stacked in the yard for weeks is a genuine obstruction to the terminal's inventory turn, and the stepped rate prices that obstruction accordingly. The escalation also creates the urgency that flat charges would not: with a progressive tariff, every day of hesitation makes the next day dearer.

Several factors shape what a shipper finally pays under these structures, and they are worth listing together.

1. The length of the free-time allowance granted on the booking, which sets where the chargeable period begins.

2. The equipment type, since refrigerated and specialised boxes commonly carry tighter allowances than dry containers.

3. The structure of the tariff itself, whether flat or progressive, and where its steps fall.

4. The duration of the delay, because under a progressive structure cost grows faster than time.

5. Whether the clocks are separate or combined, which changes how inside and outside days are counted.

6. Stacking with other charges, which the next block takes up.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: How the charges stack, when the invoice lands, and who pays
BODY:
Time-based charges rarely arrive alone. The first stacking happens inside the terminal: an overstaying import container can attract the line's demurrage and the terminal's storage simultaneously, because two parties are billing for two resources. The second stacking continues after gate-out: if the empty container then lingers at the consignee's premises, detention starts where the yard charges stopped. The third stacking is the standstill cost around the box, and it is easy to overlook. A delayed container means a rebooked truck, an idle vehicle and driver, a missed delivery slot, warehouse plans remade, and customers kept waiting. None of these appear on the port tariff, but they all flow from the same delay.

The second feature of these charges is timing. They accrue silently, day by day, and the invoice typically arrives after the fact, often after the container has already been collected and emptied. There is no meter ticking visibly at the gate. A beginner who has never seen a demurrage invoice can therefore run up weeks of charges without any signal until the bill lands, which is why tracking the last free day on a calendar, rather than waiting for a warning, is the professional habit.

Who pays? Under most trade terms, the importer or consignee. Incoterms 2020 shows why. Import clearance is the buyer's obligation under every rule except Delivered Duty Paid, and under the C rules such as CFR and CIF the seller pays the carriage to the named destination even though risk passes much earlier, at shipment. The buyer therefore owns everything that happens after arrival: clearance, collection, and the time-based charges that follow delay in either. Under other rules the allocation differs, so the working rule is to check the agreed trade term; but in the great majority of import transactions the party who arranges clearance and collection is the party who pays the clock.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Common situations that start the charges running
BODY:
Knowing the definitions is not enough; a learner must be able to look at an operational situation and see which clock is about to start. The recurring situations fall into a short list.

1. Missing or pending permits and certificates. If a required import permit has not been obtained before arrival, clearance cannot complete, and the container waits in the terminal while the paperwork catches up. Lesson 6.3 covered permits and supporting documents; this is where their absence becomes expensive.

2. Document discrepancies. An error in the bill of lading, an invoice that does not match the declaration, or an inconsistent description slows release. Lesson 6.4 covered verification and common discrepancies; every day spent correcting papers inside free time is cheap, and after expiry it is not.

3. Customs holds and examinations. A selected consignment under examination dwells until the process finishes. The terminal clock is indifferent to the reason.

4. The buyer not being ready. Funds not yet arranged, warehouse space unavailable, staffing gaps: the goods are released but nobody collects, and the yard clock runs on.

5. Transport not booked, or booked late. Release and collection are different events; a container released with no truck committed still accrues charges until it gates out.

6. Terminal congestion. Queues at the gate and pressure in the yard, which Lesson 9.1 examined, can stretch a collection planned for the last free day past it.

7. Slow unloading and late empty return. The container is out of the port but stripping drags on, and detention accrues at the buyer's own premises.

8. Disputes and refused cargo. A consignment under commercial dispute still occupies real space under a running tariff.

In each case the pattern is identical: some step in the chain from discharge to empty return stalls, and the applicable clock converts the stall into money.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Prevention and cost mitigation: the cheapest day is the first
BODY:
Cost mitigation means the measures taken to reduce or prevent unnecessary expenses arising from logistics operations. For time-based port charges, mitigation is not a negotiation technique applied after the invoice arrives; it is a routine built before the vessel sails. The recurring measures form a checklist.

1. Documents before arrival. Complete the commercial and regulatory documents while the goods are at sea, and verify them for consistency, as Lesson 6.4 taught. Permits and certificates should be in hand before the vessel berths, per Lesson 6.3.

2. Start the release chain early. Lesson 9.3 showed that collection rests on three keys: shipping line release, customs release, and terminal release. Begin satisfying each one as soon as the shipment allows, so that no single authorisation is still outstanding when free time is running out.

3. Check the last free day at booking, not at arrival. The allowance is stated in the booking confirmation and its tariff. Diary the date, count backwards, and treat the last free day as a hard deadline rather than a suggestion.

4. Book transport to collect promptly. Align the truck with the expected release date, with margin, so collection happens inside the allowance.

5. Communicate delays early. If clearance will slip, tell the line's agent before the charges start. Lesson 5.4 covered shipment tracking and customer communication; early warning is the difference between a managed delay and a surprise bill.

6. Review the tariff conditions before arranging collection. Confirm which clocks apply, when each starts, how long each free-time allowance runs, and how the rate steps. This is the habit from the tariff block earlier in the lesson.

7. Return the empty container promptly. Plan the unloading so the box goes back to the depot inside the detention allowance; the container is on loan, not on hold.

The economics of this checklist are one-sided. Time-based charges reward speed and punish drift, and no version of the invoice is cheaper than the days that never accrued. The cheapest day to clear a container is the first one.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A clean collection at Tema: the quiet success of zero charges
BODY:
A distributor of hardware and tools in Accra is importing one forty-foot container of hand tools through the port of Tema. All figures and periods in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: Check the clock at booking. When the booking is confirmed, the distributor's logistics officer reads the applicable conditions. The booking shows an illustrative free-time allowance of five days for collection, counted from the day the container is made available, and a separate illustrative allowance of seven days for returning the empty container after collection. She notes that these are this booking's terms, not universal rules. The expected last free day goes into the shared calendar as a hard deadline.

Step 2: Prepare documents before arrival. While the goods are at sea, the officer assembles the commercial invoice, packing list, and transport document details, checks them for consistency as Lesson 6.4 taught, and lodges the customs declaration early so processing can proceed ahead of arrival.

Step 3: Arrival and availability. The vessel discharges at Tema on Monday and the container is made available the same day. That Monday is day one of the illustrative five-day allowance, so the illustrative last free day is Friday.

Step 4: Complete the release chain. Using the routine from Lesson 9.3, the officer confirms shipping line release, customs release, and terminal release by Wednesday. The delivery order is issued and any applicable payments are settled. No time-based charge has accrued, because everything happened inside free time.

Step 5: Collect inside the allowance. The booked truck gates out the container on Thursday, day four. Demurrage: zero. Terminal storage: zero.

Step 6: Return the empty on time. The container is stripped at the Accra warehouse over the following days, and the empty box is returned to the line's designated depot the next Tuesday, which is within the illustrative seven-day return allowance. Detention: zero.

Step 7: The bill that never arrives. No time-based invoice is issued, because no chargeable day ever began.

What does this example demonstrate? The three charge families all existed on this shipment; they simply never woke up. The total time-based cost was zero not through luck but through sequence: documents ready before arrival, the release chain completed early, transport booked to collect inside the allowance, and the empty returned on time.

Key lesson: the cheapest shipment to land is the one whose paperwork and transport were ready before the ship was. Success here is invisible, because the evidence is an invoice that never arrives, which is exactly why disciplined importers track the clock even when nothing seems to be happening.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: The permit that came too late: watching demurrage and storage climb
BODY:
A distributor of medical consumables in Kumasi is importing one container of goods through Tema. The goods require an import permit from the responsible authority, as Lesson 6.3 covered. All figures and periods in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: The gap at arrival. The vessel discharges and the container is made available on Monday. Under the illustrative arrangement, free time runs seven days from availability, counting the day of availability as day one, so the illustrative last free day is the following Sunday. The distributor assumes clearance will be routine. It is not: the permit application was never submitted, and the company discovers this only when its clearing agent asks for the document on arrival week.

Step 2: The clock runs past the last free day. Applying for the permit after arrival takes longer than anyone hoped, and release is not completed until Friday of the following week. By then the free-time allowance expired on the Sunday, so the chargeable period begins on the Monday and runs while the container sits in the terminal.

Step 3: The illustrative arithmetic. The line's tariff is progressive: for illustration, the first three chargeable days are priced at GHS 300 per day and the next three at GHS 500 per day. The container gates out on Friday afternoon, the fifth chargeable day, and the part-day rule means Friday bills as a full day. Demurrage: three days at GHS 300, which is GHS 900, plus two days at GHS 500, which is GHS 1,000, giving GHS 1,900. The terminal separately bills storage for the same five days at an illustrative GHS 150 per day, adding GHS 750. The truck booked for the original week must be rebooked at an illustrative fee of GHS 400. The illustrative total is GHS 3,050. Had release completed by the last free day, the same movement would have cost nothing in time-based charges.

Step 4: Reading the result. The port did nothing wrong and the line's vessel was on time. Almost the entire cost came from one missing document and the days it consumed.

What does this example demonstrate? First, that demurrage and storage stack, because the line and the terminal bill different things for the same days. Second, that a progressive tariff makes late days dearer than early ones, so the fifth chargeable day cost more than the first. Third, that the surrounding costs, such as rebooking the truck, ride on the same delay.

Key lesson: the cost of the delay was mostly the clock, not the port. The permit itself cost no more because it was late; the charges came from where the container had to wait while the paperwork caught up.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Detention in Rotterdam: the charge that follows the container home
BODY:
An importer of kitchen equipment based in Rotterdam buys a container load from an overseas supplier on terms under which the importer handles import clearance and all destination costs. The container arrives at a Rotterdam terminal. All figures and periods in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: A disciplined start. The importer's team prepares the declaration before arrival and collects the container from the terminal on the third day, comfortably inside the illustrative five-day collection allowance. Demurrage: zero. So far the shipment is a model.

Step 2: The slow warehouse. The importer decides to strip the container at its own warehouse rather than unload promptly. The consignment is assorted equipment that must be sorted, checked, and put away, and the warehouse is busy with its own season. Stripping stretches from a planned few days into more than two weeks, and the empty container stands in the yard behind the building, out of sight and out of mind.

Step 3: The missed return. Under the illustrative tariff, the empty container must be returned to the line's designated empty container return depot within five days of gate-out. It is actually returned on the fourteenth day after gate-out. That makes nine chargeable detention days, and at an illustrative daily rate of US dollars 60 per day, the detention charge is nine times sixty, or US dollars 540.

Step 4: The invoice lands. Weeks after the goods are already on shelves and partly sold, an invoice arrives from the line for detention. The finance team queries it: the container left the port long ago, so what could the line possibly be charging for? The answer is the line's equipment, held out of circulation for nine billable days.

What does this example demonstrate? Detention accrues even though the container is no longer at the port. The clock that matters after gate-out is the container-return clock, and it runs wherever the box happens to be: at the buyer's warehouse, in transit, or parked in a yard. Gate-out is not the finish line; the finish line is the empty container back at the designated depot inside the allowance. Note also that this line's tariff counted collection and return as separate clocks; under a combined arrangement the arithmetic would have differed, which is why the applicable tariff must be read rather than assumed.

Key lesson: the beginner watches the port; the professional also watches the depot. A container is a borrowed box, and borrowed boxes are billed until they come home.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Time-based charges are a separate family from freight, duties, and taxes
BODY:
The most expensive misconception in this lesson is the assumption that settling one bill settles them all. It does not. The ocean freight covered in Lesson 7.4 pays for carriage; it does not buy the right to keep the container after arrival. Demurrage and detention are charged by the shipping line under its own tariff, storage is charged by the terminal under a different one, and duties and taxes are charged by customs under the customs regime. Four different charge families, four different bases, four separate invoices.

Because they are separate, they also behave separately. Paying the freight early does not pause the demurrage clock, and clearing customs does not stop storage on uncollected cargo. Shipping lines also commonly require outstanding time-based charges to be settled before they authorise release, so unpaid clock charges can themselves delay the container further and deepen the debt.

Finally, because every line and terminal publishes its own tariff, nothing transfers between shipments. The allowance that applied to last month's consignment, on one line at one terminal, tells you nothing reliable about this month's. The only figures worth budgeting are the ones on the current booking confirmation and its tariff schedule.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Definitions vary by line and port: ask which clock applies to your shipment
BODY:
The inside-the-terminal and outside-the-terminal pattern taught in this lesson is the common structure, and it is the right default picture. It is not a worldwide uniform. Practices genuinely vary between shipping lines, ports, and regions: some lines publish combined demurrage and detention arrangements with one shared clock, others run the clocks separately; the treatment of the import and export legs differs; specialised equipment carries its own conditions; and the labels used on invoices are not standardised everywhere. Two documents from different lines can use similar words for different things.

That variability is precisely why the lesson has repeated one habit. Before arranging collection, ask four questions of the booking and its tariff schedule. First, which clocks apply to this shipment: demurrage, detention, storage, or a combined arrangement? Second, when does each clock start? Third, how long does each free-time allowance run, and how are days counted? Fourth, how does the rate behave: flat or progressive, and where do the steps fall?

A learner who can answer those four questions from the document in hand is safe in any port. A learner who answers them from memory of a previous shipment is gambling with a progressive tariff. The definitions in this lesson are the map; the tariff schedule is the territory.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: The invoice lands after the fact: track the clock before it tracks you
BODY:
Time-based charges have an unpleasant property that beginners discover the hard way: they accrue silently and are invoiced after the fact. During the delay itself there is no alarm, no meter at the gate, no message counting down the last free day. The first many learners hear of demurrage is the invoice that arrives after the container has been collected and emptied, by which point every chargeable day is already history and none of it is negotiable away.

A second misconception follows from the first: the belief that delay costs belong to someone else, perhaps the seller who shipped the goods or the port that stored them. Under most trade terms the importer or consignee owns the destination leg and therefore owns these charges, and the standstill costs around the box, such as rebooked trucks and missed delivery slots, land on the same party. Waiting for someone else to move first is itself a billable decision.

The remedy is calendar discipline, which costs nothing. Put the last free day in a shared calendar the day the booking is confirmed. Count backwards and set the real deadlines: documents verified, release chain started, truck booked, empty return scheduled. Watch the detention allowance too, because it runs after the excitement of collection has passed.

The cheapest day to clear a container is the first one, and every day after it tends to cost more. The clock never surprises the trader who is watching it.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
An importer in Accra collects a container from the terminal at Tema one day before the last free day, so no charge arises while the box sits in the yard. The container is trucked to the importer's warehouse, where unloading is slow. The empty container is returned to the shipping line's designated depot twelve days after collection, although the booking allowed a much shorter period for its return. The shipping line then issues an invoice for the extra days.

Which statement best identifies the charge and the reason for it?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Demurrage, because the container was dealt with after the last free day had passed.
2. Storage, because the container occupied space at the importer's warehouse after unloading.
3. Detention, because the container was held outside the terminal beyond the free-time allowance for its return.
4. A freight surcharge, because the shipping line may adjust the freight price after delivery.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

Detention is the charge applied when a container is retained outside the terminal beyond the permitted free-time allowance for its return. The container left the terminal within free time, so the only clock still running was the container-return clock, which runs from gate-out until the empty box reaches the shipping line's designated depot. Holding the line's equipment for twelve days against a shorter allowance is exactly the situation detention prices.

Answer 1 fails because demurrage applies while the container remains inside the terminal beyond free time before collection; here the collection happened a day before the last free day, so demurrage never began. Answer 2 fails because storage is the terminal's own space charge for cargo or containers on terminal land; the container stood on the importer's private premises, not the terminal's. Answer 4 fails because freight covers carriage and was settled under the freight arrangements; time-based charges are a separate family.

The takeaway: after gate-out, the clock to watch is the container-return clock, and it runs wherever the box is.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
A freight forwarder is planning the collection of a container arriving next week through a shipping line and terminal the company has not used before. On the company's previous shipment, the free-time allowance and the daily charges followed a certain pattern, so the forwarder copies those figures into the budget without opening the new booking or its tariff.

Which statement best describes the weakness in this approach?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Free-time allowances and daily rates are set by the tariff of each line and terminal and can differ between shipments, so the new booking and tariff schedule must be checked before collection is arranged.
2. Free time is a fixed industry-wide allowance, so the figures from the previous shipment will always match the new one.
3. Time-based charges are included in the ocean freight, so no separate budget line or tariff check is needed.
4. The free-time clock only starts once customs has released the goods, so collection can safely be planned without reading the tariff.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

Free-time allowances, the rules for counting days, and the daily rates are purely contractual terms published in each line's and terminal's tariff schedule. They vary between carriers, terminals, trades, and equipment types, and the same company's consecutive shipments can carry different terms. The booking confirmation and the tariff it references are the only reliable source, which is why reviewing them before arranging collection is a core professional habit.

Answer 2 fails because no fixed industry-wide allowance exists; that is precisely the assumption this lesson exists to dismantle. Answer 3 fails because demurrage, detention, and storage are a separate charge family from the freight bill; paying freight does not cover them. Answer 4 fails because the terminal clock is tied to the container and its availability, not to the paperwork; goods awaiting clearance still accrue charges after free-time expiry, as the permit example in the lesson showed.

The takeaway: the previous shipment's figures are not evidence. The applicable tariff is.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
Three days before a vessel arrives, an importer learns that the permit its goods require is still being processed, so clearance may take longer than planned. The importer wants to limit avoidable charges.

Which option best describes the most sensible course of action?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Wait until customs examines the goods, then ask the terminal to waive storage on the grounds that the delay was regulatory.
2. Assume the shipping line grants every shipment a generous free-time allowance, and book the collection truck for the following month.
3. Expedite the permit now, confirm the free-time allowance and last free day on the booking, arrange collection transport in advance, and inform the line's agent about the possible delay early.
4. Plan to pay demurrage instead of expediting the permit, since daily rates stay flat no matter how long the container stays.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

This option assembles the lesson's cost-mitigation checklist in the right order: documents driven forward before arrival, the applicable allowance and last free day confirmed from the booking rather than assumed, transport arranged to collect promptly once release completes, and early communication with the line's agent so the delay is managed rather than discovered. Even if some charge ultimately accrues, each of these steps shortens the chargeable period and demonstrates a managed delay.
