# DRAFT — Lesson 9.3: Cargo Release, Delivery Orders, and Terminal Procedures

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: What cargo release is and why it matters
BODY:
Lesson 9.1, Port Operations and Cargo Handling, followed an import container from berth to gate and left it standing in the terminal yard at its third step, release processing. Lesson 9.2, Container Terminals and Inland Container Depots, went inside the facilities where that processing happens. This lesson answers the question they set up: the container has arrived legally and been discharged, counted, and stacked — what makes it finally leave on a truck?

Cargo release is the authorisation and process through which cargo becomes available for collection or delivery after applicable requirements have been satisfied. Release is where paperwork becomes permission: a crane can lift a container onto any chassis in minutes, but it must not let it pass the gate until release is complete.

Two companion terms travel with the idea. Cargo clearance is the completion of required procedures and formalities for goods to be released or moved under an applicable customs or regulatory process: clearance is the work, release is the permission that follows it. The consignee is the person or organisation designated to receive goods under a transport document; release exists for the consignee's benefit, since the goods become the consignee's to collect once the requirements are satisfied.

The importance is easy to see by counting what an unreleased container does while it waits. It holds the importer's goods away from the business that bought them: a shop cannot sell cartons still sealed in a box in the yard, nor can a factory run on parts that have not cleared the gate. It occupies terminal space the next vessel's cargo needs. It ties up the carrier's equipment. And it runs down the free-time clock — returned to in the closing note — that converts waiting into charges. Release is not a formality at the end of shipping; it is the step that turns a completed voyage into a usable consignment.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The cast of the release process
BODY:
Cargo release is a relay between parties who each control one lock on the same cargo. The major stakeholders are the following.

1. The shipping line and its agent. The container is the line's own equipment, lent into trade on the understanding that it returns empty in good time, and carriers commonly keep a right to withhold it until the charges owed them are settled — a form of lien kept light here. At the destination port the line works through its agent, the local office that issues documents, collects charges, and authorises release on the line's behalf.

2. The importer and its agents. The importer, usually the consignee named in the transport document, wants the goods. Importers rarely walk the process alone: a freight forwarder may coordinate transport and documentation, and in Ghana importers in practice work through licensed customs clearing agents, who lodge the declaration in ICUMS and arrange duty payment.

3. The customs authority. Customs controls legality. Goods may enter a country's commerce only through the import formalities met in Lesson 4.1, and customs administers them — in Ghana, the Customs Division of the Ghana Revenue Authority.

4. The terminal operator. The previous lesson showed the terminal as the facility that physically holds the container. It controls the gate, and its rule is closer to physics than negotiation: nothing leaves except through its systems, which release a container only when the records behind them are complete.

5. The transporter. The trucking company whose driver and chassis perform the cargo collection — the physical retrieval of goods from a port, terminal, warehouse, or other designated facility.

Each party holds one key, and the lesson's organising idea follows: a container moves only when every key turns — so professionals track all the keys at once, not only their own.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Three keys: shipping line release, customs release, and terminal release
BODY:
Because several parties hold keys, there are several releases, and they are not the same authorisation.

1. Shipping line release. The confirmation or authorisation from a shipping line or its agent that specified cargo may be delivered under the applicable transport arrangements. This is the commercial key: has the carriage been paid for as the transport terms require, and is the person asking identified in the transport document? The line grants it on the strength of the bill of lading position and settled freight and charges.

2. Customs release. Authorisation granted by customs permitting goods to proceed under the relevant customs procedure, subject to applicable requirements. This is the legal key: imported goods may not enter a country's commerce until declared, assessed, the duties and taxes paid, and any required control completed. A fully paid-for container is still unlawful to deliver if customs has not released it.

3. Terminal release. Authorisation by a terminal operator to release cargo or a container after the terminal's applicable requirements have been satisfied. This is the physical key: the terminal checks that the line's release and the customs release are in place, settles its own requirements, and only then flags the container as available. The gate obeys the flag.

The distinction matters because the three authorisations come from three different organisations, against three different sets of requirements, on three different timetables. A container can hold a shipping line release and still wait for customs; it can be customs-cleared and still held because a charge at the line's agent is unpaid. And the order is not fixed: shipping line release, customs release, and terminal release are separate authorisations whose order and requirements may vary depending on the country, the port, the terminal, the cargo type, and the applicable procedures. The professional habit: ask which keys are in place and which are missing — never assume one implies the others.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The bill of lading as the key to the box
BODY:
Before a line can release anything, it must know to whom. For sea cargo the answer lives in the transport document, usually the ocean bill of lading.

Lesson 6.2, Bill of Lading and Air Waybill, gave the ocean bill its three functions, and the third rules this lesson: the bill of lading is a document of title, so possession of the original controls the right to demand delivery of the goods. So the line does not hand over a container against a promise, a telephone call, or a photocopy; it releases only against an established release basis.

1. Surrender of the original bill of lading. The classic basis. Whoever holds the original presents and surrenders it to the line or its agent at destination, and the line authorises delivery against it. Because goods often arrive while the documents still travel through a banking chain, the importer frequently obtains the original from its own bank — the payment method agreed in the sale, such as the documentary collections met in Lesson 4.4, decides when the bank lets go. Worked Example 2 shows what happens while it waits.

2. Telex release, also called express release. The shipper surrenders the full set of originals at origin, and the carrier authorises electronic release at destination. The cargo moves with waybill-like speed while bill-of-lading control is preserved up to surrender at origin.

3. The sea waybill. The non-negotiable alternative: a receipt for the goods and evidence of the contract of carriage, but not a document of title. Cargo is released to the named consignee without paper being presented, which suits trusted, open-account trade.

Know the release basis early: it decides who must do what — surrender an original, arrange payment through a bank, or simply appear as the named consignee — before the next document in the chain, the delivery order, can exist.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The delivery order: the carrier's instruction to release
BODY:
The delivery order is the document by which the carrier's release becomes an instruction the terminal can act on.

A delivery order (DO) is a document or electronic instruction authorising a designated party to take delivery of specified cargo, subject to applicable conditions. The line or its agent issues it once the release basis is established and the charges due to the line are settled. A typical DO identifies the vessel and the bill of lading it relates to, describes the cargo — here, the container number and type — names the party authorised to take delivery, and states the conditions of release.

Two names deserve attention. The consignee is the party designated to receive the goods under the transport document. The notify party is the party the carrier is to notify on arrival — the consignee, the importer's agent, or another nominated contact. The DO is issued to the party entitled to collect, and the terminal honours it: it is the instruction the terminal's staff and systems check a truck against when it arrives.

The DO usually carries, or corresponds to, a release reference: a document number, code, or electronic identifier used to confirm or process cargo release authorisation. Increasingly the paper is thin or absent — the authorisation travels as an electronic message from the line's systems to the terminal's — but the principle is unchanged. Some instruction, headed by a reference, says: this container, to this party, under these conditions.

The delivery order therefore performs three functions: it converts the line's release into an operational instruction the terminal can execute; it identifies and limits who may collect — the designated party, no one else; and it gives the terminal a record to check, so the gate's decision is made against a document rather than a relationship. That last function returns as an important note later in this lesson.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The release chain: from surrender to gate authority
BODY:
This block assembles the pieces into the sequence a container follows from ship to gate authority. Details vary by country and port, so the order below is the common pattern, not a universal rule.

1. The release basis is established. The original bill of lading is surrendered to the line's agent, a telex release already sits in the line's systems, or the cargo moves under a sea waybill in the consignee's name. Nothing later in the chain can happen before this.

2. The line's account is settled. Freight due at destination under the transport terms, together with the line's local charges, is paid to the line or its agent. Lesson 7.4, Shipping Charges, Freight Rates, and Surcharges, covered how freight and surcharges are built; here they matter as a gate on release.

3. The line issues the delivery order and releases the container in the terminal's systems. The agent issues the DO to the entitled party and sends the release message the terminal records against the container number — from that moment, the line has no objection to the box leaving.

4. Customs release is obtained. This step usually runs in parallel with steps 2 and 3, and is treated in full later in this lesson.

5. The terminal readies the container. With all three keys and its own charge position settled, the terminal's system shows the container available and the date collection must happen by — the free-time arithmetic belongs to Lesson 9.4, Demurrage, Detention, and Storage Charges.

6. The truck is arranged and booked in. The transporter is instructed and books an interchange slot at the gate, as the next block describes.

7. Gate-out against the DO and the release message. The driver presents the documents, the container is loaded, checked, and passes gate-out.

Where the chain sticks, it sticks on documents: a release basis missing while a bank holds the bill; charges unpaid at the line's agent; a declaration held up in customs; a permit still pending for goods that require one; a name on the delivery order that does not match the collecting party; a container flagged for examination. Not one of these is a failure of cranes, trucks, or yard space: releases delay cargo; the machines merely wait.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Documents and payments for cargo collection
BODY:
Collecting cargo is a documentary transaction before it is a physical one. The documents and payments below recur, with local variation, at most ports.

Documents commonly required:

1. The delivery order, or its electronic equivalent, authorising the named party to take delivery.

2. Evidence of customs clearance: the declaration reference, the assessment, and proof that duties and taxes were paid. In Ghana the sequence runs through ICUMS: the clearing agent lodges the declaration, the system generates the tax bill, and the importer pays at a participating bank against that bill. Where goods require a regulatory permit — food and medicines under the Food and Drugs Authority, standards conformity under the Ghana Standards Authority — the permit sits with the declaration before release.

3. The gate pass. A document or electronic authorisation permitting specified cargo, containers, vehicles, or persons to pass through a controlled facility gate; the terminal issues it once its requirements are met, and the driver carries it through.

4. Identification and vehicle details. The driver's identification and the truck and chassis numbers, which must match the booking and the gate's records.

Payments commonly due before collection:

1. Import duties and taxes, per the customs assessment.

2. Ocean freight where the transport terms make it payable at destination, plus the line's local charges such as documentation fees.

3. The terminal handling charge (THC), a charge associated with specified cargo or container handling activities at a terminal. The destination THC covers the container handling at the terminal of discharge; it is distinct from the ocean freight, which pays for the voyage.

4. Terminal or storage charges where any have been incurred — a subject owned by Lesson 9.4.

No amounts are taught here, and none can be: every figure is a tariff or assessment differing by line, terminal, country, and consignment. Carry away the shape of the list: each document answers one of the three keys, each payment unlocks one of the parties — and an incomplete set means a guaranteed second trip.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Customs release: the legal key, and cargo holds
BODY:
Of the three keys, customs deserves its own treatment: it is the key that involves the state.

Goods may enter a country's commerce only through the import formalities met in Lesson 4.1, and customs authorities exist to administer them. Their responsibilities run wider than collecting money: Ghana's Customs Division collects revenue on imports and exports, facilitates legitimate trade, and protects the country's security at harbours, airports, land borders and parcel posts. Release is where all three meet: revenue is paid, trade is facilitated when release is prompt, security is protected because nothing moves unseen.

In Ghana the mechanics run as follows: the customs declaration is submitted in ICUMS — the Integrated Customs Management System, Ghana's single-window e-customs platform — by the clearing agent. ICUMS classifies, values and assesses the consignment, generates the tax bill, and the importer pays duties and taxes at a participating bank against that bill. Every consignment carries a Unique Consignment Reference (UCR) that lets every party track it end to end — this lesson's release-reference logic applied to the shipment's whole life.

No customs administration can inspect every consignment, so modern systems work by risk: low-risk declarations may be released without inspection, medium-risk ones checked on their documents, high-risk ones selected for physical examination. Where examination is required, the cargo is presented at the designated place, inspected against the declaration, and released when customs is satisfied.

Customs can also attach a cargo hold: a restriction preventing cargo release or movement until specified conditions or requirements have been satisfied — a query on the declaration, a pending permit, an unpaid assessment, a selection for examination. The word needs care: Lesson 9.1 met the ship's hold, the space inside a vessel where cargo stows; a customs hold is not a place but a status, a flag in the system that stops the cargo moving, and only the authority that set it can lift it. No delivery order, truck, or goodwill overrides a customs hold — the fastest route through is to satisfy the condition it names.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Arranging cargo collection and the interchange booking
BODY:
With the three keys secured, collection can be arranged — the transporter's side of the lesson.

Cargo collection — the physical retrieval of goods from a port, terminal, warehouse, or other designated facility — is organised by the consignee or its agent, who instructs a transporter. The instruction passes across the essentials: container number, terminal, delivery order and release reference details, delivery address — a Kumasi warehouse, a factory gate, a retailer's distribution centre — and any conditions, such as whether the load needs a particular trailer.

Terminals control truck arrivals through interchange bookings. A gate can absorb only so many trucks per hour, and Lesson 9.1 showed what happens when they arrive in a bunch: queues on the access road and an interchange slowed for everyone. Terminals therefore require trucks to book a slot — a date and time window — so yard equipment can be planned and arrivals spread through the day. Arriving without a booking, or hours outside the window, can mean the truck is turned away whatever its paperwork.

The driver arrives equipped. The standard kit is the booking confirmation, the delivery order or its release reference, the gate pass, the driver's identification, and the truck and chassis numbers that match the booking. The terminal releases against documents rather than acquaintances — a principle the closing notes develop — so it is the kit, not the driver's familiarity with the port, that opens the gate.

One planning habit belongs here: arrange the truck against the expected release, not against hope. A truck sent before the three keys are confirmed burns a day, wastes a booked slot, and must book again. The professional sequencing is confirm, then book, then drive.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: At the interchange: gate-out, condition checks, and the EIR
BODY:
The interchange is where the terminal's control becomes visible, and it follows a disciplined sequence.

1. Gate-in. The truck presents at the terminal gate in its booked slot. The clerk verifies the documents — booking, delivery order or release reference, gate pass, identification — and the system confirms the container is released and available. Any mismatch stops the process here, while the truck is still outside the stack.

2. Positioning and loading. The container is located in the yard and lifted onto the truck's chassis. From this moment it is leaving the terminal's custody — which is why what happens next matters.

3. The condition and seal check. Before the truck moves, clerk and driver inspect the container together: doors, panels, floor, roof, and the seal. The seal is the numbered lock applied when the container was closed, evidencing that the box has not been opened since it was stuffed; some terminals use electronic seals that record opening events, but the principle stands — the seal number on the paperwork must match the door. Where procedures require it, the truck or container also passes a weighbridge, for example where road weight limits must be respected.

4. The equipment interchange receipt. An equipment interchange receipt (EIR) is a document or electronic record used to acknowledge the transfer of a container or transport equipment and record relevant condition details. The clerk records the container's condition — clean, or with exceptions noted — together with the seal number; the driver checks that what is written is true, and signs.

5. Gate-out. The recorded departure of cargo, containers, or transport equipment from a terminal or depot. The gate system closes the terminal's record of the container, the truck leaves the port estate, and responsibility for the box travels with it.

The EIR is the quiet star of the sequence: the document that fixes the container's condition at the moment responsibility changed hands. Worked Example 3 follows a gate-out where the inspection finds something — and shows why a driver's honest signature is worth far more than the few minutes it costs.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Break-bulk release: the same logic without the container
BODY:
Everything so far has assumed a container. Break-bulk cargo — the packages, pallets, steel, and project pieces lifted piece by piece, as Lesson 9.1's cargo types showed — is released by the same documentary logic without the box.

The difference begins with equipment: a break-bulk consignment is not stuffed into line-owned equipment, so there is no container to release, no seal to check, and no EIR for a box. The cargo is discharged onto the quay or into a transit shed, under the port's custody.

But the three keys survive intact. The line's release still exists: the consignee's entitlement still flows from the bill of lading, surrendered or released under the same bases as a container, and the line or its agent still issues a delivery order per bill of lading. Customs release still exists: the goods are declared, assessed, and released exactly as containerised cargo would be. And the terminal's — here, the port's — release still exists: the terminal or warehouse authorises removal against its own documents once the line's DO and the customs release are evidenced, and the cargo passes out on a gate pass.

The checks at handover change shape rather than shrink. Instead of one sealed unit, tally clerk and receiver count and inspect pieces: the number of packages, their marks, their apparent condition. A short delivery or torn wrapping is noted on the receiving documents at handover, for the same reason a driver notes a dented door on an EIR — the record at handover decides what can be proved later.

Consolidated cargo is the same story in miniature: a shared container is unpacked at a container freight station, and each consignment is released against its own authorisations. The wrapping differs — box, shed, or freight station — but the lesson is one: release is a documentary chain, and the packaging merely changes where in the port the chain is checked.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A Kumasi importer's container released at Tema
BODY:
A trading company in Kumasi imports agricultural machine parts in a twenty-foot container from an overseas supplier. The container is discharged at Tema, one of Ghana's two seaports, which are managed by the Ghana Ports and Harbours Authority. All details and timings in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: Arrival and notice. The vessel berths at Tema and the container joins the terminal yard, as Lesson 9.1 described. The line's agent sends the arrival notice: the cargo has arrived, and this is what collection will require.

Step 2: Declaration and payment. The importer's licensed clearing agent lodges the customs declaration in ICUMS, Ghana's single-window e-customs platform. The system assesses the declaration and generates the tax bill, and the importer pays at a participating bank. The declaration falls in the low-risk channel: no examination, a documentary release.

Step 3: The release basis. The sale moved on documents through the banking channel; the importer has already settled with its bank, which released the original bill of lading. At the line's Tema office the agent surrenders the original bill, settles the remaining charges due to the line, including the destination terminal handling charge, and issues the delivery order naming the importer's nominated collecting party, with a release reference the terminal can check.

Step 4: The terminal readies the box. With the line's release message, the customs release, and the charge position all recorded, the terminal's system shows the container available and its last free day. The agent books an interchange slot with a transporter for the following morning.

Step 5: Gate-out. The driver arrives with booking, delivery order and release reference, gate pass, and identification. The clerk verifies each document against the system, the container is lifted onto the chassis, and driver and clerk walk the box: clean, seal number matching the paperwork, no exceptions on the interchange receipt. The driver signs, gate-out is recorded, and the truck sets off inland to Kumasi.

Where it went wrong: the arrival notice reached the agent two days after discharge, so the declaration lodged late and every later step queued behind it. Nothing physical failed — the documents lagged, and the container waited.

What does this example demonstrate?

Five organisations moved one box: the shipping line and its agent, customs through ICUMS, the terminal, and the transporter. The signatures trace the relay — the agent lodges and surrenders, the bank released the bill, the agent issues the delivery order, the driver signs the receipt — and the container moved only as fast as its slowest document.

Key lesson: start the paperwork when the vessel sails, not when it berths. Documents that travel with the cargo cost nothing; documents that lag cost days.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A release delayed by a bill of lading held at the bank
BODY:
A trading company in Mumbai imports machine tools from a manufacturer in Hamburg on documents against payment terms — D/P, the documentary collection taught in Lesson 4.4, under which the buyer's bank releases the shipping documents only against payment. All details and timings in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: The goods sail; the documents crawl. The container leaves Hamburg while the exporter's bank sends the document set — including the original bill of lading, the document of title controlling the right to delivery — to the importer's bank in Mumbai. Container and papers are now on two different timetables.

Step 2: The box arrives first. The container is discharged and stacked in the yard, and the carrier's free time begins — but collection is impossible.

Step 3: The bank holds the key. Under D/P the importer must pay before the bank releases the documents, and therefore before it can collect the goods. Funds are still being arranged; until they are, the bill of lading sits at the bank, and no surrender to the shipping line is possible.

Step 4: The line's hands are tied. Without the surrendered bill — there is no telex release; the shipment moved on a full set of originals — the line has no release basis. Its agent at the destination cannot issue a delivery order: not for money, not for urgency, not for a familiar face at the counter. Parallel paperwork, such as preparing the customs declaration, can proceed; the commercial release cannot complete.

Step 5: Payment, then the chain restarts. On day eight the importer pays the bank and receives the documents. The bill of lading is surrendered to the line's agent, the line's charges are settled, the delivery order is issued with its release reference, and the terminal shows the container released. Collection and gate-out follow within days.

Step 6: The bill for the wait. The container left the terminal well after its free time expired, so time-based charges had begun to accrue — which charges, and how they are calculated, Lesson 9.4 owns. The machine tools also reached the workshop days later than planned.

What does this example demonstrate?

The container was never blocked by physics: cranes worked, the yard had space, trucks were available. The blocker was documentary timing — a payment scheduled without reference to the vessel's arrival. D/P gives the exporter security precisely because the goods cannot be collected before payment, and it gives the importer a matching duty: plan that payment before the ship berths.

Key lesson: document timing is physical. A day lost in a bank is a day gained by the container in the yard, and the yard charges by the day.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Damage noted at the Tema gate-out: the interchange record decides
BODY:
A transporter's truck collects a forty-foot container at the Tema container terminal for an importer in Accra. All details in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: Documents verified. The driver presents the booking, the delivery order's release reference, the gate pass, and identification at the gate in the booked slot. The clerk checks each against the terminal's system, and the container shows as released and available.

Step 2: Loading. The box is located in the stack and lifted onto the truck's chassis.

Step 3: The joint inspection. Driver and clerk walk the container together, as the gate-out procedure requires. The doors open freely and the seal matches the paperwork — but the left side panel shows a deep dent, and the door frame at the top hinge is deformed, leaving a gap that could admit water.

Step 4: The record before the signature. The clerk records the exception on the equipment interchange receipt: side panel dented, door frame deformed at the top hinge, seal number as stated. The driver watches the entry being written, checks the seal number, and signs a record that tells the truth.

Step 5: The temptation declined. The clerk's shift is busy, and a quick signature on a clean receipt would move the truck out faster. The driver politely declines the shortcut. A clean-signed receipt would say the container left the terminal undamaged — and if water-stained cartons appeared at the warehouse a day later, it would point the finger squarely at the road leg, the driver's leg of the journey.

Step 6: What the record does afterwards. With the damage noted at gate-out, the trail runs the other way: the record shows the container handed over in that condition before the truck moved, so the questions point back towards the ocean carriage and the parties who held the box before the terminal, and the importer's claim starts from documented fact rather than two companies with opposite memories.

What does this example demonstrate?

The equipment interchange receipt is not paperwork about a container; it is the allocation of responsibility for one. The inspection takes minutes, and the signature decides who answers for damage found later. A driver who signs a clean receipt for a dented box has quietly accepted a claim that is not his, and a clerk who waves the inspection through has moved the terminal's problems onto the road.

Key lesson: sign the record only when the record is true. At an interchange, the signature is the moment responsibility changes hands, and the written exception is the cheapest insurance either party will ever hold.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: No release without all three keys
BODY:
No container leaves a terminal on two keys out of three.

The gate's question is not whether the truck came, whether the driver is known, or whether the cargo looks legitimate. It is whether all three authorisations are recorded: the shipping line's release, the customs release, and the terminal's own requirements met. A truck that arrives with customs cleared and a delivery order in hand, but with the line's release missing because a charge at the agent is unpaid, goes home empty. So does a truck whose delivery order is perfect but whose declaration sits under a customs hold. The gate staff cannot, and must not, complete a missing authorisation at the window; they can only read what their systems show.

The practical discipline follows. Track the three keys as three separate checklists from the day the vessel sails, and send the truck only when all three are confirmed. Dispatching collection before the keys are complete converts one planned trip into two, one booked slot into a wasted booking, one calm gate-out into a queue of rework. Completing the necessary requirements before collection is not over-caution; it is the procedure working as designed.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Terminals release against documents, not acquaintances
BODY:
A delivery order authorises a designated party. The terminal honours the DO by checking that the party presenting for collection is the party the document names — and terminals release against documents, not acquaintances.

The scenario writes itself. The DO names the consignee; a truck arrives from a different company, and the driver knows the gate clerk from years of collections, so everyone agrees the cargo is obviously meant for him. The terminal still refuses, and it is right to. The DO is the cargo owner's protection against exactly this: valuable cargo is released only to the party the carrier authorised, and a gate that bent its rule for a familiar face would be a gate anyone could talk past. The clerk has no way to verify human relationships — only documents.

The correction is documentary too. If the collecting party must change, the fix is made where the authorisation was issued: the consignee or its agent returns to the line, which issues its instruction afresh to the right party. Importers and agents should therefore make sure the party named on the delivery order — consignee, forwarder, or nominated transporter — is the party that will actually collect, before the document is issued rather than after the truck is at the window.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Free time is running while the paperwork catches up
BODY:
The release chain runs against a clock that started before anyone opened a file.

Free time is the allowance before time-based charges begin, and it commonly starts when the container is discharged and made available for collection — not when the paperwork is finished, and not when the truck arrives. Every day the documents lag is a day the container sits in the terminal with the clock running. Slow paperwork does not pause free time; it burns it.

When free time ends and the container still stands in the yard, the waiting becomes invoiced: the ocean carrier charges demurrage on the box inside the terminal, and the terminal charges its own storage for the space. This lesson teaches the concept and the discipline only — Lesson 9.4, Demurrage, Detention, and Storage Charges, owns the charges, their differences, and their calculations.

The paperwork has the whole voyage to run: declarations can be lodged in advance, banks organised while the ship is at sea, charges settled before the arrival notice lands. An importer who starts the release chain at discharge has chosen to pay for the delay; one who started at loading usually gates out within free time. Free time rewards the behaviour this lesson has taught throughout — documents must travel at least as fast as cargo.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A transporter's truck arrives at a container terminal in its booked interchange slot. Customs release is recorded in the terminal's system, and the driver holds a delivery order for the container. However, the shipping line's release has not yet reached the terminal, because a destination charge at the line's agent remains unpaid.

Which option best explains what will happen?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The terminal releases the container, because customs release and the delivery order together are sufficient authority for gate-out.
2. The terminal refuses gate-out, because shipping line release, customs release, and the terminal's requirements are separate authorisations, and all of them must be in place before the container can leave.
3. The gate staff contact the line's agent, settle the unpaid charge themselves, and release the container as a courtesy to the driver.
4. The terminal holds the truck until the vessel that brought the container has sailed, after which unreleased containers are released automatically.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The lesson's central distinction is that shipping line release, customs release, and terminal release are three separate authorisations from three different organisations, each with its own requirements, and a container may pass the gate only when all of them are in place. Customs release satisfies the legal key, and the delivery order evidences the commercial instruction, but the commercial key itself is incomplete: the line withholds release while its charges are unpaid, and without the line's release the terminal's systems cannot show the container as available. The truck goes home empty.

Answer 1 fails because it treats two of the three keys as the whole set — the terminal honours a delivery order only when the line behind it has released the container. Answer 3 describes a correction that cannot happen at the gate: the unpaid charge must be settled with the line's agent, who can then complete the release properly. Answer 4 invents a rule that does not exist; the passage of time never makes an unreleased container releasable.

The takeaway: track all three keys separately, and send the truck only when all three are confirmed.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
An importer bought machine parts on documents against payment (D/P) terms. The container has been discharged and stands in the terminal yard, but the original bill of lading is still in the custody of the importer's bank and the importer has not yet paid. No telex release exists for this shipment.

What must happen before the shipping line's agent can issue a delivery order?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The importer pays the bank, the bank releases the document set, and the original bill of lading is surrendered to the line or its agent to establish the release basis.
2. The terminal releases the container to the importer's truck as soon as the customs declaration is lodged, since the goods plainly belong to the importer.
3. The agent issues the delivery order against a copy of the bill of lading and the importer's identification, because the original is only a formality.
4. The line waits until the container's free time expires and then releases the container directly to whoever presents a collection letter at the gate.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

A delivery order can only be issued once a release basis exists. Under D/P, the buyer's bank hands over the shipping documents only against payment, so the importer's payment is the step that starts the chain: the bank releases the documents, the original bill of lading — the document of title that controls the right to delivery — is surrendered to the line or its agent, and only then can the agent authorise release and issue the delivery order. Worked Example 2 traced exactly this sequence.

Answer 2 confuses the legal key with the commercial one: lodging a declaration starts the customs process, but customs release never substitutes for the line's release. Answer 3 is wrong because the original bill is not a formality — possession of the original controls the right to demand the goods, and no agent can issue a delivery order against a photocopy. Answer 4 invents a practice that would defeat the whole control system; free time expiring triggers charges, not release.

The takeaway: under D/P the goods wait on the payment, so the payment should be planned around the vessel's arrival rather than arranged after it.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
At the gate-out of a forty-foot import container, the driver notices a deep dent in a side panel during the joint inspection. The terminal clerk, under time pressure, suggests signing the interchange record now as clean and raising the matter later if any damage is found.

Which statement best describes the correct course of action and its reason?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The driver should sign the clean record, because the terminal's equipment performed the lift, so the terminal answers for every dent found on any container.
2. The driver should sign the clean record because the seal number is the only condition that matters at an interchange, and the seal is intact.
3. The driver should insist that the dent and the seal number are recorded on the equipment interchange receipt before signing, because the signed record fixes the container's condition at handover and decides who answers for damage found later.
4. The driver should refuse the container, abandon the collection, and wait for the shipping line to send a replacement box.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The equipment interchange receipt is the record that acknowledges the transfer of the container and its condition at the moment responsibility changes hands. Exceptions recorded at gate-out show that the dent existed before the truck moved, which protects the driver and the transporter from answering for damage they did not cause and gives the importer a documented starting point for any claim on the ocean carriage. Signing a clean receipt for a dented box states the opposite of the truth, and a signed record is very hard to unwind later.

Answer 1 gets the liability backwards: the whole purpose of the joint inspection and the EIR is to establish the condition at handover, not to assign every dent to the terminal by default. Answer 2 mistakes the seal for the whole inspection; the seal shows the box was not opened, but the condition of the panels is exactly what the record exists to capture. Answer 4 overreacts — the correct response is a documented exception, not an abandoned collection.

The takeaway: the signature is the moment responsibility changes hands, so record the truth first and sign second.
