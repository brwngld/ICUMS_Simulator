# DRAFT — Lesson 5.2: Freight Quotations and Shipment Booking

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Quotations and bookings: the commercial backbone of a shipment
BODY:
The previous lesson, Role and Responsibilities of Freight Forwarders, explained that a freight forwarder arranges transportation on behalf of customers. Before any cargo moves, however, a commercial process must take place: the customer asks what the service will cost, the forwarder answers with a formal offer, and the two parties turn that offer into a reserved place on a vessel, aircraft, or vehicle.

Three terms carry this process.

An enquiry is a customer's request for transportation rates and services, usually stating the cargo, the route, and the expected timing.

A freight quotation is a statement of proposed charges and conditions for transporting goods or providing related logistics services.

A shipment booking is an arrangement made with a carrier or logistics service provider to reserve transportation capacity for cargo.

The life of a shipment enquiry follows a recognisable sequence.

1. The shipper sends an enquiry to one or more forwarders or carriers.
2. The service provider collects or confirms the details needed to price the movement.
3. The forwarder prepares and sends a quotation.
4. The shipper compares the quotations received and accepts one, usually in writing.
5. The forwarder books space with the carrier, and the carrier returns a booking confirmation.
6. The shipment is prepared and delivered against the booked schedule and its deadlines.

Each stage produces a record: the enquiry, the quotation, the booking confirmation. These records matter because they fix what was asked, what was promised, and what was confirmed. When a dispute arises about price, timing, or responsibility, the parties return to them. Errors made at this commercial stage, such as a vague cargo description or an unnoticed expiry date, surface later as operational problems. This lesson therefore treats the quotation and the booking not as paperwork but as the backbone of the shipment.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The shipper's enquiry: information a forwarder needs to quote
BODY:
A forwarder cannot price a movement it does not understand. A professional quotation begins with a complete enquiry, and the shipper who supplies the right information at the start receives accurate prices faster. The essential information, and the reason each item matters, is as follows.

1. Origin and destination. The forwarder must know where the cargo will be collected, where it must be delivered, and whether the service should cover the whole journey or only part of it. The answer determines the ports or airports used, the services available, and whether road haulage, terminal handling, or customs formalities must be included in the price.

2. Cargo description. The forwarder needs to know what the goods are, how they are packed, and how they must be handled. Food products, chemicals, machinery, and fragile goods each carry different handling requirements and restrictions, and some cargoes need special equipment or approval before a carrier will accept them.

3. Weight and dimensions. The weight and size of the cargo, including packaging, decide the chargeable weight and the equipment the shipment requires. Two consignments of equal value can be priced very differently if one is heavy and compact while the other is light and bulky.

4. Readiness dates. The forwarder must know when the cargo will be packed and ready for collection. A quotation is only useful if its sailing dates match the cargo's readiness; a price for a vessel the cargo cannot catch is worthless.

5. Service expectations. The shipper should state whether delivery is required door to door or port to port, how quickly the goods must arrive, whether insurance is wanted, and what documents the buyer or the destination country requires. Each expectation changes the price and the conditions offered.

The same information is needed whether the enquiry goes to a carrier or to a forwarder. An incomplete enquiry forces the service provider to guess, and a quotation built on guesses is usually wrong in the customer's favour or the forwarder's. Responsible forwarders ask questions before quoting.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Gross weight, volumetric weight, and chargeable weight
BODY:
Freight charges rest on a weight, but not always the weight a scale shows. Three terms are needed.

Gross weight is the total weight of the goods, including their packaging, but excluding the weight of the transport equipment unless otherwise specified. A pallet of cartons is weighed together with the pallet and the cartons.

Volumetric weight is a calculated weight based on the dimensions of the cargo, used in certain freight pricing methods. It exists because cargo space is scarce: a consignment of plastic buckets may weigh little yet fill a container as completely as a consignment of steel tools. Air carriers convert dimensions into a notional weight with a standard formula: the length multiplied by the width multiplied by the height in centimetres, divided by 6,000, gives the volumetric weight in kilograms. Some carriers or contracts apply different divisors, but 6,000 is the standard under the rules of the International Air Transport Association.

Chargeable weight is the weight used to calculate transportation charges, determined according to the applicable freight rating rules. In air freight the rule is simple: the carrier charges by the higher of the gross weight and the volumetric weight. A light, bulky consignment therefore pays for the space it occupies, and a heavy, compact one pays for its true weight.

Ocean freight follows its own pricing bases. A shipper using a whole container generally pays a rate per container, within the equipment's limits. A shipper whose goods occupy only part of a container, in what is called a less than container load shipment, is commonly rated by the weight or by the measurement, meaning the space, that the cargo occupies, whichever the tariff applies. The next lesson, Shipping Instructions and Cargo Consolidation, examines these arrangements in detail.

This is why a forwarder asks for both the weight and the dimensions of every consignment: without them, the pricing basis of the quotation cannot be established.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Factors that influence freight charges
BODY:
Freight prices are not arbitrary. They respond to identifiable factors, and understanding them explains why two quotations for similar cargo can differ.

1. Distance and route. Longer distances cost more to serve, and some routes are inherently more expensive than others because of geography, transhipment requirements, or the balance of trade on the lane.

2. Mode and service level. Air freight is faster and more expensive than sea freight; a direct service usually costs more than one requiring transhipment; premium arrangements carry a price.

3. Weight and volume. The chargeable weight introduced earlier is the engine of the calculation: heavier and bulkier cargo consumes more of the carrier's capacity.

4. Nature of the cargo. Dangerous goods, temperature-controlled cargo, and out-of-gauge items that cannot fit a standard container all require special equipment, handling, and approvals, and are priced accordingly.

5. Equipment and space availability. When containers are scarce on a route, or vessel capacity is tight in a peak season, rates rise; when capacity is abundant, rates soften.

6. Fuel, currency, and market conditions. Fuel price movements and exchange-rate movements feed into surcharges, which the next concept explains. More broadly, freight rates are set commercially. A spot rate is the price quoted for one shipment under current market conditions, typically valid for a short period. A contract rate is a rate agreed in advance between a shipper and a carrier or forwarder for a defined period or volume, giving the shipper protection against short-term movements. Liner shipping is a scheduled, tariff-based industry, and carriers from time to time announce a general rate increase, an across-the-board upward adjustment of base freight rates on a trade lane, typically to manage capacity or restore profitability. None of these mechanisms has a fixed amount attached; the learner should understand the concepts, not memorise figures.

Every factor on this list appears, sometimes invisibly, in the lines of a quotation.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Freight rates, surcharges, and additional service charges
BODY:
A freight quotation is rarely one number. Its lines belong to three families, and a competent learner can name the family each line comes from.

1. The freight rate. The freight rate is the price charged for transporting cargo, usually calculated according to an applicable pricing basis. It is the core price of the movement itself: an ocean freight rate per container or per weight or measurement, or an air freight rate per kilogram of chargeable weight. Everything else on a quotation is built around it.

2. Freight surcharges. A freight surcharge is an additional charge applied to a freight service to recover or account for specified costs or operating conditions. Two long-standing examples give the idea. The bunker adjustment factor, usually abbreviated to BAF, is a fuel-related surcharge reflecting the cost of the fuel a vessel burns. The currency adjustment factor, CAF, reflects movements in exchange rates. A security charge linked to the International Ship and Port Facility Security Code, known as the ISPS charge, recovers the cost of security measures required in ports and on ships. Surcharges are attached to the main carriage and can change when the underlying cost changes, which is one reason quotations carry validity periods.

3. Additional service charges and local charges. These cover the handling and services at each end of the movement that sit outside the main carriage. Typical examples include the terminal handling charge, THC, applied at the origin and again at the destination for handling the container at the terminal; CFS charges for handling less than container load cargo at a container freight station; the documentation fee charged for issuing the bill of lading; the seal charge for the container seal; collection, delivery, and customs clearance services; and storage where cargo waits beyond the arrangements made.

The distinction matters in practice. A quotation showing only a headline freight rate, with local charges absent, is not a cheaper quotation; it is an incomplete one.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The components of a freight quotation
BODY:
What does a complete quotation contain? Formats vary between forwarders, but a professional freight quotation presents the following elements, and the customer should expect to find each of them.

1. The parties and the reference. Who is quoting, to whom, for which enquiry, and on what date.

2. The routing and scope. The origin and destination, the ports or airports to be used, whether the service is door to door, port to port, or something between, and the expected sailing frequency or departure dates.

3. The charges, grouped and itemised. The main carriage, meaning the freight rate plus any surcharges; the origin local charges such as collection, terminal handling, export formalities, seal, and security; and the destination local charges such as terminal handling, clearance assistance, and delivery. Each line should be identifiable, because unnamed lump sums hide the exclusions a careful reader must catch.

4. The pricing basis. The chargeable weight or measurement used, or the equipment to be provided, so the customer can verify how the price was built.

5. The schedule information. The expected transit time and, where given, the estimated time of departure and the estimated time of arrival.

6. The validity period. The date until which the quoted prices hold.

7. The conditions and exclusions. What the price does not include: commonly customs duties and taxes at destination, cargo insurance unless requested, storage beyond the agreed arrangements, and charges arising from delay caused by the customer. Payment terms and the currency of the quotation belong here too.

A quotation is the forwarder's formal offer. Once the customer accepts it, it becomes the reference against which the invoice is checked, so the time to notice a missing item is before acceptance, not after the goods have sailed.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Validity, transit time, ETD, and ETA
BODY:
Quotations speak the language of schedules, and four terms carry it.

Transit time is the estimated or actual duration required to transport goods between specified locations. A quotation stating a transit time of twenty-one days port to port is indicating that, under normal operating conditions, the cargo should complete the sea journey in about three weeks. Transit time matters to cash flow, to stock planning, and above all to any deadline the buyer has set.

The estimated time of departure, ETD, is the anticipated date and time when a vessel, aircraft, or other transport service will depart. The estimated time of arrival, ETA, is the anticipated date and time when it will arrive. Both words deserve attention: these are estimates, not guarantees. Scheduled services are planned, but vessels can sail late, connections can be missed, and arrival dates can move. A professional forwarder communicates schedule changes promptly rather than allowing the customer to treat the ETA as an appointment.

Container liner services on major trade lanes commonly sail on a weekly frequency, which gives shippers a rhythm to plan against: if one sailing is missed, the next is usually close behind, and the cost of missing it is measured in that interval.

The freight validity period is the period during which a quoted freight rate or commercial offer remains valid under its stated conditions. Ocean rates, surcharges, and exchange rates move, sometimes quickly, so a forwarder cannot responsibly hold a price open forever. A quotation valid for fourteen days tells the customer to decide within fourteen days; after that, the forwarder must re-check the market before confirming. Validity protects both sides: the customer against silent changes, and the forwarder against being bound to a price the market has left behind.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The booking procedure: from acceptance to confirmed space
BODY:
When the shipper accepts a quotation, the operational half of the lesson begins. The procedures for requesting and confirming a booking follow the same logic across sea, air, road, and warehouse services.

1. Acceptance. The shipper accepts the quotation in writing, before the validity period ends, and instructs the forwarder to proceed. The acceptance should confirm the cargo details, the dates, and the scope of service, so that the instruction matches the offer.

2. The booking request. The forwarder submits a booking to the carrier, or to the provider of the service concerned, stating the cargo, the equipment required, the ports or places, and the intended sailing or flight. Where the forwarder acts as a consolidator, it books space in its own name for the combined cargo of several customers.

3. Space confirmation. The carrier allocates the capacity and returns a booking confirmation, a document or electronic notification confirming that the carrier or service provider has accepted the shipment booking. Historically, a paper called a shipping order instructed the vessel to receive the booked cargo; in modern practice these workflows are largely electronic, and the booking confirmation is the document the learner will actually meet.

4. Communication. The forwarder passes the confirmation to the customer with the practical instructions: when and where the cargo must be delivered or collected, the equipment details, and the applicable deadlines, known as cut-offs, that the shipper must plan around.

5. Amendments and problems. If the cargo details change, or space is unavailable on the intended service, the booking must be amended or moved. A confirmed booking secures space on a planned service; it does not freeze the schedule. Departure dates can shift, and cargo that misses its deadlines can be left behind, which is why the confirmation must be read carefully on the day it arrives.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: What a booking confirmation contains
BODY:
A booking confirmation is short, but every line on it drives an operational decision. The important information it typically contains is the following.

1. The booking reference. The number that identifies the booking with the carrier. Every subsequent document, invoice, and communication refers to it, and quoting it in correspondence prevents confusion between shipments.

2. The parties. The shipper or forwarder on whose account the booking is made, and the consignee details, which may follow later under a to-be-advised arrangement.

3. The routing. The port or place of loading, the port or place of discharge, and any transhipment point, together with the vessel and voyage where known.

4. The equipment. The type, size, and number of units booked, for example one 20-foot container, so the shipper can plan loading and the forwarder can plan collection.

5. The schedule. The estimated time of departure and the estimated time of arrival, or the sailing and its frequency.

6. The deadlines. The cargo cut-off and the documentation cut-off for the booked sailing, which the shipper must plan around.

7. The terms. The freight terms agreed, whether charges are payable at origin or at destination, and the carrier's conditions of carriage, together with any special instructions such as temperature requirements for refrigerated cargo or acceptance conditions for dangerous goods.

Reading the confirmation is not an administrative formality. An error discovered on the day of issue, such as the wrong container size or a mismatched sailing date, can be corrected cheaply. The same error discovered at the terminal gate, or after the vessel has sailed, becomes a cost, a delay, or both.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Cut-off discipline: cargo and documentation deadlines
BODY:
Every sailing is guarded by deadlines, and the booking confirmation states them. Container shipping recognises two principal cut-offs, and they protect different things.

1. The cargo cut-off. This is the deadline by which the packed container must be delivered to the terminal, either to the container yard of the port or to a container freight station for consolidated cargo, with its verified gross mass declared. Verified gross mass, VGM, is a safety requirement under the International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea: since 1 July 2016 the shipper must obtain and communicate the verified gross mass of every packed container, and a container without it may not be loaded. The cut-off exists because the terminal needs time to plan the stowage of the vessel.

2. The documentation cut-off. This is the deadline for finalising the shipping instruction, meaning the details the carrier needs to prepare the bill of lading: the shipper, the consignee, the cargo description, the weights, and the marks. The documentation cut-off usually falls after the cargo cut-off, but both must be met.

A shipment that misses either deadline is normally rolled over. A rollover is the movement of booked cargo from its intended sailing to a later one. On a weekly service the rollover typically costs about a week of transit, and with it the shipment may incur additional handling or storage costs, while the customer's downstream plans, from shelf dates to factory schedules, slip with it.

Cut-off discipline is therefore a planning habit, not a scramble. Professional forwarders work backwards from the cut-offs: they confirm cargo readiness early, submit shipping instructions as soon as the booking is confirmed, chase outstanding details days before the deadline rather than hours, and escalate immediately when a deadline is at risk.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Comparing quotations: reading beyond the headline price
BODY:
Comparing quotations is a skill, and like any skill it has a method. The aim is not to find the lowest number but to establish which offer delivers the required service at the best total cost under dependable conditions.

The first step is to make the quotations comparable. Two quotations can only be compared line by line if they cover the same scope: the same origin and destination points, the same service coverage, the same equipment. If one quotation is door to door and the other port to port, the comparison must be corrected before any conclusion is drawn.

The comparison then turns on four questions, matching the criteria of price, transit time, service coverage, and conditions.

1. Price: what does each quotation truly cost? Add back what each excludes. A quotation that omits destination terminal handling, clearance, and delivery is not cheaper; those charges will arrive, either as a second invoice or as a stoppage at destination until they are paid.

2. Transit time: when does the cargo actually arrive? Compare the estimated departure dates and the transit times against the buyer's deadline, and add the risk of missed connections where the routing includes a transhipment.

3. Service coverage: what exactly is included? Door collection, customs formalities, insurance, and delivery each change what the shipper must still arrange and pay for separately.

4. Conditions and reliability: how dependable is what is promised? Check the validity period against the date the shipment will actually be ready, the payment terms, and the dependability of the service offered. A direct sailing with a dependable carrier is worth more than a marginally cheaper routing with tight connections and a record of slippage.

Where two quotations are genuinely unequal in scope, ask the forwarders to re-quote on identical terms. A like-for-like comparison is the only honest one, and the cheapest headline is not always the best quotation.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A quotation for six pallets of shea butter, Kumasi to London
BODY:
A shea butter processor in Kumasi has concluded a sale to a cosmetics buyer in London. Six pallets are planned, each 1.2 by 1.0 by 1.5 metres and about 300 kilograms gross: 1,800 kilograms and about 10.8 cubic metres in total. The buyer needs the goods within two months. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: The enquiry. The processor asks a freight forwarder to quote, stating the collection point in Kumasi, the delivery address in London, the cargo description of palletised shea butter in cartons, the weight and dimensions, a readiness date three weeks away, and the required door-to-door service including export formalities. The enquiry gives the forwarder everything needed to price the movement.

Step 2: The quotation. The forwarder replies with a structured offer, valid for fourteen days.

- Collection, Kumasi to the Port of Tema: 600 US dollars
- Export documentation and port formalities: 150 US dollars
- Origin terminal handling and container freight station receiving: 320 US dollars
- Container seal and ISPS security charge: 80 US dollars
- Ocean freight, Tema to London, including fuel-related surcharge: 1,350 US dollars
- Destination terminal handling and container freight station charges in London: 480 US dollars
- Delivery to the buyer's warehouse in London: 390 US dollars
- Total: 3,370 US dollars, excluding customs duties and taxes and cargo insurance

The quotation states a transit time of about four weeks door to door, weekly sailings, estimated departure and arrival dates, and its pricing basis: because the consignment is light for the space it occupies, the ocean leg is rated by volume.

Step 3: The review. The processor checks the offer: the scope is genuinely door to door, the destination charges are included so no second invoice awaits in London, and the estimated arrival fits the buyer's deadline.

Step 4: The acceptance. On day five, inside the validity period, the processor accepts in writing and instructs the forwarder to book. Had she waited past day fourteen, the prices would have needed reconfirmation.

Step 5: The booking. The forwarder books space on a sailing after the readiness date and receives a booking confirmation stating the vessel, the cut-off deadlines, and the equipment, and tells the processor when the truck will collect the pallets and when her documents must be finalised.

What does this example demonstrate?

Each stage converts the previous one: the enquiry into a priced offer, the acceptance into an instruction, the booking into reserved capacity. The records accumulate, and the accuracy of the first stage governs all the others.

Key lesson: accept within validity, and check inclusions before accepting, because a complete quotation accepted on time is the foundation of an uneventful shipment.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Two quotations compared: Mumbai to Rotterdam
BODY:
A distributor of machinery spare parts in Rotterdam needs four pallets of parts collected from a supplier in Mumbai and delivered to its warehouse in time for a fixed distribution date six weeks ahead. Two forwarders reply. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Quote A: a total of 1,080 US dollars, port to port. It includes ocean freight from Mumbai to Rotterdam with surcharges at 830 US dollars and origin charges in Mumbai at 250 US dollars. It excludes destination terminal handling, customs clearance assistance, and delivery. It states a transit time of about thirty-two days with a transhipment, a departure in ten days, and a validity of seven days.

Quote B: a total of 1,780 US dollars, door to door. It includes ocean freight with surcharges at 900, origin charges at 250, destination terminal handling and container freight station charges at 320, customs clearance assistance at 140, and delivery to the warehouse at 170, all in US dollars. It states a transit time of about twenty-five days on a direct service, a departure in six days, and a validity of fourteen days.

Step 1: Correct the scope. Quote A's headline is 700 US dollars lower, but it stops at the port. Priced separately, the excluded items come to about 660 US dollars: 320 for destination terminal handling and freight station charges, 140 for clearance assistance, and 200 for delivery. Added to the headline, the like-for-like cost of Quote A is about 1,740 US dollars, barely below Quote B.

Step 2: Compare the schedules. Quote A departs four days later and takes about seven days longer, arriving on the very edge of the distribution date, with a transhipment connection that can slip. Quote B arrives with more than a week in hand.

Step 3: Compare the conditions. Quote A is valid for only seven days, and the distributor needs five days for internal approval, leaving no room. Quote B stays open twice as long.

Step 4: Decide. The distributor accepts Quote B. Once destination charges are counted, the price advantage of Quote A has effectively vanished, while B is faster, direct, and open longer.

What does this example demonstrate?

The headline price of a quotation is not its cost. Exclusions, later departures, longer transits, and short validity all carry a price that appears only when the quotation is read line by line against the shipment's real requirements.

Key lesson: compare like with like, add back what is missing, and weigh time and reliability alongside money.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A booking that rolls: the missed documentation cut-off at Tema
BODY:
A company exporting palletised cocoa products from Tema to Antwerp holds a confirmed booking on a vessel with an estimated departure on Friday. The confirmation states, illustratively and for this booking only, a cargo cut-off of Wednesday at 4 p.m. and a documentation cut-off of Thursday at noon. All times in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: The plan. The forwarder arranges a truck to deliver the container to the terminal on Tuesday, one clear day before the cargo cut-off, and asks the customer for the final shipping instructions, meaning the consignee details, cargo description, weights, and marks the carrier needs for the bill of lading, requesting them by Monday.

Step 2: The slip. The customer is still awaiting its buyer's confirmation of the consignee details and sends complete instructions only on Thursday at 3 p.m., three hours after the documentation cut-off. The container is standing in the terminal, safely inside its cargo deadline, but the carrier cannot issue accurate transport documents without the instruction.

Step 3: The rollover. The carrier rolls the container to the next sailing on the following Friday. The goods, delivered to the terminal a day early, now wait a full week beyond plan, and additional terminal and administration costs begin to build, as they typically do when cargo lingers against its booked schedule.

Step 4: The consequences. The cocoa products reach Antwerp about a week later than promised. The buyer, a distributor, has to rearrange a warehouse slot and disappoint a downstream customer. The exporter spends days on corrective administration, and notices that the buyer's confidence has not rolled over with the container.

Step 5: The prevention. Afterwards, the forwarder and the exporter agree new habits: shipping instructions are submitted when the booking is confirmed, not at the deadline; outstanding details are chased days ahead; and a deadline at risk is escalated before it passes, because a cut-off raised in advance can sometimes be discussed with the carrier, while a cut-off missed in silence cannot be undone.

What does this example demonstrate?

The booking confirmation is not the end of the booking process; its deadlines are. Cargo that is physically ready can still miss its vessel on paperwork.

Key lesson: cut-offs are immovable planning anchors. Work backwards from them, chase details early, and never assume that a booked sailing will wait.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: A quotation is an offer with an expiry
BODY:
A quotation is an offer, not a permanent price, and three of its limits deserve special respect.

First, the validity period. A quotation held open for fourteen days means fourteen days. Rates move with fuel prices, exchange rates, capacity, and the carriers' general rate adjustments. A forwarder who honours an expired quotation may be subsidising the customer out of its own margin, and a customer who assumes an expired price is still on the table may receive a revised figure after acceptance. Accept within validity, or ask for the price to be reconfirmed.

Second, the estimates. The estimated time of departure and the estimated time of arrival are the industry's honest word for uncertainty. Scheduled services are planned to keep them, but weather, congestion, and missed connections move them. Sensible buyers build a buffer between the promised arrival and the date the goods are truly needed, and sensible forwarders report schedule changes the day they learn of them.

Third, the scope. A quotation covers exactly what it says it covers. Silence about destination charges, duties, or insurance is not a promise to include them; it is a gap the customer will pay for later.

The habit to build is simple: read the validity date, the estimates, and the exclusions before comparing prices, and again before accepting.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Never book on a vague cargo description
BODY:
The most damaging booking errors begin with vague cargo information. A note reading "general goods, about two truckloads" is not a description a forwarder can book with.

The consequences arrive in a predictable sequence. The quotation is built on assumed weights and dimensions, so the chargeable weight is a guess. When the cargo is actually presented, the verified figures differ: the shipment is heavier, or bulkier, than assumed. The booked equipment may be too small, the quoted price must be re-rated, the planned space may be insufficient, and in the worst case the cargo cannot travel as booked at all, missing its sailing or flight while a new arrangement is made.

There is a harder edge to the same error. Carriers and terminals are entitled to know what they are carrying. A description vague enough to hide dangerous goods does not merely cause delay; it creates a safety risk and can lead to the cargo being refused or reported. Accuracy here is a safety obligation, not a formality.

The discipline is equally simple. Before a quotation is requested, the shipper should establish the true gross weight including packaging, the dimensions of each package or pallet, the honest description of the goods, and the readiness date. Before a booking is confirmed, the forwarder should check that the accepted quotation rests on those facts, not on assumptions. If the facts change between quotation and booking, the quotation should be revisited, rather than the difference discovered at the terminal gate.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Common booking errors and how discipline prevents them
BODY:
Most booking failures repeat a small set of patterns. Recognising them is the cheapest form of prevention.

1. Booking on assumptions. Cargo details that were estimated rather than verified turn the quotation into fiction and the booking into a dispute.

2. Ignoring the calendar. Accepting a quotation whose validity expires before the cargo is ready, or accepting a sailing the goods cannot realistically catch, converts a good price into a rollover.

3. Reading the booking confirmation carelessly. The confirmation carries the reference, the equipment, the schedule, and the cut-offs. Treating it as a receipt rather than as an instruction sheet means the deadlines that matter are discovered from the carrier's reminders, or from the rollover notice.

4. Confusing the booking with the schedule. A confirmed booking is space on a planned service, not a guarantee that the vessel will sail on the stated minute. Plans built with no buffer absorb every schedule change as a crisis.

5. Leaving documents to the end. Shipping instructions that arrive after the documentation cut-off roll the cargo as surely as a container that misses the terminal gate.

Each pattern ends the same way: a later sailing, additional costs, and a customer who planned around a date that moved. Each is prevented the same way too: accurate information before quoting, careful reading at confirmation, and deadline-driven planning between booking and departure.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A trader in Accra emails a freight forwarder: "Please quote me to move 300 cartons of footwear to Dubai next month." No weights, no dimensions, and no service details are given.

Which option best explains why the forwarder cannot provide an accurate quotation yet?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Freight quotations may be issued only by ocean carriers, so the enquiry must be passed to a shipping line before any figure can be given.
2. The forwarder should quote immediately using a standard rate for all cargo, because shipments of footwear are always priced by the carton.
3. Without the weight and dimensions of the cartons, the expected readiness date, and the service expectations, the forwarder cannot determine the chargeable weight, the suitable service, or a defensible price.
4. No quotation is needed, because freight charges for international shipments are fixed amounts prescribed by customs authorities.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

A quotation is only as sound as the information beneath it. The forwarder needs the weight and dimensions of the cartons to establish the chargeable weight, which is the weight used to calculate transportation charges; the readiness date to match the cargo to a realistic sailing; and the service expectations to decide the scope and the cost of the offer. Without them, any figure would be a guess that could be re-rated later, to the customer's cost and embarrassment.

Answer 1 fails because freight forwarders prepare quotations every day; they are the normal first point of contact for exactly this kind of request. Answer 2 fails because cargo is not priced by the carton: the pricing basis follows the applicable freight rating rules, built on weight and measurement or on the equipment used. Answer 4 fails because freight charges are commercial prices set by service providers in a competitive market, not fixed amounts prescribed by customs authorities.

The takeaway: a complete enquiry, covering weight, dimensions, description, readiness, and service expectations, is the first requirement of an accurate quotation.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
An importer in Takoradi receives two quotations for a consignment of machinery parts from Antwerp. Quote 1 shows a lower total but covers port-to-port carriage only, excludes the destination charges, and offers a slower, later sailing. Quote 2 shows a higher total that includes terminal handling, clearance assistance, and delivery to the warehouse, on a faster direct service.

Which statement best describes the correct way to choose between them?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Quote 1 should be accepted without further checks, because the lowest figure on a quotation is by definition the cheapest shipment.
2. The quotations should be compared line by line, adding the destination charges Quote 1 omits and weighing transit time, service coverage, and validity against the delivery deadline, before deciding.
3. Quote 2 should be rejected automatically, because a quotation whose total is higher can never represent better value.
4. The two quotations cannot be compared at all, because different forwarders are not permitted to quote different scopes of service.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The comparison method taught in this lesson is line by line: first make the quotations comparable by correcting the scope, then add back what each excludes, then weigh transit time, service coverage, validity, and reliability against the shipment's real deadline. Quote 1's lower headline excludes destination charges that will certainly arrive, and its slower, later sailing puts the delivery date at risk; only a line-by-line comparison exposes this before the decision is made.

Answer 1 fails because the lowest figure on paper is frequently the cheapest quotation rather than the cheapest shipment, once the exclusions are priced. Answer 3 fails because a higher total can reflect genuinely included services and a better service level, which is exactly what this scenario illustrates. Answer 4 fails because forwarders routinely quote different scopes; those differences are precisely why a like-for-like comparison is required.

The takeaway: compare price, transit time, service coverage, and conditions together, and treat any quotation with significant exclusions as incomplete rather than cheap.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A forwarder at the Port of Tema holds a confirmed booking for a container sailing on Friday. The complete shipping instructions reach the carrier after the documentation cut-off for the booked vessel has passed.

Which option best explains the likely consequence and the correct response?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The container will usually be rolled to the next sailing, delaying arrival by roughly one weekly service and possibly adding costs, so cut-offs must be planned from the moment of booking.
2. The vessel will wait in port until the shipping instructions are completed, because a booked container must always sail on its confirmed vessel.
3. The carrier is obliged to reduce the ocean freight as compensation whenever documents are submitted late.
4. Documentation cut-offs apply only to full container loads, so the delay will have no effect on consolidated shipments.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

The documentation cut-off is the deadline for finalising the shipping instructions the carrier needs in order to prepare the bill of lading. Instructions that arrive after it leave the carrier unable to include the container in the vessel's documentation, so the cargo is normally rolled to the next sailing. On a weekly service that costs about a week of transit, together with possible additional handling and storage costs and the delay of every downstream plan that depended on the arrival date. Prevention is procedural: submit instructions when the booking is confirmed, chase outstanding details early, and escalate before a deadline passes rather than after it.

Answer 2 fails because scheduled liner services keep their published schedules; vessels do not wait for late paperwork. Answer 3 fails because no such obligation exists; the real remedy is deadline discipline, not compensation. Answer 4 fails because documentation cut-offs apply to consolidated and full-container traffic alike.

The takeaway: cargo can be fully ready and still miss the vessel on paperwork, so the cut-offs on the booking confirmation govern the plan.
