=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: What shipping instructions are
BODY:
The previous lesson, Freight Quotations and Shipment Booking, followed a shipment from the first enquiry to booked space: the forwarder quoted, the customer accepted, and the carrier confirmed the booking with its deadlines. What the carrier still does not have, at that point, is the detailed knowledge of the goods. A booking reserves space; it does not describe what will fill it. That description arrives in the shipping instructions.

Shipping instructions are the information provided to a carrier or its representative for arranging shipment details and preparing transport documentation. In daily work they are so central that the industry simply calls them the SI. They are the shipper's written statement of what is being shipped, by whom, to whom, from where, to where, and under what handling requirements.

Two features give the shipping instructions their importance. First, the carrier never sees the sales contract, rarely questions the trader about the goods, and has no independent knowledge of what is inside the boxes. Everything the carrier knows about a consignment, it knows because the shipping instructions told it. Second, and following directly from the first, the carrier prepares the transport document from these instructions. The details typed into the instructions become the details printed on the bill of lading; they also feed the manifest the carrier declares to authorities, and the data the forwarder uses for the export declaration.

The shipping instructions are therefore the single authoritative source from which the shipment's paperwork flows. A mistake made at this stage is not one wrong field among many; it is an error planted at the head of a chain, and every document downstream repeats it. That is why careful forwarders treat the preparation and checking of instructions as skilled work, not as typing, and why this lesson gives them a full half of its attention.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The information shipping instructions contain
BODY:
A complete set of shipping instructions answers every question the carrier and the forwarder need to book, document, and handle the consignment. The commonly required information falls into six groups.

1. The parties. The shipper, the consignee, and the notifying party, each with its full name, address, and contact details. The notifying party is the person or organisation to be informed when the goods arrive; often it is the consignee itself, sometimes its agent or customs representative. Misspelled or incomplete party details are among the most consequential errors, because the transport document will show exactly what the instructions said.

2. The cargo description. A plain, accurate statement of the nature and type of the goods: what they are, how they are packed, and the number of packages. The description must match the goods as they truly are, not as a catalogue might wish them to be. The marks and numbers stencilled on the packages, which Module 3's lesson Cargo Types, Packaging, and Handling treated under cargo marking, are recorded here so that each package can be connected to its document.

3. Quantities, weights, and measurements. The number of packages, the gross weight of each package and of the whole consignment, and the volume in cubic metres. These figures decide whether the cargo fits the booked space and are the basis on which consolidated freight is assessed.

4. The route. The port or airport of loading and the port or airport of discharge, the place of delivery where relevant, and the requested sailing, meaning the vessel or flight named in the booking confirmation from the previous lesson.

5. The commercial terms. Whether freight is payable at origin or at destination, following the delivery term agreed in the sales contract, and the party to be charged.

6. Special handling requirements. Dangerous goods must be declared as such with their proper details, temperature-sensitive cargo must state its required temperature, and unusually large, heavy, or awkward pieces must be described so the carrier can plan the handling.

Most of this information already exists in the exporter's commercial documents, so preparing the instructions is less a matter of composing than of transferring, which is why the checking discipline below matters so much.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: From instructions to transport document
BODY:
The flow from instruction to document is mechanical, and that is precisely its danger. The carrier's documentation staff do not investigate the goods; they read the shipping instructions and produce the transport document from them. If the instructions say ninety cartons, the bill of lading says ninety cartons. If the consignee's name is wrong in the instructions, it is wrong on the bill, and it is wrong on the carrier's manifest, and it follows the container across the ocean.

The bill of lading, the classic ocean transport document, serves three functions at once: it is a receipt for the goods as received, evidence of the contract of carriage, and a document of title, meaning that possession of the original document controls the right to claim the goods at destination. A document with such powers deserves accurate inputs. Module 6 treats the transport documents themselves in detail; this lesson is concerned with the instructions that feed them.

Timing governs the exchange. The carrier sets a documentation cut-off, the deadline by which the shipping instructions, and the bill of lading details drawn from them, must be finalised before departure. This is one of the cut-offs the booking confirmation announced. Submit the instructions in time and there is a period in which corrections are still routine. Submit them late, or amend them after the cut-off, and the change becomes an amendment, attracting fees and administration, or the shipment rolls to the next sailing.

The professional habit that follows is simple to state: instructions go to the carrier as early as possible, never as late as possible. An early submission leaves room for the carrier's queries and for corrections discovered in checking; a last-minute submission converts every small defect into a deadline problem.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Why accuracy matters: the cost of instruction errors
BODY:
Because the documents are made from the instructions, errors in the instructions are never merely clerical. Consider the common ones.

1. The wrong consignee. A misspelled or entirely wrong consignee name propagates onto the bill of lading. At destination the document controls delivery and the customs declaration must agree with it, so a mismatch can hold the goods while corrections are arranged, and a document naming a party with nothing to do with the goods invites questions no shipment wants.

2. The inaccurate cargo description. A vague or wrong description may not match the commercial invoice, the packing list, or the goods themselves. Customs administrations assess and control goods by their descriptions, so discrepancies surface at clearance, at either or both ends, and dangerous goods that are described as something else are a safety matter, not a paperwork matter.

3. The wrong quantities, weights, or measurements. A wrong package count makes the transport document disagree with the invoice and packing list. A wrong weight upsets the booking's fit, contradicts the verified gross mass of the packed container, and can lead to the container being reweighed or rejected.

4. The missing special-handling flag. Undeclared dangerous goods, an unrequested temperature setting, or an unmentioned oversized piece create problems ranging from rebooking to genuine danger to the vessel and other cargo.

The discipline that prevents all of these is checking the instructions against the commercial invoice and the packing list before submission: carton against carton, weight against weight, name against name, line by line. Those two commercial documents are themselves the subject of Module 6; here they matter as the source against which the instructions are verified. The economics are lopsided in favour of checking: an error caught before the documentation cut-off costs minutes, the same error caught after the transport document has been issued costs amendment fees and often days, and after the container has sailed it can cost the delivery itself. Worked Example 2 makes the comparison concrete.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: What cargo consolidation is and why forwarders consolidate
BODY:
The first half of this lesson concerned information. The second half concerns what happens physically to small shipments, and it begins with a problem of size. A standard container offers tens of cubic metres of space, as Module 3's lesson on containerised cargo explained, and most exporters in the world never produce enough in one order to fill one. An exporter with three pallets of goods faces an unpalatable choice: pay for a whole container and waste most of it, or leave the goods behind. Cargo consolidation exists to dissolve that choice.

Cargo consolidation is the process of combining multiple smaller shipments into a larger transport unit or consignment. The consolidator, the logistics service provider that combines smaller cargo consignments into larger shipments for transportation, collects many customers' part-container cargo, combines consignments bound for the same destination into one full container, and tenders that container to the ocean carrier as a single unit. The first lesson of this module introduced consolidation among the forwarder's services; here it is treated as an operation in its own right.

The benefits explain why consolidation is universal. The ocean cost of the container is shared among the customers according to the space each uses, so a small shipper pays for three pallets rather than a whole box and can reach markets that would otherwise be unaffordable. The carrier deals with one unit and one contracting party instead of dozens. Container capacity is used instead of sailing half empty. The trade-offs are equally real: consolidated cargo is handled more often, at the container freight station described below, and its journey includes building the container at origin and breaking it apart at destination. Consolidation, known in parts of Europe as groupage, is thus a bargain: the small shipper trades extra handling and a little extra time for access to container economics, and the forwarder makes the bargain work through organisation.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Full container load and less than container load
BODY:
The two ways cargo can travel in a container carry labels every forwarder uses daily. A full container load, FCL, is a container shipment generally arranged for the use of one shipper or consignee, rather than being consolidated with unrelated shipments. A less than container load shipment, LCL, occupies part of a container and is typically consolidated with other shipments. An FCL container is booked by, packed for, and sealed for a single shipper's consignment, even if that consignment leaves some space unused. An LCL shipment buys space inside a box that belongs to no single customer.

The choice between them follows the size and nature of the consignment, judged against the container capacities Module 3 introduced. A consignment large enough to occupy most of a container, or one that is heavy and dense and approaches the container's weight limit, suits FCL. A consignment of a few pallets or a few dozen cartons suits LCL, because paying for a whole box would be wasteful. The distinction, in short, is not about how full the box happens to be; it is about who has the right to the box.

The handling differs sharply. FCL cargo is usually packed at the shipper's premises and sealed, and the box is opened only at its destination: the goods are handled twice. LCL cargo is delivered to a container freight station, handled again when the container is built, handled again when it is broken open, and handled once more when each consignment is separated out. Every extra handling is a chance of damage and a cost.

That cost leads to the comparison Important Note 3 returns to: per unit of volume, LCL costs more than FCL. Sharing a box is far cheaper than chartering a whole one for a small load, but a shipper whose goods nearly fill a container is usually better off booking the box outright.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The container freight station and the cut-offs
BODY:
Consolidation needs a facility equipped to receive cargo from many shippers, check and store it, and build it into containers. The container freight station, CFS, is a facility where cargo may be received, consolidated, deconsolidated, stored, and prepared for onward transportation. At the origin end, the CFS is where the shippers' LCL cargo arrives, is weighed and measured against its instructions, and is built into the outbound container. At the destination end, another CFS performs the reverse, breaking the container and returning each consignment to its owner. FCL cargo generally bypasses the CFS: a full container goes from the shipper's premises to the container yard, the terminal area where complete boxes are stacked awaiting their vessels, and is handled as one sealed unit throughout.

Around this facility, the sailing's deadlines acquire physical meaning. The shipping cut-off, in general, is the deadline established by a carrier or terminal for completing a specified shipment requirement before departure. Two such cut-offs matter most. The cargo cut-off is the time by which the cargo must be inside the terminal or CFS, ready to be loaded. The documentation cut-off, introduced earlier in this lesson, is the time by which the shipping instructions must be finalised. A container that misses its cargo cut-off physically misses the vessel; instructions that miss the documentation cut-off leave the paperwork unable to follow. Either way the usual outcome is the rollover this module has already met: the shipment waits for the next sailing.

One further requirement guards the loaded box. The verified gross mass, VGM, is the verified total gross mass of a packed container, determined in accordance with applicable SOLAS requirements. The party that packs the container must ensure its verified gross mass is obtained and declared, and a container without a declared VGM may not be loaded. For a consolidated container, the consolidating forwarder ensures the weighing and declaration are done.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The house bill of lading and the master bill of lading
BODY:
Consolidation creates an interesting contractual situation: several shippers, one container, one carrier. The transport documents sort it out, using the two-level structure the module's first lesson introduced when it explained forwarders acting as principals.

The master bill of lading, MBL, is the bill generally issued by an ocean carrier to the party contracting with it, which may be a freight forwarder or consolidator. The house bill of lading, HBL, is the bill issued by a freight forwarder or non-vessel-operating common carrier to its customer for a shipment. In a consolidated shipment the two documents work as a pair. On the master document, the shipper is the consolidating forwarder, because the forwarder is the customer that booked the box. On each house document, the shipper is the actual exporter and the consignee is that exporter's actual buyer. The master document covers the whole container, while each house document covers one customer's consignment inside it, with that customer's own parties, cargo description, and quantities.

A common practice on the master document for a consolidated container is to describe the cargo as said to contain the various house consignments, since the carrier receives a sealed box and takes its contents on trust from the consolidator. The forwarder's agent at destination, the partner office to which the container is consigned on the master document, receives the box and performs the deconsolidation described below.

The practical consequence for an exporter is worth stating plainly: when its goods travel as LCL under a forwarder's consolidation, the transport document in its hands, which it may need to present to its bank or its buyer, is the house bill of lading, issued by the forwarder, not the ocean line's master document. Lesson 5.1's distinction between acting as agent and acting as principal is visible on the paper itself.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Consolidation step by step
BODY:
Assembling a consolidated container is a routine with many participants and no slack. A typical sequence runs as follows.

1. Bookings accumulate. Several shippers, or their forwarders, book LCL space with the consolidator for the same destination and sailing. Each delivers its shipping instructions at this stage.

2. Cargo is received at the CFS. Each consignment is delivered to the container freight station by the cargo cut-off, where it is received against its instructions: packages counted, weights checked, condition noted, and marks identified.

3. Instructions are finalised. The consolidator completes the shipping instructions for the whole container to the carrier before the documentation cut-off, drawing on each shipper's details.

4. The container is built. The CFS staff load the consignments into the container according to a stow that separates incompatible goods, respects weights and floor loads, and keeps each consignment identifiable by its marks. The box is sealed.

5. The container's mass is verified. The verified gross mass of the packed container is determined and declared to the carrier within the VGM cut-off.

6. Documents issue in both directions. The ocean carrier issues its master bill of lading to the consolidator for the sealed container; the consolidator issues a house bill of lading to each shipper for its own consignment.

7. The container sails. From this point the box travels as one unit, however many owners its contents have.

Notice where the lesson's two halves meet. The build-up at the CFS is performed from the shipping instructions, and the house documents are prepared from the same instructions. An error in one shipper's instruction does not stay in that shipper's paperwork: it enters the container's build plan, the manifest, and the document chain, while the box that carries it also carries four other customers' goods, all of which are waiting on the same cut-offs.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Deconsolidation at destination
BODY:
What was combined must be separated. Deconsolidation is the process of separating consolidated cargo into its individual shipments for onward delivery or handling. It is consolidation performed in reverse, at the far end of the voyage, and it is where the consolidated shipment finally becomes a set of ordinary individual consignments again.

When the container is discharged at the destination port, it moves to the destination CFS rather than to a consignee's door, and the sequence runs: the container is opened; each consignment is identified by its marks and separated according to the deconsolidation list prepared from the house documents; the condition and quantity of each consignment are checked and recorded as the container is broken; and each consignment is stored pending its release. The consignees, or the notifying parties named in the instructions, are informed that their goods have arrived, which is exactly the function the notifying party field exists for.

Release then follows the documents. A consignee claiming goods under a house bill of lading deals with the forwarder's destination office or agent, which releases each consignment according to the terms of its house document. The import formalities of the destination country are completed by whoever's task they are under the arrangement, and the goods are delivered. In Ghana, an importer typically works through a licensed customs clearing agent, who lodges the customs declaration in ICUMS and arranges duty payment, and then collects the goods once they are released.

Deconsolidation is the moment when small inefficiencies become visible. If the marks are incomplete, separation slows. If one consignment's papers disagree with its house document, that consignment waits, and because it is physically inside a shared process, others can wait with it. Worked Example 3 shows how.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The forwarder's responsibilities in consolidated shipments
BODY:
Consolidation multiplies the forwarder's duties by the number of customers in the box. The responsibilities that matter most are these.

1. Collecting complete and accurate instructions from each shipper, checking them against each consignment's commercial documents, and passing the container's details to the carrier before the documentation cut-off.

2. Identifying and separating the cargo. The CFS build must keep each customer's consignment distinct, marked so that deconsolidation at destination can reverse the process without guesswork.

3. Refusing incompatible neighbours. Goods that would damage or contaminate each other, strong-smelling goods beside foodstuffs, moisture-producing goods beside absorbent ones, must not share space, and dangerous goods travel only properly declared, under the controls Module 3's dangerous goods lesson described.

4. Weighing and measuring honestly. Consolidated freight is assessed on the space and mass each consignment occupies, so the CFS figures must be recorded accurately for every customer.

5. Meeting every cut-off for the box as a whole. One late shipper must not cost five customers their sailing; equally, the consolidator must protect the sailing by enforcing the deadlines it announced.

6. Issuing the documents accurately and promptly. Each house bill of lading must show its own shipper's parties and cargo exactly as checked, and the master document must be obtained from the carrier in time for the customers' needs.

7. Communicating. Each shipper is told when its cargo is received, when the container is loaded, and when the vessel sails; at destination, the agent must receive the full documentation to deconsolidate and release without delay.

The underlying duty is custody. Between the moment a shipper's pallet is received at the origin CFS and the moment the consignee's copy of the process ends at the destination CFS, the consolidating forwarder holds other people's property in mixture with strangers' property. Every responsibility above serves the same end: that the mixture can always be undone, exactly, and on time.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Three exporters, one container: who issues what to whom
BODY:
Three Ghanaian exporters have separate orders with buyers in the Netherlands. A cocoa products company in Kumasi has six pallets of processed cocoa goods, about 9 cubic metres and 6,800 kilograms. A shea butter processor in Accra has 14 pallets of cartoned products, about 11 cubic metres and 5,900 kilograms. A craft exporter in Kumasi has about 150 cartons of handicrafts, roughly 8 cubic metres and 2,300 kilograms. None could sensibly book a container alone, so a consolidating forwarder in Tema offers each LCL space in one forty-foot container sailing to Rotterdam. A forty-foot box offers roughly 67.5 cubic metres and a payload of approximately 26,700 kilograms, the figures Module 3 introduced, so the three consignments, about 28 cubic metres and 15,000 kilograms, fit comfortably. All quantities and figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: Instructions. Each exporter delivers its own shipping instructions: its parties, cargo description, marks, package counts, weights, and measurements. The consolidator checks each set against that exporter's commercial invoice and packing list, and one discrepancy, a carton count shown as 150 where the packing list reads 156, is caught and corrected before submission.

Step 2: Receipt at the CFS. The three consignments reach the container freight station in Tema by the cargo cut-off and are received against their instructions: packages counted, weights confirmed, marks identified.

Step 3: The build. CFS staff load the three consignments with a stow that keeps each company's goods identifiable, places no incompatible neighbours together, and respects the weight distribution. The container is sealed and its verified gross mass is declared.

Step 4: The documents. The consolidator finalises the container's shipping instructions before the documentation cut-off. The ocean carrier issues one master bill of lading to the consolidating forwarder, showing the forwarder as shipper and its Rotterdam agent as consignee, for the sealed container described as said to contain consolidated cargo. The forwarder then issues three house bills of lading, one to each exporter, each showing that exporter as shipper, its Dutch buyer as consignee, and the buyer's clearing agent as notifying party.

Step 5: The voyage and the separation. The container sails to Rotterdam as one unit. There the forwarder's agent sends it to a CFS, deconsolidates it, separates the three consignments by their marks, and releases each against its house document.

What does this example demonstrate? Three unrelated sellers shared one box, and the documents mirrored the physics: one master document for the single contract with the carrier, three house documents for the three contracts with the shippers.

Key lesson: consolidation shares the container, never the identity of the cargo; each shipper's paperwork remains entirely its own, which is only possible because its instructions were accurate.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: The wrong consignee: caught early, caught late
BODY:
An electronics accessories manufacturer in Shenzhen sells a consignment of 340 cartons to a buyer in Accra and sends its shipping instructions to its forwarder. In the consignee field, the clerk types the name of a similarly named company in the same commercial district of Accra, a firm the manufacturer dealt with two years earlier. The goods, the invoice, and the packing list are all correct; only the instruction is wrong. The two branches of this example show what follows. All details are illustrative.

Branch A: caught before the documentation cut-off. The forwarder's documentation clerk, checking the instructions line by line against the commercial invoice and packing list before submission, notices that the consignee on the invoice, Adinkra Trading Company Ltd, differs from the consignee in the instructions. She queries it, the manufacturer confirms the error, and the corrected instruction goes to the carrier in time. The bill of lading issues correctly, and the entire cost of the incident is a short phone call and twenty minutes of checking.

Branch B: caught after the documents issue and the container sails. The error slips through unchecked. Weeks later, in Accra, the buyer's clearing agent lodges the import declaration in ICUMS and finds that the consignee named on the transport document is not the importer. The goods cannot simply clear: the document names a party with no connection to the transaction, and the release of the cargo under a house or master document follows that document. An amendment to the transport document must be requested through the carrier, with amendment fees and administration, and identity checks on both ends while the container sits at the terminal and time-based charges begin to accrue. The delivery that should have taken days takes weeks, and the buyer's confidence takes a larger wound than its delay costs.

The difference between the branches is not luck; it is timing. The instruction error existed in both. In Branch A it was intercepted where errors are cheap, before the document was made from it. In Branch B it propagated onto the transport document, exactly as the lesson's flow predicts, and every downstream party then had to work around a paper fact that did not match reality.

What does this example demonstrate? The carrier prepares the document from the instructions, so the instruction is the cheapest place in the entire chain to catch a mistake, and the only place where catching it is free.

Key lesson: check the instructions against the invoice and the packing list before submission, every time, because after the cut-off the same check becomes an amendment and after the sailing it becomes a crisis.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: One damaged pallet, five waiting consignees
BODY:
A consolidator in Shenzhen builds a forty-foot container of LCL cargo for Tema: five unrelated consignments from five shippers, including one pallet of bagged cooking oil and, stowed above it by a CFS error, two pallets of cartoned textiles for another shipper. The container sails, arrives at Tema, and moves to the destination container freight station for deconsolidation. When the box is opened and handling begins, the cooking-oil pallet is found to have partially collapsed in transit; several bags have split, and oil has stained the textile cartons above. All details in this example are illustrative, and the account is kept general.

Deconsolidation now cannot proceed as a simple separation. The damage must be recorded before the cargo moves further: the condition of the collapsed pallet, the stained cartons, and the container's interior are noted, because every later claim, by the oil shipper against the carrier, by the textile shipper against whoever's handling caused the stow error, and by others whose goods were merely near, will rest on that record. Only after the damage is documented can the remaining, undamaged consignments be separated, and even then the affected consignments are held pending agreement on survey, assessment, and release.

Meanwhile, the other three consignees in the container, importers of machine parts, stationery, and spare hardware, have done nothing wrong. Their goods are undamaged. Yet their deliveries wait, because their cargo shares the box, the deconsolidation process, and the sequence of release with the damaged pallets. Each extra day of the box remaining unbroken and the goods unreleased adds cost for everyone and anxiety for the clearing agents waiting to lodge declarations for their importers.

What does this example demonstrate? Consolidation couples the fates of unrelated shippers at both ends of the voyage. At origin, careful stow and honest packaging prevent such events; at destination, deconsolidation is where any negligence or misfortune inside the shared box becomes everyone's delay until it is sorted out.

Key lesson: your goods share a container with strangers', so the record made when the box is opened protects every consignee, and the care taken when it was stuffed decides whether that record is ever needed.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: The shipper signs the instructions, and everyone relies on them
BODY:
The first lesson of this module established that the forwarder works from the information its customer supplies. The shipping instruction is where that general principle becomes formal. The instructions are the shipper's statement: the shipper, or its agent acting on its written behalf, signs or otherwise submits them as the authoritative account of the shipment. The forwarder relies on them, the carrier relies on them, and the documents are prepared from them.

Reliance has consequences. The forwarder that submits an accurate set of instructions has done its duty even if the goods turn out to differ, because it could only document what it was told; the shipper whose instructions were wrong owns the error and the amendment, delay, and cost that follow. This is not a technicality but the division of labour that makes the whole system workable: the party that knows the goods states the facts, and every other party may act on the statement.

The gravest case is the undeclared dangerous good, described in the instructions as something innocent. Module 3 treated dangerous goods and their controls; here the lesson is about where the declaration duty lives: it lives with the shipper, in the instructions, before anyone else can possibly know. An inaccurate description that merely inconveniences clearance is bad; one that conceals a hazard endangers crews, vessels, and other cargo, and no commercial pressure justifies it.

The professional habit is therefore to sign nothing unchecked. Compare the instructions with the commercial invoice and the packing list, line by line, before submission. The signature is not a formality; it is a statement that the facts are true.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Your goods share a box with strangers: packing and marking matter
BODY:
In an FCL container, the shipper answers to itself for how the cargo is packed and stowed. In an LCL shipment, that private responsibility becomes a shared one, and the strangers sharing the box include everyone whose goods touch yours at the container freight station and inside the container.

Three practical consequences follow. First, packaging must stand independent handling. A consolidated consignment is received, stacked, and built into the container by people who do not know it, alongside other people's goods; a pallet that would have survived gentle treatment in its owner's private container may collapse under a mixed stow, and when it does, the damage reaches downwards and outwards onto neighbours, as the third worked example showed. Second, marking must be complete on every piece. Module 3 treated cargo marking as the information on the package; in consolidation it becomes the consignment's identity. At deconsolidation, the marks are how five shippers' goods are returned to five owners, and an unmarked or mismarked piece is a piece nobody can deliver. Third, the consignment must declare what it is. Goods with strong odours, moisture, or hazards must be honestly described in the instructions so the consolidator keeps them away from vulnerable neighbours, and dangerous goods must be declared without exception.

The habit to carry forward is that in consolidated cargo, consideration for strangers is not sentiment but risk management. The care you take with your packing and marking is the care your goods receive from everyone else's.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: LCL is convenient, but it is not cheap per cubic metre
BODY:
A misconception worth dismantling before it costs someone money: consolidation is not a discount scheme, and LCL freight is not cheaper freight. Per unit of volume, LCL consistently costs more than FCL, and the reasons are structural rather than opportunistic.

An FCL rate prices one container, carried as a single sealed unit from the shipper's chosen handover to the destination terminal. The line handles a box; nothing inside it is anyone's business. An LCL rate, by contrast, must recover the cost of everything the consolidator does that the line never sees: collecting or receiving small lots from many shippers, handling each consignment at the origin CFS, weighing and measuring it, building the container, breaking it at the destination CFS, separating and storing the individual consignments, and issuing a separate house document for every customer. Each of those steps is real work with real costs, and because an LCL consignment enjoys them individually, it pays for them individually. The CFS handling of LCL cargo is even a recognised charge line in ocean freight practice.

The conclusion is not that LCL is a bad product; it is that each product suits a different size of shipment. For the exporter with three pallets, paying the LCL rate for the space used is far better than paying for a container that is mostly air. For the exporter whose goods nearly fill the box, booking FCL removes the per-unit premium, the extra handling, and the sharing of fate with strangers, and the goods travel sealed from their own premises. The forwarder's advice, when a customer's volumes grow, is to revisit the choice honestly: LCL is the small shipper's bargain, not a permanent arrangement.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
An exporter in Takoradi prepares shipping instructions for 90 cartons of cocoa butter with a total weight of 1,200 kilograms, but the packing list it supplied shows 96 cartons at 1,350 kilograms. Nobody checks, the instructions go to the carrier, and the bill of lading is issued from them.

Which statement best describes the consequence?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. There is no consequence, because the carrier weighs every container before loading and automatically corrects paperwork that disagrees with the scales.
2. The carrier prepares the transport document from the shipping instructions, so the wrong carton count and weight are copied onto the bill of lading, where they disagree with the commercial documents and must be corrected afterwards at cost, with delays.
3. The packing list governs the shipment, so the bill of lading shows the correct figures regardless of what the shipping instructions said.
4. The error is harmless, because a transport document may be corrected at any time, even after the goods are delivered, without fees or consequences.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The lesson's central mechanism is at work: the carrier does not investigate the goods, it reads the shipping instructions and prints the transport document from them. Wrong figures in the instructions therefore become wrong figures on the bill of lading, and the document then disagrees with the commercial invoice and packing list, a discrepancy that surfaces at payment or clearance and must be repaired by amendment, with fees, administration, and delay.

Answer 1 fails because weighing a container verifies its gross mass, not the number of packages described on paper; the verified gross mass is declared by the shipper's side, not discovered by the carrier, and a scale cannot correct a carton count. Answer 3 fails because no document automatically overrides another: the shipping instructions are the source from which the document is made. Answer 4 fails because amendments after issue attract cost and administration, and after arrival they are often impossible.

Takeaway: check the instructions against the invoice and the packing list before the documentation cut-off; that check is free, and it is the only point at which the error is still cheap.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
A consolidating forwarder in Tema combines less-than-container-load cargo from four unrelated exporters into one forty-foot container sailing to Rotterdam, and the ocean carrier carries the box.

Which statement best describes the transport documents for this movement?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The four exporters share one combined document, because their goods travel in the same container and therefore form a single consignment.
2. The ocean carrier issues a separate master bill of lading to each exporter, because each exporter's goods are carried on the vessel.
3. The forwarder issues a house bill of lading to each exporter for its own consignment, and the ocean carrier issues one master bill of lading to the forwarder for the consolidated container.
4. The forwarder receives a house bill of lading from the carrier and issues master bills of lading to the four exporters.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The document structure mirrors the contract structure. Each exporter contracts with the consolidating forwarder, which answers for the movement as principal, so the forwarder issues each of the four a house bill of lading showing that exporter as shipper and that exporter's own buyer as consignee. The forwarder in turn contracts with the ocean line for the whole box, so the carrier issues exactly one master bill of lading, to the forwarder, showing the forwarder as its customer.

Answer 1 fails because sharing a box never merges the consignments: each shipper needs its own receipt and document for its own buyer, on its own terms. Answer 2 fails because the carrier's customer is the forwarder, not the four exporters; the line does not contract with them. Answer 4 reverses the two documents: the carrier issues the master, and the forwarder issues the house.

Takeaway: one container, one master document held by the consolidator, and one house document per shipper; the paper always follows the contracts.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A business in Accra regularly exports enough light, bulky goods to fill most of a forty-foot container, but it keeps sending everything as LCL because booking a full container sounds more expensive. Its forwarder advises switching to FCL for these shipments.

Which option best explains the forwarder's advice?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. FCL suits this shipper because one container rate for a nearly full box avoids the LCL premium per unit of volume, the repeated handling at two container freight stations, and the shared fate that comes from travelling with strangers' cargo.
2. FCL is advisable because LCL cargo travels by slower vessels than FCL cargo on the same routes.
3. The advice follows from FCL being available only to large importers and shipping lines, never to ordinary exporters.
4. The advice follows from LCL shipments being banned on most trade routes because consolidated cargo is unsafe.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

LCL rates cost more per unit of volume than FCL because they must recover everything consolidation adds: receiving many shippers' lots, container freight station handling at both ends, weighing and measuring, building and breaking the container, and issuing a separate house document per customer. A shipper whose goods nearly fill the box pays that premium on almost a full container's worth of cargo, while also accepting extra handling and the coupling of its delivery to strangers' goods, as the damaged-pallet example showed. Booking FCL prices the box as one unit, keeps the goods sealed from its own premises, and removes the premium.

Answer 2 fails because consolidation adds handling steps, not slower vessels; LCL and FCL travel on the same liner services. Answer 3 invents a restriction that does not exist: FCL is available to any shipper that books a box. Answer 4 is false; consolidation is a safe, universal practice when properly handled.

Takeaway: LCL is the small shipper's bargain, and FCL is the nearly-full shipper's bargain; the right product depends on how much of the box you really use.
