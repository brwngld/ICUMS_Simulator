=== ASSESSMENT Q1 | tests lesson 4.1 ===
QUESTION:
Two containers are discharged from the same vessel at Tema Port on the same day. One is declared for home consumption in Ghana; the other is declared under transit to a buyer in Niger. The two consignments travelled in the same way, arrived at the same berth, and are of similar value.

Which factor decides that the two consignments receive different customs treatment in Ghana?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The value of the goods: only consignments above a certain value may move as transit, while lower-value goods must always be entered for home consumption.
2. The port of arrival: goods that physically land at a Ghanaian port are imports into Ghana, so both containers should in principle be treated alike.
3. The customs procedure each consignment is declared under: the first is entered for home consumption and joins Ghana's market, while the second crosses under transit arrangements without being entered for consumption.
4. The nationality of the carrier: goods carried by a Ghanaian haulier become imports, while goods carried by a foreign haulier remain transit goods.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The lesson's central distinction is that goods arriving at a country's port are not automatically that country's imports; the test is the customs procedure the goods are declared under, not the geography. The first container is entered for home consumption: duties and taxes are assessed on it, and once released it joins the local market as an import. The second is declared under a transit arrangement: it is never entered for consumption in Ghana, moves sealed and documented under customs control along the corridor, and its duties are secured by a guarantee and collected at the destination.

Answer 2 is the exact misconception the lesson dismantles: physical arrival at a port does not make goods imports, otherwise every landlocked neighbour's cargo would be taxed twice and the coastal ports would be useless to it. Answer 1 invents a value threshold; nothing in the lesson treats value as the deciding factor. Answer 4 invents a carrier-nationality test; the haulier's identity has no bearing on the procedure the goods are declared for.

The takeaway: the declaration decides the treatment, so the trade type of every movement must be fixed, and declared, before the goods arrive.

=== ASSESSMENT Q2 | tests lesson 4.1 ===
QUESTION:
A container of machinery arrives at Tema Port declared under transit to Ouagadougou. Customs seals it, the single transit document is lodged, and the container is released northward along the corridor without any import duty being collected. A colleague asks why Ghana collects no duty on goods merely passing through its port, and what would happen if such a container disappeared into the local market.

Which option best answers the colleague?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The duty is not waived but suspended: it is collected where the goods will actually be consumed, and a guarantee covers the duties at risk, so if the container disappeared into the local market the passing country's customs could recover the duty from the guarantor.
2. Duties are genuinely waived on transit goods; Ghana is compensated by the port and haulage fees it earns, and if the container disappeared the shipping line would be billed for the lost duty.
3. Transit goods pay a reduced rate of duty at the border where they enter the transit country, with the balance collected at the destination.
4. No duty is owed on transit goods anywhere: because the goods have already crossed one border, the destination country must release them without assessing import duties.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

Transit exists to solve a geography problem. Several of Ghana's neighbours, such as Burkina Faso, Mali, and Niger, have no seaport, so their overseas goods must arrive at a coastal port and cross one or more neighbouring countries. If the passing country assessed import duties, the goods would be charged twice, once in transit and again at the real destination, and the coastal ports would be useless to landlocked traders. The answer is duty suspension: the goods cross under customs control without being entered for consumption, and the duties they owe are collected where they will actually be consumed. The movement travels with four ingredients: a single transit document (in West Africa, the ISRT triptyque), a customs seal, a guarantee covering the duties at risk, and prescribed routes and time limits. The guarantee answers the colleague's second question: if the goods disappear into the market of a passing country, its customs can recover the duty from the guarantor, and the guarantee is released only when the transit is properly closed at destination.

Answer 2 is wrong because the fees Ghana earns do not replace the duty, and the shipping line is not the party standing behind the guarantee. Answer 3 invents a border duty that never arises in the transit country. Answer 4 erases the duty altogether; it is owed, and the destination country assesses and collects it on import.

The takeaway: transit suspends duty, it never cancels it, and the guarantee is what keeps the suspension safe.

=== ASSESSMENT Q3 | tests lesson 4.1 ===
QUESTION:
A Ghanaian importer buys a consignment of rice grown and milled in Thailand. The rice is carried first to Singapore, where it is shifted to another vessel, and is then shipped onward to Tema, where it will be entered for home consumption.

Which statement correctly identifies the country of origin and its significance?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Singapore, because the country from which goods are last shipped before import is treated as their origin, so the declaration and the certificate of origin should name Singapore.
2. Thailand, because origin is the country where the goods were grown, produced, or substantially transformed, not where they were shipped from; duties, trade statistics, and preferential treatment are decided against the origin, which the certificate of origin attests.
3. Ghana, because goods take the origin of the country where they are entered for consumption once the import declaration is lodged.
4. Either Thailand or Singapore, because origin is a labelling choice the importer may make to suit whichever document is easiest to obtain.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The country of origin is the country in which goods are considered to have originated under applicable rules of origin: where they were grown, produced, or substantially transformed. The rice was grown and milled in Thailand, so its origin remains Thailand even though it was transhipped in Singapore, exactly as the lesson's own illustration states. Origin matters because duties, trade statistics, and preferential treatment are all decided against it, and the certificate of origin in the shipment file is the document that attests it.

Answer 1 mistakes the shipment point for the origin; the port a consignment is forwarded from says nothing about where the goods were produced. Answer 3 confuses origin with destination: the country of destination is where the goods are intended to be delivered and entered, and entering them there does not rewrite where they came from. Answer 4 treats origin as a matter of convenience, but origin is determined under the applicable rules of origin, not chosen by the importer; a wrong origin on a certificate can change the duty, which is why origin documents carry financial weight.

The takeaway: origin follows production, not the shipping route, and every document in the file must state it consistently.

=== ASSESSMENT Q4 | tests lesson 4.2 ===
QUESTION:
A buyer in Accra sends an enquiry to a machinery supplier abroad and receives a quotation followed by a document that sets out the supplier's proposed goods, prices, and transaction terms. The buyer wants to use this document to confirm the deal internally, arrange funding and permits, and check every proposed condition before its own organisation is committed to buy.

Which statement best describes the document's role?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. It is the pro forma invoice: a preliminary document outlining the proposed goods, prices, and transaction terms before any final sale or shipment, which commits neither party; the buyer's commitment normally comes later, with the purchase order raised after internal approval.
2. It is the commercial invoice, and its issue makes the sale binding and starts the payment period running.
3. It is the purchase order, the seller's document that commits the buyer to buy and obliges immediate shipment.
4. It is a draft contract of carriage, which the freight forwarder must confirm before any goods can be booked onto a vessel.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

An international transaction hardens step by step: enquiry, quotation, then the pro forma invoice, a preliminary document outlining the proposed goods, prices, and transaction terms before any final sale or shipment. It is a quotation in invoice form, and its purposes match the buyer's needs in the scenario exactly: confirming the deal internally, arranging funds or permits, and checking that every proposed condition is acceptable. It commits neither party. The buyer's commitment normally arrives later, with the purchase order raised only after internal approval, because the purchase order commits the organisation to buy. The transaction is established when the seller confirms that order or the parties sign a fuller contract.

Answer 2 confuses the pro forma with the commercial invoice, which states the goods, quantities, prices, and terms of a concluded sale and travels with the shipment; it does not create the deal. Answer 3 reverses the parties: the purchase order is the buyer's document, issued by the buyer, which is precisely why it waits for the buyer's internal approval. Answer 4 belongs to the wrong document family altogether; carriage documents govern the carrier's work, not the formation of the sale.

The takeaway: the transaction exists commercially before the goods move — by the time a container is stuffed, everything about the sale should already be agreed in writing.

=== ASSESSMENT Q5 | tests lesson 4.2 ===
QUESTION:
A trader in Ghana buys packaged rice from a mill in a country outside ECOWAS and only repacks it into smaller bags at a warehouse in Tema before sending it to a buyer in a neighbouring ECOWAS member state. The trader asks for the regional preferential certificate of origin for the consignment, arguing that the goods left Ghana for a member state under a regional trade arrangement, so they must qualify.

Which statement best evaluates the trader's position?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The trader is right: goods dispatched from an ECOWAS member state to a buyer in another member state qualify for the scheme's preferential treatment, whatever their processing history.
2. The trader is right, provided the repacking in Tema was done carefully and the new bags are properly labelled.
3. The trader is wrong: preferential qualification requires the goods to be wholly obtained in, or substantially transformed in, the claiming country; goods produced elsewhere and merely repacked or passed through do not qualify, and issuing a preferential certificate for them would be a false declaration.
4. The trader is wrong, because agricultural products such as rice can never qualify under a regional trade arrangement, whatever their processing history.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

An agreement between countries does not exempt a shipment by itself. Three requirements stand between the agreement on paper and a reduced duty at the border: the agreement must cover the goods; the goods must genuinely qualify under its rules of origin; and the claim must be evidenced by the required preferential certificate of origin, issued through the proper channel. The origin rules recognise two core tests: wholly obtained goods, and goods substantially transformed in the claiming country. The lesson is explicit that goods produced elsewhere and merely repacked or passed through do not qualify, however they are described. Repacking foreign rice in Tema is not substantial transformation, so the goods do not qualify, and the certificate the trader wants cannot properly be issued. The integrity edge applies in full: a certificate of origin must reflect genuine qualification, and obtaining one for goods that do not qualify is a false declaration with consequences that outlast any single shipment.

Answer 1 treats dispatch from a member state as qualification, but the origin rules, not the dispatch point, decide. Answer 2 invents a standard of careful repacking; the obstacle is the processing, not the packing. Answer 4 invents a product exclusion that does not exist; rice processed substantially within a member state can qualify — this rice simply did not.

The takeaway: the agreement opens the door, the goods must fit through it, and the certificate is the key.

=== ASSESSMENT Q6 | tests lesson 4.2 ===
QUESTION:
A single international shipment gives rise to three questions: when the buyer must pay the seller; whether the goods qualify for preferential duty treatment at the border under a trade agreement between the two countries; and which terms govern the movement of the goods as agreed between the shipper and the carrier.

Which option correctly assigns each question to the agreement that answers it?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Payment belongs to the sales contract; preferential duty belongs to the trade agreements between the governments, applied through the goods' origin and documents; and the carriage terms belong to the contract of carriage between the shipper and the carrier.
2. Payment belongs to the trade agreements between the governments; preferential duty belongs to the sales contract; and the carriage terms belong to the sales contract as well, since the seller prepares every document in the file.
3. Payment belongs to the contract of carriage; preferential duty belongs to the trade agreements; and the carriage terms are set by the destination country's customs authorities.
4. Payment belongs to the sales contract; preferential duty belongs to the contract of carriage; and the carriage terms are fixed by the trade agreements, because governments regulate carriers.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

A single shipment rests on several agreements at once, and the lesson's final note teaches the trader to hear which agreement a question belongs to. The sales contract, binding only the buyer and the seller who signed it, governs the commercial deal: goods, price, delivery terms, payment terms, and documents — so when the buyer must pay lives there. The trade agreements between governments govern the conditions at the border: whether the goods may receive preferential duty treatment, under what origin rules and what documentation — neither the buyer nor the seller signed them, and they promise nothing about anyone's payment. The contract of carriage, between the shipper and the carrier, governs the movement itself: what is carried, to which port or place, and under what terms — the carrier is not a party to the sales contract and does not care when the buyer pays.

Answer 2 assigns payment to the layer that never touches it and hands the sales contract the border's job. Answer 3 makes the carrier the creditor of the price and gives carriage terms to customs, which is a party to no commercial agreement at all. Answer 4 gives preferential duty to the carriage contract and lets governments fix private carriage terms.

The takeaway: payment lives in the sales contract, the border lives in the governments' agreements, and the movement lives in the contract of carriage.

=== ASSESSMENT Q7 | tests lesson 4.3 ===
QUESTION:
A buyer in Hamburg purchases a consignment of cocoa products on CIF Hamburg, Incoterms 2020 terms from a seller in Tema. The seller clears the goods for export, contracts and pays the ocean freight to Hamburg, and obtains the specified minimum insurance for the buyer's risk. While the container is still standing in the terminal at Tema, before it is loaded on board the vessel, it is damaged by fire.

Which statement best describes who bears the loss?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The buyer bears the loss, because the seller has paid the freight to Hamburg and arranged the insurance, so the goods were the buyer's concern from the moment the sale was agreed.
2. The buyer bears the loss, because under a CIF sale risk passes when the container is handed to the freight forwarder at the terminal.
3. The insurance company bears the loss, because the seller's insurance obligation under CIF covers the goods from the seller's factory gate onwards.
4. The seller bears the loss, because under CIF risk passes only when the goods are on board the vessel at the port of shipment; damage in the terminal before loading is still at the seller's risk, even though the seller's freight obligation runs to Hamburg.
CORRECT: 4
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 4.

Under CIF the seller contracts and pays the freight to the named destination port and must obtain the specified minimum insurance for the buyer's risk, but the risk itself passes when the goods are on board the vessel at the port of shipment — Tema, in this scenario. While the container stands in the terminal before loading, it is still at the seller's risk, whatever the seller's freight obligation says. This is the C-rule principle read from its other side: the cost line and the risk line are two separate lines, and under CFR and CIF they do not sit together. Paying for carriage to Hamburg does not put the destination-side risk on the seller, and equally it does not put the pre-loading risk on the buyer.

Answer 1 states the classic trap in full: freight paid to destination is a cost obligation, not an early transfer of risk. Answer 2 invents a transfer point; handover to the first carrier is where risk passes under CPT and CIP, the any-mode counterparts of CFR and CIF, not under CIF itself. Answer 3 misreads the insurance: the CIF insurance duty exists for the buyer's risk from shipment onwards; it does not extend back over the seller's pre-loading risk, and it does not decide whose loss it is — the rule does.

The takeaway: quote the C rule with its edition, then ask two questions — where do costs run, and where does risk pass.

=== ASSESSMENT Q8 | tests lesson 4.3 ===
QUESTION:
An exporter of shea butter sells a container load to a buyer in Europe. The container will be stuffed and handed over to the buyer's nominated carrier at a terminal in Tema before the vessel arrives, the normal pattern for containerised shipments. A colleague proposes the clause FOB Tema, Incoterms 2020, reasoning that the goods are travelling by sea.

Which option best evaluates the colleague's proposal?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The proposal is correct, because FOB is the standard rule for sea carriage and the destination is a European port.
2. The proposal is flawed: FOB requires delivery on board the vessel at the port of shipment, while containerised cargo handed to the carrier at a terminal before the vessel arrives matches FCA, under which the seller delivers the goods, cleared for export, to the buyer's nominated carrier at the named place.
3. The proposal is flawed: the parties should use EXW, because handing goods over at a terminal is the same as making them available at the seller's premises.
4. The proposal is flawed only because the named place is incomplete; FOB would be correct once the destination port in Europe is named instead of Tema.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The eleven Incoterms 2020 rules divide into two families: four rules intended only for sea and inland waterway transport (FAS, FOB, CFR, CIF) and seven suitable for any mode (EXW, FCA, CPT, CIP, DAP, DPU, DDP). But choosing the right rule asks more than whether the goods will touch the sea. FOB requires delivery on board the vessel at the port of shipment, and containerised cargo is normally handed to the carrier at a terminal before the vessel arrives. The lesson's drafting note is direct: choosing FOB for a container handed over at a terminal writes an obligation into the contract that does not match the physical operation. FCA describes exactly the delivery that actually happens: the seller delivers the goods, cleared for export, to the carrier nominated by the buyer at the named place, and the buyer arranges and pays the main carriage.

Answer 1 treats the sea leg as decisive, but the rule must match the manner of delivery, not merely the destination. Answer 3 reaches for the wrong end of the scale: EXW would leave collection, loading, and export clearance to the buyer from the seller's premises, a very different allocation. Answer 4 misreads the named place; FOB's named place is the port of shipment, and naming a destination port does not repair the mismatch.

The takeaway: identify where and how the goods will actually be delivered, choose from the family that describes that delivery, and always cite the edition.

=== ASSESSMENT Q9 | tests lesson 4.3 ===
QUESTION:
An engineering company in Accra buys one industrial machine from a manufacturer in Mumbai on EXW Mumbai (the seller's works), Incoterms 2020 terms. After signing, the buyer discovers that it must arrange collection from the factory, inland transport across India, and the export formalities in India before the machine can leave.

Which option best evaluates this position?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The parties have made an error that the law will correct: under every Incoterms rule the seller must clear the goods for export, so the seller must perform the Indian export formalities after all.
2. The outcome follows from EXW: export clearance is the buyer's obligation under this one rule — its great practical weakness, because the buyer must complete export formalities in a country where it does not operate — which is why plain EXW is widely regarded as impractical for international sales and a rule such as FCA usually fits better.
3. The buyer can avoid the export formalities by instructing a freight forwarder to declare the machine as the forwarder's own goods.
4. EXW passes the cost of export clearance to the buyer, but the export clearance itself remains the seller's obligation; only the paperwork's expense is the buyer's.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

EXW is the seller's minimum: the seller produces, packs, and places the goods at the buyer's disposal at its premises, and from that point the buyer assumes most transportation responsibilities and risks. Export clearance is the buyer's obligation under EXW — the only one of the eleven rules under which the seller does not clear the goods for export — so the buyer must complete export formalities in a country where it does not operate. That allocation is exactly what the rule provides, and it is why the lesson calls export clearance the rule's great practical weakness and regards plain EXW as impractical for most international sales. Where the buyer cannot take on export clearance, a rule such as FCA usually fits better: the seller delivers the goods, cleared for export, to the carrier the buyer nominates, while the buyer keeps control of the main carriage.

Answer 1 states the general pattern and forgets its exception; export clearance falls to the seller under every rule except EXW, and no law rewrites the rule the parties chose. Answer 3 invents an unlawful workaround; a declaration must describe the true position. Answer 4 splits an obligation that cannot be split: under EXW the export formalities, not merely their cost, belong to the buyer.

The takeaway: choose the rule whose obligations a party can actually perform, and read the three letters carefully before signing.

=== ASSESSMENT Q10 | tests lesson 4.4 ===
QUESTION:
A first-time exporter in Takoradi agrees a sale with an overseas buyer who refuses to pay in advance. The exporter will not trade on open account with a stranger, so the parties agree a documentary collection with documents against payment (D/P), the document set including an original ocean bill of lading.

Which statement best describes what this arrangement gives the exporter?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. A commitment from the collecting bank to pay the invoice if the buyer does not, because a bank that accepts a collection instruction steps into the buyer's place.
2. Payment on shipment, because the exporter's bank pays against the bill of lading and recovers the funds from the buyer's bank afterwards.
3. Transfer of the seller's ownership in the goods to the banks, which hold the cargo as the seller's agents until payment arrives.
4. Control of the documents, and through them of the goods, until the buyer pays — the original ocean bill of lading being a document of title — but no promise from any bank to pay, since banks in a collection handle documents without generally guaranteeing payment.
CORRECT: 4
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 4.

A documentary collection works through document control. The exporter ships the goods and hands the documents — commercial invoice, transport document, certificates — to its bank with a collection instruction; the documents travel to a bank in the buyer's country; and the buyer must pay before the bank releases them. Because the original ocean bill of lading is a document of title, whoever holds it controls the right to claim delivery: while the documents sit with the banks, the buyer cannot collect the cargo. That is what D/P gives the exporter — more protection than open account. What it does not give is a promise: the banks handle the documents without generally guaranteeing payment, acting, in the lesson's image, as a postal service for documents. If the buyer refuses to pay, the banks will not pay from their own funds, and the exporter may face cargo sitting at a distant destination.

Answer 1 crosses the clean dividing line the lesson draws: only under a letter of credit does a bank commit, and then against complying documents, never by stepping into the buyer's place. Answer 2 invents financing the banks do not provide; the exporter's bank remits what it receives. Answer 3 confuses control of delivery with ownership, which the contract and the law governing it decide.

The takeaway: a collection buys supervision and document control, not payment security.

=== ASSESSMENT Q11 | tests lesson 4.4 ===
QUESTION:
An exporter ships goods under a documentary collection with documents against acceptance (D/A), the time draft payable 60 days after sight. The buyer accepts the draft, takes the documents from the bank, collects the goods from the port, and sells them. At maturity the buyer does not pay. The exporter asks the banks in the collection chain to pay the invoice or to recover the goods.

Which statement best describes the exporter's position?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The banks must pay the invoice, because releasing the documents against the accepted draft transferred the buyer's debt to the collecting bank.
2. The acceptance of the draft was itself payment, so the exporter has no remaining claim against the buyer.
3. The banks have no payment obligation: under a collection they handle documents without generally guaranteeing payment, the exporter's security was the buyer's promise to pay at maturity, and with the goods already sold the exporter is left pursuing an unpaid claim against the buyer.
4. The banks must recover the goods, because the buyer's acceptance of a draft gives the exporter a claim on the cargo until the draft is paid at maturity.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

Under D/A the documents are released against acceptance of a time draft — the buyer's formal undertaking to pay at maturity. Acceptance is a promise, not money, and the buyer can take the documents, clear the goods, and even sell them long before maturity. That is precisely the difference the lesson draws between D/P (pay to get the documents: cash before cargo) and D/A (sign a promise to get the documents: the goods go on a promise), which is why D/A is always riskier for the exporter. Here the promise failed. Under a collection the banks handle documents according to the collection instruction without generally guaranteeing payment; they never step into the buyer's shoes, and they still have not promised anything after a default at maturity. The exporter's security was the buyer's signature on the draft, and with the cargo sold the realistic course is to pursue the buyer for the unpaid claim.

Answer 1 assigns the banks a commitment they never gave; that kind of commitment exists only under a letter of credit. Answer 2 mistook acceptance for settlement; if acceptance were payment, there would be nothing for the draft to promise at maturity. Answer 4 invents a claim on the cargo that no arrangement in the lesson provides.

The takeaway: D/P keeps the goods as leverage; D/A gives them away on a promise, and the exporter carries the risk that the promise is not kept.

=== ASSESSMENT Q12 | tests lesson 4.4 ===
QUESTION:
An exporter ships goods of excellent quality under a letter of credit and presents its documents within the credit's validity. During examination, the banks find that the certificate of origin names the buyer by its trading name, while the credit states the buyer's full registered name. The exporter protests that the goods themselves are perfect and demands immediate payment.

Which statement best explains the position?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The banks must pay, because the quality of the goods is what ultimately secures payment under a letter of credit.
2. The banks must pay, because a difference between a trading name and a registered name is commercially minor and can never affect payment.
3. The banks are not obliged to pay: they examine the documents on their face against the terms of the credit, a discrepancy frees them from the obligation to honour, and the exporter's practical remedy is to correct the document and re-present while the credit remains valid.
4. The banks must pay and recover the amount from the buyer afterwards, because the buyer received the goods and the letter of credit ultimately charges the buyer for whatever is shipped.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

A letter of credit is an undertaking to honour a complying presentation of documents. The banks examine the documents on their face — on what the documents themselves state — within a maximum of five banking days, and if the presentation does not comply with the credit, the bank is not obliged to pay; it must give a single notice of refusal listing all the discrepancies. A discrepancy is any failure of the documents to match the credit, and the lesson's own example is exactly this one: a certificate naming a party differently from the way the credit names it. The principle runs in both directions: banks deal in documents, not goods, so perfect goods with flawed papers are as far from payment as flawed goods with perfect papers. The practical remedy is the exporter's: cure the discrepancy and re-present while the credit remains valid.

Answer 1 inverts the whole instrument: the quality of the goods secures nothing under a credit, just as the credit guarantees nothing about quality. Answer 2 asks the banks to weigh commercial significance, but compliance is judged on the documents against the credit's wording, not on materiality. Answer 4 invents an arrangement that contradicts the credit's independence from the sales contract.

The takeaway: document accuracy is the price of the undertaking — read the credit on arrival and produce every document to match its exact wording.

- Q1-Q3 — lesson 4.1 (Importing, Exporting, and Transit Trade): the Important Note "Transit is not import" (the test is the customs procedure the goods are declared under, not the geography; consequences for charges, control, and documents); the transit blocks (why duties are suspended — the double-charging problem for landlocked neighbours — and the four ingredients: single transit document, seal, guarantee, prescribed routes and time limits; the guarantee released when the transit is closed at destination); and the definition of country of origin (grown, produced, or substantially transformed, not where shipped from), using the lesson's own Thailand-via-Singapore rice illustration, plus the statement that duties, trade statistics, and preferential treatment are decided against origin and that a wrong origin on a certificate can change the duty.
- Q4-Q6 — lesson 4.2 (Buyers, Sellers, and International Trade Agreements): the transaction-establishment sequence (enquiry, quotation, pro forma invoice as a quotation in invoice form used to confirm the deal internally and arrange funds or permits, purchase order raised only after internal approval, then agreement and performance); the Important Note "An agreement between countries does not exempt a shipment by itself" (three requirements; goods produced elsewhere and merely repacked or passed through do not qualify; wholly obtained and substantial transformation as the core origin tests; the integrity rule that a certificate must reflect genuine qualification and a false declaration carries consequences); and the Important Note "One shipment, several agreements" (sales contract, contract of carriage, and government trade agreements each answering different questions).
- Q7-Q9 — lesson 4.3 (Incoterms and Allocation of Responsibilities): the Important Note "Under the C rules, risk passes at shipment" and the CFR/CIF risk-transfer point (goods on board at the port of shipment), with CPT/CIP's first-carrier handover as the contrast; the two families (FAS, FOB, CFR, CIF maritime-only; EXW, FCA, CPT, CIP, DAP, DPU, DDP any mode) and the Important Note "Match the rule to the journey" (FOB wrongly used for containerised cargo handed over at a terminal, FCA correct; cite the edition); and the EXW block (export clearance as the buyer's obligation under the one rule that excepts it, plain EXW impractical for international sales, FCA as the usual better fit).
- Q10-Q12 — lesson 4.4 (International Trade Payment Methods): the documentary-collection blocks (collection instruction, banks as a postal service for documents handling documents without generally guaranteeing payment; D/P versus D/A; the ocean bill of lading as document of title giving document control; acceptance of a time draft as a promise to pay at maturity, D/A riskier than D/P); and the letter-of-credit blocks and Important Note "A letter of credit is not a guarantee" (undertaking to honour a complying presentation; banks deal in documents, not goods; examination on their face within five banking days; single notice of refusal listing all discrepancies; the certificate-name discrepancy drawn from the lesson's worked example; cure and re-present while the credit remains valid).
- All companies, places, quantities, and scenarios in the questions are illustrative teaching constructions consistent with the lessons; no real organisation is named or described. No duty rates appear anywhere in the assessment; the ECOWAS trade liberalisation arrangement and preferential certificates are referenced only as the lessons describe them.
