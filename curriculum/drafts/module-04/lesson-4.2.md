=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The buyer and the seller in international trade
BODY:
Every international sale has two parties at its centre. The buyer is a person or organisation that purchases goods or services from another party. The seller is a person or organisation that supplies goods or services in exchange for an agreed consideration, normally a price to be paid. Using the language of the previous lesson on importing and exporting, the seller is typically the exporter located in the country of origin, and the buyer is typically the importer located in the country of destination. The same two roles sit behind every trade, whether the goods cross one border or five.

The two parties want different things from the same transaction.

1. What the seller wants
The seller wants payment security: to be paid the agreed price, in the agreed currency, at the agreed time, without having to chase the money across a border. Before shipping, the seller also wants confidence that the order is genuine and clearly defined.

2. What the buyer wants
The buyer wants goods certainty: the right goods, in the agreed quantity and condition, arriving at the agreed place and time, at the agreed price. Paying for a shipment that arrives late, incomplete or wrong is the buyer's nightmare.

3. What each party is responsible for
The seller's responsibilities normally include supplying goods that conform to the contract in description, quality, quantity and packaging, preparing the goods for international transport, carrying out whatever export arrangements the agreement allocates to it, and providing the agreed documents. The buyer's responsibilities normally include paying according to the agreed payment terms, taking delivery of the goods, and carrying out whatever import arrangements the agreement allocates to it.

Notice that phrase: whatever the agreement allocates. In international trade, the split of tasks between buyer and seller is agreed, not automatic. Who arranges the ocean carriage, who insures, who clears the goods through customs at either end — all of this is settled in the contract between the two parties. The next lesson on Incoterms examines precisely how that allocation is expressed.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The gap between trading partners
BODY:
In a domestic sale, the distance between buyer and seller is small enough to manage with ordinary trust. A shopkeeper in Takoradi buying goods from a distributor in Accra can visit the warehouse, meet the same people again, pay in the local currency after delivery, and take a disagreement to a lawyer operating under one familiar legal system.

International trade removes every one of those comforts. Four gaps open up between the parties.

1. Distance
Goods may travel for days or weeks, pass through ports and terminals, and change hands several times. Neither party can simply walk next door to check on the cargo.

2. Different legal systems
If a dispute arises, enforcing a claim in another country's courts is slow, expensive and uncertain. The buyer and the seller grew up under different rules, and neither can assume the other's law works in their favour.

3. Different currencies
The parties must agree which currency the price is paid in, and each of them faces the risk that the exchange rate moves between signing the contract and settling the payment.

4. Limited trust
The parties may never meet. The buyer cannot inspect the goods in the seller's warehouse; the seller knows nothing of the buyer's business except what documents and references show.

These gaps produce two mirrored fears. The seller fears shipping goods and never being paid. The buyer fears paying and receiving the wrong goods, late goods, or nothing at all. The whole machinery of international trade — the sales contract, the document system you met in the lesson on logistics documentation, the payment arrangements, and the agreements between countries — exists to bridge this gap so that two strangers on different continents can trade with confidence.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: How an international transaction is established
BODY:
An international sale rarely begins with a signature. It begins with a conversation between organisations and hardens step by step into a binding agreement. A typical sequence runs as follows.

1. Enquiry and quotation
The buyer identifies a need and sends an enquiry to a potential supplier, asking whether the goods are available, at what price, and on what conditions. The seller replies with a quotation setting out its proposed price and terms.

2. The pro forma invoice
The seller commonly issues a pro forma invoice, the preliminary document the lesson on logistics documentation introduced as a quotation in invoice form. It outlines the proposed goods, prices and transaction terms before any final sale or shipment. The buyer uses it to confirm the deal internally, arrange funds or permits, and check that every proposed condition is acceptable.

3. The purchase order
The buyer then issues a purchase order, or PO, a document specifying the goods or services requested and the proposed purchase terms. Inside the buyer's organisation, a purchase order is normally raised only after an internal approval, because it commits the organisation to buy.

4. Agreement
When the seller confirms the purchase order, or the parties sign a fuller contract document, the transaction is established: there is an offer, an acceptance, and a common understanding of the terms. For small or repeated trades, the pro forma invoice, the purchase order and the commercial invoice may together carry the whole arrangement. For larger or first-time transactions, the parties usually sign a formal written contract recording every agreed term.

5. Performance
From that point the deal moves into execution: the goods are prepared, shipped, documented, delivered and paid for, exactly as the following blocks in this lesson describe.

The important point is that the transaction exists commercially before the goods move. By the time a container is stuffed, everything about the sale — goods, price, delivery, payment — should already be agreed in writing.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The international sales contract
BODY:
The international sales contract is a legally binding agreement governing the sale of goods between parties involved in an international commercial transaction. It is the master document of the shipment: every other paper in the file exists to carry out what the contract records.

Think back to the document families from the lesson on logistics documentation: commercial documents, transport documents, certificates, customs declarations, delivery records. None of those documents decides anything on its own. The commercial invoice states the price because the contract fixed the price. The goods are packed as they are because the contract specified the packaging. The certificate of origin is obtained because the contract, or the destination requirements behind it, called for one. The contract sits above the file and gives every document its purpose.

The contract performs four jobs.

1. It records the bargain
The contract is the single authoritative statement of what the parties agreed, so that nobody depends on memory or scattered emails.

2. It allocates obligations
A contractual obligation is a duty or responsibility that a party is legally required to fulfil under an agreement. The contract sets out, item by item, which obligation belongs to the buyer and which belongs to the seller.

3. It allocates risk
The contract determines where the seller's responsibility ends and the buyer's begins: who bears the risk of loss or damage at each stage of the journey, and who pays each cost.

4. It provides evidence and remedies
If something goes wrong, the contract is the reference both parties, and if necessary a court or arbitrator, will use to decide what was promised and what was not.

The contract binds its parties — the buyer and the seller. It is not the contract of carriage, because the carrier is not a party to it, and it is not a customs document, because customs is not a party to it either. One shipment, as you will see later in this lesson, rests on several agreements at once.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Essential terms of an international sales contract
BODY:
A contract protects the parties only as far as it is specific. Vague contracts store trouble; precise contracts prevent it. International sales contracts commonly include the following essential terms.

1. The parties
The full legal names and addresses of buyer and seller. Obligations are worthless unless it is clear whose they are.

2. Description of the goods
A precise description of what is being sold, with specifications of quality, grade or standard where relevant. Processed foods carry recipes of a commercial kind: variety, packaging type, labelling, shelf life.

3. Quantity
How much is being sold, and in what unit of measure — cartons, kilograms, litres. Ambiguity here feeds the document discrepancies the documentation lesson warned about.

4. Price and currency
The unit price and total price, and the currency of payment. In international sales the currency must be stated, not assumed.

5. Delivery terms
The terms of sale are the agreed commercial conditions governing the transaction, including matters such as price, delivery and payment. The delivery clause answers: who arranges and pays for each stage of transport, who insures, and at what point the risk passes from seller to buyer. International sales commonly express this clause by naming an Incoterms 2020 rule together with a named place — the subject of the next lesson.

6. Payment terms
When payment falls due, by what method, in what currency, and against which documents. Payment terms are the seller's answer to its fear of not being paid.

7. Documents
Which documents the seller must provide: the commercial set, the transport document, and any certificates such as a certificate of origin or health certificate.

8. Governing law and dispute resolution
Which country's law governs the contract, and how disagreements will be resolved. Dispute resolution is the process of settling disagreements between parties through negotiation, mediation, arbitration, litigation or another agreed mechanism. Naming the mechanism in advance turns a future crisis into a planned procedure.

Each of these terms exists because leaving it blank costs money later, as the worked examples in this lesson demonstrate.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Why delivery and payment terms must be clearly defined
BODY:
Two contract terms work hardest in practice: the delivery terms and the payment terms. Each answers a question the parties must not leave open.

The delivery terms answer: who does what, and where does responsibility change hands? If the contract states the delivery term precisely, the seller knows whether it must arrange carriage to the buyer's door or only deliver the goods at its own premises; the buyer knows whether import clearance and its costs are its own problem; and the freight forwarder engaged to move the goods knows exactly how far its instructions run. If the delivery term is vague, every one of those parties guesses, and the guesses rarely match.

The payment terms answer: when and against what does the seller get paid? Payment may fall due before shipment, on shipment, against documents, or after arrival; the difference decides which party carries the financial risk while the goods travel. If the contract is silent, the seller invoices when it chooses and the buyer pays when it chooses, and the gap between those choices is a dispute.

When terms are unclear, the commercial risks that follow are predictable. Costs that nobody allocated are settled by argument. Shipments wait at ports while the parties decide who pays the charges. One party withholds payment because it believes the goods were not what it agreed to buy. A breach of contract occurs — a failure by a party to fulfil a contractual obligation without lawful justification — and the parties discover that an unclear obligation is the hardest of all to enforce or defend.

Breach carries consequences: claims for damages, goods detained, payment withheld, and business relationships that do not survive. The prevention costs nothing but discipline: define the delivery, payment and document terms fully, in writing, before the contract is signed. The rest of this lesson shows what that discipline looks like in practice.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: From private contracts to trade agreements between countries
BODY:
Everything so far in this lesson has concerned the private layer of international trade: the sales contract, binding only the buyer and the seller who signed it. But above that private layer sits a public layer that shapes the conditions under which every shipment crosses a border.

A trade agreement is an agreement between countries or trading blocs establishing rules or conditions governing trade between them. A free trade agreement, or FTA, is a trade agreement intended to reduce or eliminate specified barriers to trade — the tariffs, quotas and regulatory restrictions the previous lesson introduced as trade barriers.

Governments conclude trade agreements for reasons that matter directly to traders.

1. To reduce barriers
Duties make goods more expensive at the border. By agreeing to reduce or remove specified barriers, governments make each other's goods cheaper and more competitive, and their exporters win markets they could not serve profitably at full duty.

2. To create predictable rules
Traders can plan when they know the rules will not change without warning. Agreements fix the conditions of trade — which goods receive what treatment, under what procedures — so a business can sign a two-year supply contract with reasonable confidence about what the border will look like for two years.

3. To open markets
Agreements give a country's producers access to larger markets, and give buyers a wider choice of suppliers.

Here is the essential relationship between the two layers. The exporter does not sign the trade agreement — governments do. But every shipment the exporter makes is priced, documented and cleared under the conditions that agreement created. The sales contract decides what the parties owe each other; the trade agreement decides what the border will demand from the goods. A trader who understands only one of the two layers is working with half the picture.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The levels of trade agreements
BODY:
Trade agreements exist at several levels, and a learner should be able to recognise each one.

1. Multilateral rules
At the widest level sit the multilateral rules of the world trading system, maintained under the World Trade Organization, an organisation in which a very large number of countries agree common rules for trade. The baseline of this system is that members grant one another equal, non-discriminatory treatment — known as most-favoured-nation treatment — so goods from fellow members normally enter on the same ordinary terms. Multilateral rules set the floor for world trade; they are not tied to any one region or product.

2. Regional economic communities and customs unions
At the regional level, neighbouring countries integrate more deeply. A regional economic community is a group of countries in the same region that cooperate on economic matters. When members go further and form a customs union, goods move between them with reduced or zero duties, and the members apply a common external tariff — one shared set of duties on goods entering the bloc from outside, so that the same product faces the same border charge whichever member state it enters through. In West Africa, the regional economic community is ECOWAS, and under its trade liberalisation scheme goods approved as originating in member states move duty-free. The operative word is originating, which the next block explains.

3. Continental and bilateral agreements
Agreements also operate at continental scale. The African Continental Free Trade Area is a verified example: an agreement under which participating African countries extend preferential treatment to one another's goods, trading under agreed rules of origin and evidencing claims with a dedicated certificate of origin. At the narrowest level, two countries may conclude a bilateral agreement governing trade between them alone.

The levels stack: a single shipment may be governed by multilateral rules, a regional arrangement and a national customs law at the same time. What changes with deeper integration is the benefit available — and the knowledge needed to claim it.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Rules of origin and the certificate of origin
BODY:
A trade agreement promises nothing to a shipment until the goods qualify. Whether they qualify is decided by rules of origin — the rules under which goods are considered to have originated in a particular country. The previous lesson introduced the country of origin; here the concept becomes practical.

Origin is assessed in two different modes. Non-preferential origin is the ordinary declaration of where goods come from, used for ordinary customs treatment, statistics and marking rules. Preferential origin is a stronger claim: that the goods qualify for reduced or zero duty under a specific trade agreement. Only preferential origin unlocks a preference.

Origin rules use two core tests. The first is wholly obtained: goods grown, harvested, extracted or caught entirely in one country — cocoa grown in Ghana, fish landed in its ports — clearly originate there. The second is substantial transformation, used when goods contain inputs from elsewhere: the question is whether manufacturing or processing in the claiming country was significant enough for the goods to be considered its product. Agreements measure this in defined ways, commonly by whether the processing changes the goods' classification under the Harmonized System, the international product classification customs authorities use, or by tests expressed as processing operations or value percentages. The details differ from agreement to agreement, which is why they must be checked, not assumed.

The claim is then evidenced in writing. A certificate of origin certifies where goods come from. The non-preferential certificate is routine paperwork supporting ordinary treatment. The preferential certificate of origin is the document that evidences qualification under a specific agreement and must be presented at import to claim the reduced or zero duty — for example, the ECOWAS Certificate of Origin under the ECOWAS trade liberalisation scheme, or the AfCFTA certificate of origin for trade under the continental agreement.

The practical consequence for a trader is direct: qualification plus documentation equals preference, and a missing or wrongly issued certificate can cost the buyer the entire benefit even though the goods and the agreement both allow it. The module on logistics documentation treats certificates of origin in depth.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: How contracts support logistics planning and shipment coordination
BODY:
A well-drafted sales contract is not only a legal shield. It is the operating brief from which the entire logistics plan is built. Every participant in the shipment reads some part of the contract as instructions.

1. Delivery terms direct the transport plan
The delivery term tells the parties who arranges each leg of the journey and to which point. From it, the seller knows whether to engage a freight forwarder at all, the forwarder knows the scope of its instructions, and the buyer knows what to prepare at the receiving end.

2. Delivery dates drive the schedule
The agreed delivery period sets the production and preparation timetable, the booking of vessel or aircraft space, and the receiving arrangements at destination. Booked sailings are missed when nobody has anchored the transport plan to a contractual date.

3. Payment terms dictate the document flow
The payment clause determines which documents must be produced, when, and in what order — the invoice, the transport document, the insurance document where required, the certificates. Preparing a shipment with the payment terms in view prevents the situation where goods are ready but the papers the payment depends on do not exist.

4. Document requirements set the lead times
Certificates take time to obtain. A contract that names the required certificate of origin forces the exporter to start the qualification and application process early, not after the truck has left.

5. Packaging and marking terms protect the cargo
Specifications for packaging, marking and handling translate directly into warehouse and carrier instructions.

Coordination is the thread through all of this. The seller's factory, the forwarder, the carrier, the customs representative and the buyer's warehouse are separate organisations that will never hold a meeting. The contract is the meeting: it is the one document all of them work from, and when its terms are clear, their separate actions fit together. That is what the last objective of this lesson means by contracts supporting effective logistics planning and shipment coordination.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: One product, two buyers: why origin documentation decides the duty
BODY:
A fruit-processing company near Kumasi produces packaged fruit juice from fruit grown and processed in Ghana. It sells 1,200 cartons of the same juice to a wholesaler in Ouagadougou, Burkina Faso, and 1,200 cartons of the same juice to a distributor in Dubai. The product is identical; what differs is the paperwork. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: Two contracts are signed
Each sale is recorded in its own contract: the goods, the quantity, the price, the delivery terms, the payment terms. The contract with the Ouagadougou wholesaler states that the goods are of Ghanaian origin and that the seller will provide the preferential certificate of origin required under the regional arrangement. The contract with the Dubai distributor lists the ordinary export documents, with a non-preferential certificate of origin.

Step 2: The qualification is checked before the promise is made
Before promising preferential treatment, the exporter checks its goods against the rules of origin of the regional agreement. The fruit is wholly obtained in Ghana and the juice is processed and packaged at the Kumasi plant, so the goods qualify as originating. Only then does the certificate application go in.

Step 3: The certificates are obtained
For the regional sale, the exporter obtains the preferential ECOWAS certificate of origin, evidencing that the goods are approved as originating in an ECOWAS member state under the ECOWAS Trade Liberalisation Scheme. For the Dubai sale, a non-preferential certificate of origin is issued, supporting ordinary customs treatment at destination.

Step 4: The two borders
The truck carrying the 1,200 cartons to Ouagadougou crosses the border with the preferential certificate in the file. The buyer clears the goods at reduced or zero duty, because the goods qualify and the document proves it. The consignment to Dubai arrives without any applicable preference, and the buyer pays full duty under the ordinary treatment that applies to goods from outside any agreement with its country.

Step 5: The comparison
Same company, same juice, same carton count. The duty outcomes differ entirely because of three things: an agreement between countries, goods that qualify under its origin rules, and the certificate that proves qualification.

What does this example demonstrate?

Preferential treatment is claimed shipment by shipment, not granted by the existence of an agreement. The agreement created the possibility; the origin rules decided whether these goods fit; the certificate carried the claim across the border.

Key lesson: origin documentation is money. A complete, correct preferential certificate turns an ordinary export into a competitive one, and its absence turns a qualifying shipment into a fully dutiful one.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Negotiating the contract: when buyer and seller priorities collide
BODY:
An equipment trading company in Accra needs an industrial rice mill from a manufacturer in Mumbai, in time for the milling season. It is a costly machine, and this is the first deal between the two companies. Watch how the contract is built out of two clashing sets of priorities. All figures and arrangements in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: The enquiry and the collision
The buyer sends an enquiry; the manufacturer replies with a quotation and a pro forma invoice. Then the priorities collide. The manufacturer wants full payment before the machine leaves its factory: once the mill sails for West Africa, its leverage is gone. The buyer refuses to pay a large sum up front for a machine it has never seen: if the mill never ships, or ships wrong, its money is gone. Advance payment is safest for the seller and riskiest for the buyer; payment after delivery reverses the risk. Both positions are rational and unacceptable to the other side.

Step 2: The contract resolves the deadlock
Neither party wins outright, so the terms are engineered to give each what it needs: security, converted into checkable obligations.

1. Part payment with the order
The buyer pays part of the price when placing the order, proving commitment and funding the build.

2. Balance against documents
The balance falls due only against documents evidencing that the machine was built and shipped as contracted: the commercial invoice, the transport document, an inspection certificate.

3. Pre-shipment inspection
An independent inspector examines the mill against the agreed specification before shipment. The buyer never touches the machine, but holds third-party evidence of what was built.

4. A defined delivery term
The contract states the delivery term precisely: who arranges the carriage to Tema, and where the risk passes. The next lesson on Incoterms explains the standard rules for this purpose.

5. An agreed dispute resolution clause
The parties name arbitration, so a disagreement has a planned route instead of a courtroom lottery.

Step 3: Performance
The mill is built; the inspector certifies it; it ships; the documents are exchanged and the balance is paid; the machine arrives, clears and is installed before the season. Each payment and handover landed exactly on the checkpoints the contract defined.

What does this example demonstrate?

The contract did not create trust between two companies on opposite sides of the Indian Ocean. It did something better: it replaced trust with obligations each party could verify before taking its own next step.

Key lesson: a well-drafted contract is not paperwork that follows the deal. It is what makes the deal possible between parties who have every reason not to trust each other.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: When the terms are not defined: a costly gap in the contract
BODY:
A food-processing company in Takoradi buys an automatic packaging machine from a machinery supplier in Dubai. The deal is made quickly, by email: supply one packaging machine, at an agreed price, payment of the balance on delivery. Nothing else is written down. No delivery term, no statement of who clears the goods for import, no list of documents, no packaging specification for the machine's sensitive control parts. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: The shipment
The supplier ships the machine to Tema and sends the transport documents. In the supplier's understanding, its job ends at the port: its price covered carriage to the port of destination, and import clearance and onward transport were always the buyer's business. In the buyer's understanding, delivery on delivery meant one thing: the machine standing installed-ready at its factory gate in Takoradi. Both parties are sincere. The contract supports neither of them.

Step 2: The gap appears
The machine arrives at the port. The import declaration must be lodged, import duties and taxes must be paid, terminal charges must be settled, and the machine must be trucked 200-plus kilometres to Takoradi. The supplier points to its quotation; the buyer points to the words on delivery. Each waits for the other to move first.

Step 3: The cost of vagueness
The container sits. Time-based storage charges begin to run. The production start date the buyer had promised its own customers slips. Two companies that concluded a friendly sale now exchange reproachful emails instead of plans. Eventually they settle by splitting the unexpected costs between them: nobody is satisfied, the machine arrives late, and neither party is eager to trade with the other again.

Step 4: What a clear contract would have said
Ten lines of defined terms would have prevented all of it: a delivery term naming the point of delivery and who arranges carriage and clearance to it; payment terms tied to defined stages; a list of the documents each party must provide; packaging and marking requirements. Every one of these is a standard term of a well-drafted international sales contract.

What does this example demonstrate?

Undefined terms do not make obligations disappear. They leave the obligations standing, to be allocated later by argument, at the worst possible moment — after the money is committed and the goods are in the port.

Key lesson: the cheapest time to agree who pays for what is before the signature, and the most expensive time is after the container arrives.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: An agreement between countries does not exempt a shipment by itself
BODY:
The most dangerous misunderstanding in this lesson is believing that a trade agreement automatically makes shipments duty-free. It does not. An agreement between countries creates a possibility; the shipment itself must earn it.

Three requirements stand between an agreement on paper and a reduced duty at the border.

1. The agreement must cover the goods
The agreement must be in force between the two countries concerned, and its scope must include the goods being shipped. Agreements list what they cover; not every product of every sector is included.

2. The goods must qualify under the rules of origin
The goods must genuinely meet the agreement's origin rules — wholly obtained, or substantially transformed in the claiming country. Goods produced elsewhere and merely repacked or passed through do not qualify, however they are described.

3. The documents must be right
The claim must be evidenced at import by the required preferential certificate of origin, issued through the proper channel and presented with the consignment. A qualifying shipment without its certificate normally pays full duty.

Miss any one of the three and the preference is lost, even though the agreement exists and the trader speaks confidently about free trade.

There is an integrity edge to this as well, and it echoes the rule from the lesson on logistics documentation that a professional never signs a document its contents cannot support. A certificate of origin must reflect genuine qualification. Obtaining a preferential certificate for goods that do not qualify is a false declaration, with consequences that outlast any single shipment.

Remember the sentence in full: the agreement opens the door, the goods must fit through it, and the certificate is the key.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: A sales contract binds the parties, not the state
BODY:
A second misconception follows closely from the first: believing that what the contract says overrides what the law requires. It does not.

The sales contract is a private agreement between the buyer and the seller. It binds them, and only them. Customs authorities, licensing bodies and other regulators in either country are not parties to the contract, owe nothing under it, and will not bend their procedures because of it.

Three consequences follow.

1. A contract cannot license what the law prohibits
If goods are prohibited or restricted at either end, a clause in the contract cannot make them tradable. The parties must establish that the goods may lawfully be imported and exported before they sign, not after.

2. Permits and procedures apply regardless
If the import requires a permit, a declaration, payment of duties and taxes or inspection, those requirements attach to the shipment by law. A contract clause saying the seller is responsible for import clearance allocates the duty of doing that work; it does not exempt the work from the law's requirements.

3. Each party remains subject to its own law
If the contract allocates a responsibility to a party that its national law does not permit it to carry out, the law prevails. The disappointment lands on the parties, because the state was never a signatory.

The practical discipline is straightforward: before signing an international sales contract, check the regulatory requirements at both ends of the trade — what may be imported, what permits are needed, what the customs procedures will demand. The contract shapes the deal between the parties; the law of each country shapes what the parties are allowed to do.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: One shipment, several agreements
BODY:
A single international shipment rests on several agreements at once, and confusing them is the classic beginner's error. Learn to hear which agreement a question belongs to.

1. The sales contract
The agreement between buyer and seller governs the commercial deal: the goods, the price, the delivery terms, the payment terms, the documents. When the question is when must I be paid or who supplies the certificate of origin, the answer lives in the sales contract.

2. The contract of carriage
The agreement between the shipper and the carrier governs the movement itself: what is carried, to which port or place, under what terms. The carrier is not a party to the sales contract and does not care when the buyer pays; when the question is who moves the goods and to which point, the answer lives in the contract of carriage.

3. Trade agreements between governments
The agreements between countries govern the conditions at the border: whether the goods may receive preferential duty treatment, under what origin rules and what documentation. Neither the buyer nor the seller signed them, and they promise nothing about anyone's payment. When the question is what duty will the buyer pay, the answer comes from the national tariff as shaped by the applicable trade agreements and the origin of the goods.

Notice how the errors happen. A trader assumes a free trade agreement guarantees payment terms — it does not; that is the sales contract's job. A buyer assumes the sales contract can change the border rules — it cannot; that is the governments' layer. Keeping the three agreements separate in your mind is the final distinction this lesson asks you to carry forward, and it is the distinction the practice questions below test.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A retailer in Accra buys a container of goods from a supplier in a neighbouring country. After the goods are delivered, the retailer refuses to pay the invoice, arguing that the two countries have a trade agreement, so the sale should have cost nothing and no payment terms apply.

Which statement best describes this situation?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The retailer is right, because a trade agreement between two countries removes the payment obligations of the businesses trading under it.
2. The retailer is wrong, because a trade agreement sets conditions at the border between countries and does not replace the sales contract, which still binds the buyer to the agreed price and payment terms.
3. The retailer is right, because only written contracts signed by both governments can create payment obligations between businesses.
4. The retailer is wrong, because payment terms are set by the shipping line and apply whether or not the parties agreed them.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

A trade agreement is an agreement between countries or trading blocs establishing the rules and conditions that govern trade between them, such as reduced duties for qualifying goods at the border. It is made by governments, not by the trading parties, and it says nothing about what one business owes another. The obligation to pay the agreed price comes from the sales contract between the retailer and the supplier, and that contract remains fully binding regardless of what exists at the government level.

Answer 1 confuses the two layers of trade exactly as the lesson's final note warns: an agreement between states never cancels a private payment obligation. Answer 3 invents a requirement that does not exist; commercial contracts between businesses bind them without any government signature. Answer 4 assigns payment terms to the wrong party entirely; the carrier transports goods and does not set the commercial terms of the sale.

The takeaway: keep the layers separate. The trade agreement governs the border; the sales contract governs the parties' obligations, including payment.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
A Ghanaian exporter sells packaged juice to a buyer in a neighbouring country under a regional trade arrangement that grants reduced duties to originating goods. The goods qualify as originating, but the exporter ships the consignment without obtaining the preferential certificate of origin, thinking the agreement alone is enough.

Which option best explains what will happen at import?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The buyer will receive the reduced duty automatically, because the goods qualify and customs can see the goods are from the region.
2. The buyer will normally pay full duty, because the preferential claim must be evidenced at import by the required preferential certificate of origin, which was not presented.
3. The buyer will receive the reduced duty once the seller sends a photocopy of the trade agreement with the shipment.
4. The buyer will pay no duty at all, because goods that qualify under the rules of origin never require supporting documents.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

An agreement between countries does not exempt a shipment by itself. The lesson established three requirements: the agreement must cover the goods, the goods must genuinely qualify under the rules of origin, and the claim must be evidenced at import by the required preferential certificate of origin. This shipment meets the first two conditions but fails the third, and a qualifying consignment presented without its preferential certificate normally pays full duty.

Answer 1 fails because customs grants preferences on the basis of documents, not visual judgement about where goods appear to come from. Answer 3 misunderstands the certificate: a copy of the agreement is not an origin document, and the preferential certificate must be issued through the proper channel for the specific consignment. Answer 4 reverses the rule; qualifying goods are precisely the goods that need the preferential certificate, because the certificate is what carries the claim across the border.

The takeaway: agreement, qualification and documentation must all be present. The certificate of origin is money, and leaving it behind forfeits the benefit it exists to prove.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A company orders machinery from an overseas supplier. The email agreement states only the machine and the price. It does not state who arranges carriage and import clearance, at which point delivery is complete, or when payment beyond a vague balance on delivery falls due. When the machine arrives at the port, both parties believe the other must pay the arrival charges and transport it inland.

Which statement best explains the commercial problem?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The problem is unavoidable in international trade, because delivery and payment responsibilities can never be predicted before goods move.
2. The problem arose because the contract left the delivery and payment obligations undefined, so costs that nobody allocated must now be settled by argument after the money is already committed.
3. The problem is the shipping line's fault, because carriers decide who pays arrival charges whenever the contract is silent.
4. The problem shows that email agreements are not contracts, so neither party owes the other anything.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Undefined terms do not remove obligations; they leave them to be allocated later by dispute, at the worst possible time. The delivery terms of a sales contract exist precisely to state who arranges and pays each stage of the journey and where responsibility passes, and the payment terms exist to state when and against what the seller is paid. When a contract omits them, each party reasonably assumes the arrangement that suits it, and the gap appears when the goods arrive and the charges fall due.

Answer 1 is defeated by the lesson's own worked examples: defined delivery and payment terms are standard, routine contract content, and clarity prevents exactly this dispute. Answer 3 assigns a commercial decision to the wrong party; the carrier applies the transport document, it does not allocate the parties' contractual obligations. Answer 4 overstates: an agreed exchange, even by email, can bind the parties, which is why vague agreements are dangerous rather than harmless.

The takeaway: define delivery, payment and document terms fully before signing. The cheapest time to agree who pays for what is before the signature.
