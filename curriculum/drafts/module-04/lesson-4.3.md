=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: What Incoterms are
BODY:
When a seller in one country and a buyer in another conclude a sale, the goods may travel thousands of kilometres, pass through a seaport or airport, cross a border, and be handled by many organisations along the way. The previous lesson examined how buyers and sellers reach agreement across borders. This lesson examines the short clause that decides who does what during the journey that follows. Before the goods move, both parties must agree on practical questions: who arranges and pays for the international transport, who completes export and import formalities, and who bears the loss if the goods are damaged on the way.

Incoterms exist to answer these questions in a way both parties understand in exactly the same manner.

Incoterms are internationally recognised trade rules published by the International Chamber of Commerce (ICC), the international business organisation responsible for publishing and maintaining the Incoterms rules. They define specified delivery responsibilities, costs, and risks between buyers and sellers. Each rule is a three-letter code, such as EXW, FOB, or CIF.

The current edition is Incoterms 2020, which entered into effect on 1 January 2020 and contains eleven rules. The parties choose one of the eleven, complete it with a named place or port, and record it in their contract of sale. A clause reading FOB Tema, Incoterms 2020 tells both parties who must do what, which costs each bears, and where risk passes from seller to buyer.

The purpose of Incoterms is certainty. Without an agreed standard, a seller in Ghana and a buyer in the Netherlands might each assume the other would arrange the ocean freight, pay the import duties, or carry the risk of damage at sea. Because the rules are published and recognised internationally, a buyer in Hamburg and a seller in Accra read the same meaning in FOB or CIF. Incoterms perform one central job: they take the long journey between seller and buyer, divide it into individual obligations, and allocate those obligations, with their costs and their risks, between the two parties.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The journey the rules divide
BODY:
To see how the eleven rules differ, picture the segments every international consignment passes through. An earlier lesson walked through the full journey of goods stage by stage; Incoterms look at that same journey as a set of responsibilities to be allocated.

1. The seller's premises. The goods are produced, packed, and made available for the journey.

2. Export clearance. The goods are cleared for export in the country of shipment, through export formalities and any applicable export duties.

3. Main carriage. The goods travel the international leg by sea, air, road, rail, or a combination of modes.

4. Arrival. The goods reach the destination port or place and are handled on arrival.

5. Import clearance. The goods are cleared for import in the country of destination, through import formalities and any applicable duties or taxes.

6. The buyer's premises. The goods are delivered to the buyer's warehouse or site.

For each segment, an Incoterms rule answers two questions: who arranges and pays for the segment, and who bears the risk if the goods are lost or damaged during it. Every quotation such as CIF Antwerp or DAP Accra is a completed answer sheet for all six segments.

Two allocations hold across the eleven rules, and they are worth memorising now. Export clearance is the seller's obligation under every rule except EXW, where it falls to the buyer. Import clearance is the buyer's obligation under every rule except DDP, where it falls to the seller. What distinguishes the individual rules is chiefly the allocation of the main carriage and the remaining segments, and the point at which risk passes.

One caution before going further: the party who pays for a segment is not always the party who risks it. The rules separate those two matters, and later in this lesson we will see where the separation matters most.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Named places and the transfer of risk
BODY:
Every Incoterms clause must be completed with a named place. A named place is the specific location or port identified in an Incoterms clause to establish the relevant delivery or cost obligations. Depending on the rule, it may be the seller's factory, a port of shipment, a destination port, or the buyer's premises. FOB Tema names Tema as the port of shipment. DAP Accra names Accra as the destination. The named place is not an administrative detail: it fixes where the seller's delivery obligations end and the buyer's begin.

The second key idea is the transfer of risk. The transfer of risk is the point at which the risk of loss or damage to goods passes from the seller to the buyer under the applicable contractual arrangement. While goods are at the seller's risk, damage before that point is the seller's problem. Once risk has passed, damage is the buyer's problem even if the goods are still at sea, still in a port, or still standing at the seller's factory awaiting collection.

Each rule places this boundary at a different point along the journey. Under EXW, risk passes when the goods are placed at the buyer's disposal at the seller's premises. Under FCA, it passes when the goods are handed to the buyer's carrier at the named place. Under FOB, CFR, and CIF, it passes when the goods are on board the vessel at the port of shipment. Under DAP, it passes only when the goods are placed at the buyer's disposal, ready for unloading, at the named destination.

Two lines therefore run side by side through every Incoterms clause: a cost line, showing who pays for each segment, and a risk line, showing who carries each segment. The skill of reading Incoterms is keeping the two lines apart, because they do not always sit in the same place.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: What Incoterms do not determine
BODY:
Incoterms allocate tasks, costs, and risk, and nothing more. Three matters sit permanently outside them.

1. Transfer of ownership. No Incoterms rule governs when title to the goods passes from seller to buyer. A sale agreed on CIF terms does not mean the buyer owns the goods from the moment they are loaded on board, and a sale on EXW terms does not mean ownership passes when the goods are placed at the buyer's disposal. Ownership is a matter for the contract and the law that governs it.

2. Payment. Incoterms say nothing about how or when the buyer must pay the price. The three letters allocate transport obligations; they do not create a duty to pay at shipment, on arrival, or at any other point. Payment terms are agreed separately in the contract.

3. Remedies for breach. If a party fails to perform its obligations, Incoterms do not set the consequences. The contract and the applicable law govern what follows a failure to deliver, to pay, or to insure where such an obligation exists.

For these reasons, Incoterms never replace the contract of sale. They operate inside it: the parties agree an Incoterms rule as one clause among many. A contract that states only FOB Tema, Incoterms 2020 and nothing else would be a very incomplete contract, because it would say nothing about price, payment, delivery schedule, quality, or dispute resolution. The Incoterms clause answers one set of questions precisely — who does what, who pays which costs, where risk passes — and is silent on everything else.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The two families: rules for any mode and rules for the sea
BODY:
The eleven Incoterms 2020 rules divide into two families according to the transport they suit.

The first family contains four rules intended only for sea and inland waterway transport: FAS (Free Alongside Ship), FOB (Free on Board), CFR (Cost and Freight), and CIF (Cost, Insurance and Freight). These rules describe delivery in maritime terms — goods placed alongside the ship, or on board the vessel, at the named port of shipment. They should be chosen when the agreed delivery genuinely takes place in that way, at a seaport.

The second family contains seven rules suitable for any mode of transport — road, rail, air, sea, or a combination of modes: EXW (Ex Works), FCA (Free Carrier), CPT (Carriage Paid To), CIP (Carriage and Insurance Paid To), DAP (Delivered at Place), DPU (Delivered at Place Unloaded), and DDP (Delivered Duty Paid). These rules describe delivery as handing goods over at a named place rather than loading them on board a vessel, which makes them fit containerised cargo, air consignments, and multimodal journeys that combine several modes.

The distinction matters in practice. A manufacturer in Accra sending spare parts by air from Kotoka International Airport needs a rule from the any-mode family, such as CIP or DAP; agreeing CIF would misstate the parties' obligations, because CIF is reserved for sea and inland waterway transport whatever the goods are. The rule must match the manner of delivery, not merely the destination.

Matching the family to the mode is therefore the first discipline of using Incoterms correctly: identify where and how the goods will actually be delivered, then choose from the family that describes that delivery.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The four groups: a progression of seller responsibility
BODY:
The eleven rules are conventionally arranged in four groups — E, F, C, and D — according to how far along the journey the seller's responsibilities extend. Read in order, the groups form a progression from the seller's minimum to the seller's maximum.

1. Group E — EXW (Ex Works). The seller's only delivery obligation is to make the goods available at its own premises. From that point the buyer assumes most transportation responsibilities and risks. This is the seller's minimum.

2. Group F — FCA, FAS, and FOB. The seller delivers the goods to a carrier nominated by the buyer: handed to the carrier at the named place under FCA, placed alongside the ship under FAS, or loaded on board the vessel under FOB. The buyer arranges and pays for the main carriage. The seller's responsibility stops at the agreed handover.

3. Group C — CFR, CIF, CPT, and CIP. The seller takes one large step further: it arranges and pays for the main carriage to the destination. But the risk does not follow the freight. Under CFR and CIF risk passes when the goods are on board the vessel at the port of shipment; under CPT and CIP it passes on handover to the first carrier. The seller pays to the destination while the buyer already carries the risk — the group's defining feature.

4. Group D — DAP, DPU, and DDP. The seller bears responsibility until the goods arrive at the destination: DAP, when they are placed at the buyer's disposal, ready for unloading, at the named destination; DPU — renamed from DAT in Incoterms 2020 — once they are unloaded, the only rule under which the seller unloads; and DDP, when delivery is complete with import clearance and applicable duties also carried by the seller. This is the seller's maximum.

As the groups progress from E to D, the seller's tasks and costs reach further along the journey. Only in group C, as we shall see, do the cost and the risk travel different distances.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: EXW: Ex Works, the seller's minimum
BODY:
EXW (Ex Works) is the rule under which the seller makes the goods available at a named place — in practice usually the seller's own factory or warehouse — with the buyer assuming most transportation responsibilities and risks from that point. The seller's obligation is close to the minimum imaginable: produce the goods, pack them, and place them at the buyer's disposal. Under the standard allocation of Incoterms 2020, export clearance is the buyer's obligation under EXW — the only one of the eleven rules under which the seller does not clear the goods for export.

Consider what that means for an international sale. A buyer in another country, possibly with no presence in the seller's country at all, must arrange collection from the factory, complete export formalities in a foreign administrative system, and carry the risk of the goods from the moment they are placed at its disposal at the seller's premises. For this reason plain EXW is widely regarded as impractical for international sales.

Where the buyer cannot take on export clearance, a rule from the F group usually fits better, most commonly FCA, under which the seller delivers the goods, cleared for export, to the carrier nominated by the buyer at the named place. The seller performs the formalities of its own country; the buyer keeps control of the main carriage.

EXW still has legitimate uses, particularly for domestic-style purchases or for buyers with a local representative able to handle export clearance on the spot. Risk under EXW passes when the goods are placed at the buyer's disposal at the seller's premises. From that point — loading, inland transport, export clearance, the main carriage, arrival, and import clearance — the buyer pays for each segment and carries the risk of each segment. Of the eleven rules, EXW gives the seller the shortest list of duties and the buyer the longest.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: FCA: Free Carrier, handing over to the buyer's carrier
BODY:
FCA (Free Carrier) is the rule under which the seller delivers the goods to the carrier, or another person, nominated by the buyer at the named place. The buyer chooses the carrier and contracts for the main carriage; the seller's job is to deliver the goods, cleared for export, into that carrier's custody at the agreed place.

The allocations follow the F-group pattern. Export clearance belongs to the seller, as it does under every rule except EXW. The main carriage belongs to the buyer, who nominated the carrier and pays the freight. Risk passes when the goods are handed over to the buyer's carrier at the named place: damage before the handover is the seller's to bear, damage after it is the buyer's.

The named place deserves attention, because it decides where the handover happens. Under FCA it may be the seller's own premises or another agreed point such as a terminal; whatever the parties choose, the named place fixes where the seller's delivery ends and the buyer's carriage begins. Vague wording such as FCA Tema without specifying the precise point invites disputes about whether delivery was completed.

FCA belongs to the any-mode family, which makes it suitable for containerised cargo: containers are commonly handed to the carrier at a terminal before the vessel arrives, and FCA describes exactly that kind of handover at a named place. An exporter of 400 cartons of shea butter agreeing FCA a named terminal in Tema clears the goods for export and delivers them to the buyer's nominated carrier there; from that handover the buyer carries the risk and pays for the carriage onward to, say, Hamburg.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: FOB: Free on Board at the port of shipment
BODY:
FOB (Free on Board) is a rule for sea and inland waterway transport under which the seller delivers the goods on board the vessel nominated by the buyer at the named port of shipment.

The pattern will now be familiar. Export clearance is the seller's obligation. The buyer nominates the vessel and arranges and pays for the main carriage. Risk passes when the goods are on board the vessel at the port of shipment: getting the goods onto the ship is at the seller's risk, and from the moment they are on board, the voyage and everything after it are at the buyer's.

FOB sits in the maritime family alongside FAS, which describes delivery one step earlier: under FAS the seller delivers when the goods are placed alongside the ship at the port of shipment, and the loading step itself belongs to the buyer. FOB moves the boundary one stage forward, onto the vessel.

A practical warning belongs here and is developed later in this lesson. FOB requires delivery on board the vessel. Where cargo is handed to the carrier at a terminal before the vessel arrives — the normal pattern for containerised shipments — the delivery that FOB describes does not match what actually happens, and FCA is the appropriate rule. FOB fits a sale in which the goods genuinely go on board the ship at the load port.

Summarising FOB in the two lines used throughout this lesson: the cost line runs from the seller's premises through export clearance to the ship at the named port of shipment, after which every cost is the buyer's; the risk line ends at the same point, when the goods are on board. Under FOB the two lines sit together; the split comes with the C rules, where the cost line runs on while the risk line does not.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: CFR and CIF: paying to the destination, risk remaining at shipment
BODY:
CFR (Cost and Freight) is the rule under which the seller pays the cost and freight necessary to bring the goods to the named destination port, while risk transfers when the goods are on board the vessel at the port of shipment. Read those two halves slowly, because they point in different directions. The seller's cost obligation runs to the destination port; the seller's risk ends at the port of shipment, the moment the goods are on board.

Everything between the two ports — the ocean voyage itself — is paid for by the seller yet carried at the buyer's risk. If the vessel suffers a fire mid-voyage, the loss falls on the buyer, not on the seller who bought the freight. Paying for carriage does not mean bearing the risk to destination. The remaining allocations are conventional: the seller clears the goods for export and contracts the ocean freight to the named destination port; import clearance is the buyer's, as it is under every rule except DDP.

CIF (Cost, Insurance and Freight) is the same rule with one addition: the seller must obtain cargo insurance for the buyer's risk at the specified minimum level of cover. Under Incoterms 2020 that minimum is Institute Cargo Clauses (C) — the most basic of the standard market covers, directed at major casualties such as fire, explosion, or a vessel stranding or sinking.

A duty to insure exists only under CIF and CIP. Under every other rule, neither party is obliged by the rule to insure, although a party bearing the risk of a segment may insure in its own interest.

CIF has an any-mode counterpart: CPT (Carriage Paid To) and CIP (Carriage and Insurance Paid To) mirror CFR and CIF for any mode of transport. Under CPT and CIP, risk passes on handover to the first carrier — earlier still than on board a vessel — and CIP's insurance duty sits at the higher Institute Cargo Clauses (A) level, the broadest standard cover, insuring against all risks of loss or damage except those specifically excluded. A CIF price is therefore a higher price than an FOB price at the same load port, because the freight to the destination sits inside the seller's costs.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: DAP and DDP: delivery at the destination
BODY:
DAP (Delivered at Place) is the rule under which the seller delivers the goods when they are placed at the buyer's disposal on the arriving means of transport, ready for unloading, at the named destination. The seller arranges and pays for the transport to that destination: export clearance, the main carriage, and the journey to the named place all belong to the seller. Unloading is not the seller's job under DAP — the goods are delivered ready for unloading, and unloading them falls on the buyer's side of the boundary. If the parties want the seller to unload, DPU (Delivered at Place Unloaded), renamed from DAT in Incoterms 2020, is the rule that provides it, and it is the only one of the eleven under which the seller unloads.

Import clearance remains the buyer's under DAP, as under every rule except DDP. Risk passes when the goods are placed at the buyer's disposal, ready for unloading, at the named destination: the seller carries the risk across the entire journey, up to that point.

DDP (Delivered Duty Paid) is the seller's maximum. Under DDP the seller assumes extensive responsibilities, including import clearance and the applicable import duties, to deliver the goods at the named destination ready for unloading. The seller carries export clearance, the main carriage, arrival at destination, and even the formalities and duties of the customs system on the buyer's side of the border. Risk passes at the same point as under DAP, with the added feature that import clearance has already been completed by the seller.

Between them, DAP and DDP mark the far end of the progression that began with EXW. Under EXW the seller's duty ends at its own premises and the buyer does nearly everything. Under DAP the seller carries the goods to the buyer's door while the buyer clears them in. Under DDP the seller does even that. What the buyer must arrange shrinks from almost everything to almost nothing — and that is the scale on which every Incoterms choice moves.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: FOB Tema or CFR Rotterdam: what changes for the seller
BODY:
A Ghanaian manufacturer of cocoa products receives an order from a chocolate maker in Rotterdam for one container load — 18 pallets — of cocoa butter. The goods will sail from Tema to Rotterdam. The parties consider two versions of the same sale.

Step 1: the sale on FOB Tema, Incoterms 2020 terms. The seller packs and marks the 18 pallets, completes export clearance in Ghana — the seller's obligation under FOB — and delivers the goods on board the vessel nominated by the buyer at the named port of shipment, Tema. The buyer has booked the ocean freight and nominated the vessel. Risk passes when the goods are on board at Tema. From that moment, the ocean voyage, arrival in the Netherlands, and import clearance there are the buyer's affair, at the buyer's cost and risk.

Step 2: the same sale on CFR Rotterdam, Incoterms 2020 terms. The seller still packs the pallets and still completes export clearance. The seller must now do one large extra thing: contract and pay the cost and freight necessary to bring the goods to the named destination port, Rotterdam. The buyer no longer books the ocean leg.

Step 3: notice what does not change. Risk still passes when the goods are on board the vessel at Tema, because under CFR risk transfers at the port of shipment even though freight has been paid to Rotterdam. If the container is damaged by seawater halfway across the ocean, the loss is the buyer's, not the seller's. Note also that under CFR neither party is obliged by the rule to insure; had the parties chosen CIF instead, the seller would additionally have had to obtain minimum cover — Institute Cargo Clauses (C) — for the buyer's risk.

Step 4: compare the seller's position under the two rules.

1. Packing and making the goods available: the seller, under both rules.
2. Export clearance in Ghana: the seller, under both rules.
3. Ocean freight: the buyer arranges and pays under FOB Tema; the seller under CFR Rotterdam.
4. Point of risk transfer: on board at Tema, under both rules.
5. Import clearance in the Netherlands: the buyer, under both rules.

What does this example demonstrate? Moving from FOB to CFR shifts one cost obligation: the ocean freight moves from the buyer to the seller. It does not move the risk. A seller offering CFR Rotterdam is buying a service for its customer — carriage to the destination port — not accepting the risk of the voyage.

Key lesson: under the C rules, the cost boundary and the risk boundary sit in different places. Quote the rule with its edition, and know which line is which.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Buying machinery DAP Accra or EXW Mumbai: what the buyer arranges
BODY:
An engineering company in Accra needs one industrial machine from a manufacturer in Mumbai. The same purchase can be written in two very different ways, and the Incoterms clause decides how much of the journey the buyer must organise.

Scenario A: EXW Mumbai (the seller's works), Incoterms 2020. The seller makes the machine available, packed, at its factory. Everything after that is the buyer's to arrange.

1. Collection from the factory and loading: the buyer.
2. Inland transport across India to a port: the buyer.
3. Export clearance in India: the buyer's obligation under EXW — the rule's great practical weakness, because the buyer must complete export formalities in a country where it does not operate.
4. Ocean freight from India to Ghana: the buyer.
5. Arrival handling and import clearance in Ghana: the buyer.
6. Delivery from the port to the buyer's premises in Accra: the buyer.

The buyer also carries the risk from the moment the machine is placed at its disposal at the Mumbai factory. If the machine is damaged while being loaded onto the collecting truck outside the factory, that is the buyer's loss.

Scenario B: DAP Accra (the buyer's premises), Incoterms 2020. Almost the whole picture reverses. The seller arranges and pays for the transport from its Mumbai factory to the buyer's premises in Accra, including export clearance in India and the main carriage. The machine travels at the seller's risk until it is placed at the buyer's disposal, ready for unloading, at the named destination. What remains for the buyer?

1. Unloading the machine: the buyer's, because DAP delivery is ready for unloading.
2. Import clearance in Ghana, with any applicable duties: the buyer's under DAP, as under every rule except DDP.

Had the parties chosen DDP instead, the seller would also have completed import clearance and paid the import duties, and the buyer's only remaining task would have been to unload.

What does this example demonstrate? The Incoterms rule decides what an importer must organise. On EXW terms the buyer is the transport manager for the entire journey, including export formalities in a foreign country — which is why plain EXW is impractical for many international purchases. On DAP terms the buyer's practical involvement begins at its own gate, with unloading and import clearance.

Key lesson: before signing, an importer should read the three letters carefully. The difference between EXW and DAP is, for the buyer, the difference between arranging everything and arranging almost nothing.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: One journey, three rules: who pays and who risks
BODY:
A shea butter exporter in Ghana ships 800 cartons from Tema to Antwerp. The clause will be completed with the edition in every case — for example, CIF Antwerp, Incoterms 2020. To see how three different rules allocate the same journey, walk it segment by segment.

The segments are: 1. the seller's premises, where the goods are packed and made available; 2. export clearance in Ghana; 3. the main carriage, ocean freight from Tema to Antwerp; 4. arrival at Antwerp; 5. import clearance in Belgium; 6. final delivery at the buyer's premises.

Under EXW terms, the seller completes only segment 1. The buyer arranges export clearance, books the freight, manages arrival, clears the goods in Belgium, and delivers them to its own premises. Risk passes at the seller's premises, at the very start of segment 2.

Under CIF terms, the seller packs, clears export, and contracts and pays the ocean freight to Antwerp, and must also obtain cargo insurance at the minimum Institute Cargo Clauses (C) level for the buyer's risk. But the risk passes when the goods are on board the vessel at Tema. So the seller pays for segments 1 to 3, while the risk leaves the seller partway through segment 3, at loading, and the buyer handles segments 4 to 6, carrying the risk of the voyage itself.

Under DDP terms, the seller packs, clears export, pays the freight, manages arrival, completes import clearance in Belgium, pays the applicable import duties, and delivers the goods ready for unloading at the buyer's premises. Risk stays with the seller until the goods are placed at the buyer's disposal at the named destination.

Read the allocation as a whole.

1. Segment 1 is the seller's under all three rules — the one segment no rule takes away from the seller.
2. Segment 2, export clearance, is the buyer's only under EXW.
3. Segment 3, the main carriage, is the buyer's under EXW and the seller's under CIF and DDP.
4. Segments 4 to 6 return progressively to the seller as the rule moves towards DDP.
5. Import clearance in Belgium is the seller's only under DDP.

What does this example demonstrate? The three rules are points on one scale. EXW and DDP are the two ends, and CIF sits between them with its split character: costs run to Antwerp, risk stops at Tema.

Key lesson: for any rule, a trader can ask two questions per segment — who pays, and who risks — and the eleven rules give eleven different but complete sets of answers.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Under the C rules, risk passes at shipment
BODY:
The most common misunderstanding in all of Incoterms practice concerns the C group. A buyer hears that the seller is paying freight to the destination and concludes that the goods are the seller's problem until they arrive. Under CFR, CIF, CPT, and CIP, that conclusion is wrong. Under all four C rules the seller pays for carriage to the destination, but the risk passes to the buyer at shipment: under CFR and CIF, when the goods are on board the vessel at the port of shipment; under CPT and CIP, on handover to the first carrier. Both points lie far earlier than the destination.

Picture a consignment sold on CIF Rotterdam, Incoterms 2020 terms. The seller has cleared the goods for export, paid the ocean freight, and obtained the minimum insurance — Institute Cargo Clauses (C) — for the buyer's risk. Halfway across the ocean the vessel suffers a fire and the goods are damaged. Who bears the loss? The buyer, because risk passed at the port of shipment when the goods went on board, even though the seller paid the freight and the goods never reached Rotterdam. The buyer's practical protection is the insurance the seller was obliged to arrange for the buyer's benefit.

Paying for carriage does not mean bearing the risk to destination. The cost line and the risk line are two separate lines, and under the C rules they sit in different places. This single point explains a great deal of commercial behaviour, and it is why the phrase the seller pays to destination must always be completed with but risk passes at shipment.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Incoterms do not transfer ownership or decide payment
BODY:
Two questions arise in almost every sale, and Incoterms answer neither of them.

The first is ownership. No Incoterms rule determines when title to the goods passes from seller to buyer. A contract on FOB terms does not mean the buyer owns the goods from the moment they are on board; a contract on DDP terms does not mean the seller keeps ownership until delivery at the destination. Ownership is decided by the contract and the law that governs it, not by the trade term.

The second is payment. Incoterms say nothing about how or when the buyer must pay. Whether the buyer pays before shipment, against documents, or weeks after delivery is a matter for the contract — the subject of the next lesson, which examines international trade payment methods. The same silence extends to what happens when a party fails to perform: remedies for breach are for the contract and the applicable law.

For all these reasons, an Incoterms clause does not replace the contract of sale. It is one clause inside the contract, doing one defined job: allocating tasks, costs, and risk along the journey. A complete international sales contract still needs its price, payment terms, delivery schedule, quality specification, and arrangements for disputes — and it should state which Incoterms edition applies, because the rules are revised between editions.

Remember: Incoterms tell the parties who carries the goods along the journey. They do not tell the parties who owns the goods, who owes money, or when.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Match the rule to the journey, and always cite the edition
BODY:
Three drafting habits prevent most Incoterms mistakes.

1. Cite the edition. Always name the edition and the named place: CFR Rotterdam, Incoterms 2020; DAP Accra, Incoterms 2020. The word Incoterms without a year is incomplete, because the rules are revised over time, and an unspecified reference invites argument about which version governs.

2. Match the rule to the actual delivery. FOB requires delivery on board the vessel at the port of shipment. Containerised cargo is normally handed to the carrier at a terminal before the vessel arrives, yet FOB is often wrongly used for such shipments. The correct rule is FCA, which describes exactly that handover of goods to the buyer's carrier at a named place. Choosing FOB for a container handed over at a terminal writes an obligation into the contract that does not match the physical operation.

3. Respect what each rule demands of the parties. Under EXW the buyer bears the obligation to clear the goods for export in the seller's country, which makes plain EXW impractical for most international sales. Under DDP the seller must complete import clearance and pay import duties in the buyer's country — rarely workable for a seller without the necessary registration or presence there. Choose the rule whose obligations a party can actually perform.

A well-written clause answers three things at once: the three-letter rule, the named place as precisely as the parties can make it, and the edition. Getting this right is not pedantry: the Incoterms clause is the shortest sentence in the contract and the one that decides, if a container is lost at sea, whose loss it is.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A company in Accra imports ceramic tiles sold on CFR Tema, Incoterms 2020 terms. The seller in Antwerp clears the tiles for export, contracts and pays the ocean freight to Tema, and loads them on board at Antwerp. During the voyage, seawater damages part of the consignment.

Which statement best describes who bears the loss?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The seller bears the loss, because it arranged and paid the ocean freight to Tema.
2. The buyer bears the loss, because under CFR risk transferred when the goods were placed on board the vessel at the port of shipment.
3. The seller bears the loss, because risk passes only when the goods arrive at the destination port.
4. The loss falls to whichever party owns the goods at the time of the damage, as determined by the Incoterms rule.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Under CFR the seller pays the cost and freight necessary to bring the goods to the named destination port, but risk transfers when the goods are on board the vessel at the port of shipment — in this case, Antwerp. From that moment the ocean voyage is at the buyer's risk, even though the seller bought the freight. Paying for carriage does not mean bearing the risk to destination; under all four C rules the cost line and the risk line sit in different places.

Answer 1 fails because paying freight is a cost obligation, not a risk obligation. Answer 3 fails because it describes a D-group expectation: under CFR, risk does not wait for arrival at Tema. Answer 4 fails twice, because Incoterms do not determine ownership at all, and the allocation of risk comes directly from the rule itself.

The takeaway: quote the C rule with its edition, and remember that the seller pays to the destination while the buyer risks the voyage from shipment.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
A supplier in Hamburg and a buyer in Tema sign a contract stating: CIF Tema, Incoterms 2020. A dispute later arises about when the buyer must pay for the goods and when ownership of the goods passes to the buyer.

Which statement best explains how these matters are settled?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Payment and ownership follow the Incoterms rule, so CIF fixes both the moment of payment and the moment of transfer of ownership.
2. Incoterms allocate delivery responsibilities, costs, and risk only; payment and transfer of ownership are governed by the rest of the contract and the applicable law.
3. Ownership passes on delivery and payment falls due on arrival, because every Incoterms rule contains these two default rules.
4. Because the contract names Incoterms 2020, the rule replaces the contract entirely for all disputed matters.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Incoterms do one defined job: they allocate delivery responsibilities, costs, and risk between seller and buyer along the journey. They do not govern the transfer of ownership, they do not set payment terms, and they do not provide remedies for breach. Those matters come from the contract of sale itself and the law that governs it, with the Incoterms clause operating inside the contract rather than above it.

Answer 1 fails because no Incoterms rule determines ownership or payment. Answer 3 fails because the rules contain no such defaults: CIF says nothing about payment falling due on arrival. Answer 4 fails because Incoterms never replace the contract; they are a single clause within a contract that must still cover price, payment, and everything else the parties have agreed.

The takeaway: the three letters answer the transport questions — who does what, who pays, where risk passes — while money and title live in the rest of the contract.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A manufacturer in Accra will send a consignment of spare parts by air from Kotoka International Airport to a customer in Dubai. A colleague proposes agreeing CIF Dubai, arguing that the term is suitable because it includes insurance.

Which option best explains the appropriate choice?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. CIF is suitable, because air freight is simply a faster form of carriage to the named destination.
2. CIF should be replaced by DDP, because air shipments always require the seller to complete import formalities.
3. CIF is reserved for sea and inland waterway transport; for an air consignment the parties should use an any-mode rule such as CIP, which also contains a seller's insurance obligation.
4. CIF may be used for air freight provided the named place is changed from a port to an airport.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The eleven Incoterms 2020 rules divide into two families. Four rules — FAS, FOB, CFR, and CIF — are intended only for sea and inland waterway transport. The other seven — EXW, FCA, CPT, CIP, DAP, DPU, and DDP — can be used for any mode, including air. An air consignment therefore needs a rule from the any-mode family, and CIP is the natural choice here because it mirrors CIF: the seller pays for carriage to the destination, carries the risk only to the first carrier, and must obtain cargo insurance, at the higher Institute Cargo Clauses (A) level.

Answer 1 fails because speed is irrelevant to the mode restriction. Answer 2 fails because nothing about air transport requires DDP, under which the seller would also clear import formalities in Dubai. Answer 4 fails because renaming the place does not change the rule's restriction to sea and inland waterway transport.

The takeaway: match the family to the mode of transport first, then refine the choice within that family.

=== FACTS & SOURCES ===
- Incoterms are internationally recognised trade rules published by the International Chamber of Commerce (ICC) that define specified delivery responsibilities, costs, and risks between buyers and sellers — source: curriculum/research/trade-transport-standards.md, section 1 (ICC-sourced).
- The current edition is Incoterms 2020, eleven three-letter rules, in effect since 1 January 2020 — source: curriculum/research/trade-transport-standards.md, section 1 (ICC).
- Incoterms do not govern transfer of ownership/title, payment terms, or breach remedies; they allocate transport tasks, costs, and risk only — source: curriculum/research/trade-transport-standards.md, section 1 (ICC).
- Four rules are maritime-only (sea and inland waterway): FAS, FOB, CFR, CIF; seven rules suit any mode: EXW, FCA, CPT, CIP, DAP, DPU, DDP — source: curriculum/research/trade-transport-standards.md, section 1 (ICC).
- Export clearance is the seller's obligation under all rules except EXW, where it is the buyer's; this makes plain EXW impractical for international sales — source: curriculum/research/trade-transport-standards.md, section 1 (ICC).
- Import clearance is the buyer's obligation under all rules except DDP, where it is the seller's — source: curriculum/research/trade-transport-standards.md, section 1 (ICC).
- Main carriage is arranged and paid by the buyer under E and F rules and by the seller under C and D rules — source: curriculum/research/trade-transport-standards.md, section 1 (ICC).
- Risk transfer points: EXW — goods placed at seller's disposal at its premises; FCA — handover to the buyer's carrier at the named place; FAS — goods placed alongside the ship at the port of shipment; FOB, CFR, and CIF — goods on board the vessel at the port of shipment; CPT and CIP — handover to the first carrier; DAP — goods at the buyer's disposal, ready for unloading, at the named destination; DPU — same but after unloading; DDP — same as DAP plus import clearance done by the seller — source: curriculum/research/trade-transport-standards.md, section 1 (ICC).
- Under all four C rules (CFR, CIF, CPT, CIP) the seller pays carriage to destination but risk passes at shipment — paying for carriage does not mean bearing the risk to destination — source: curriculum/research/trade-transport-standards.md, section 1 (ICC).
- A duty to insure exists only under CIF and CIP: CIF at minimum Institute Cargo Clauses (C) cover, CIP at the higher Institute Cargo Clauses (A) cover; under all other rules there is no obligation, though a party bearing risk may insure in its own interest — source: curriculum/research/trade-transport-standards.md, section 1 (ICC).
- Institute Cargo Clauses (C) is directed at major casualties (fire, explosion, stranding/grounding/sinking, collision and similar); ICC(A) is the broadest standard cover, insuring against all risks of loss or damage subject to exclusions — source: curriculum/research/trade-transport-standards.md, section 10.
- DPU is the renamed DAT (Incoterms 2020 change) and is the only rule under which the seller unloads — source: curriculum/research/trade-transport-standards.md, section 1 (ICC).
- Common misuse warnings taught in this lesson: naming Incoterms without the year is incomplete; FOB is often wrongly used for containerised cargo handed over at a terminal, for which FCA is correct; DDP is rarely workable for sellers without import registration or tax presence in the buyer's country — source: curriculum/research/trade-transport-standards.md, section 1 (ICC guidance / trade.gov).
