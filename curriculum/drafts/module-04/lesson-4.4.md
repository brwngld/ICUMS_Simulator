=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Why payment arrangements matter in international trade
BODY:
In every sale of goods, two movements must take place: goods travel from the seller to the buyer, and money travels from the buyer to the seller. In a shop, both movements happen at the same moment. In international trade they almost never do. A consignment may take weeks to cross an ocean, so one party must always act first: either the seller ships goods it has not yet been paid for, or the buyer pays for goods it has not yet seen.

An international trade payment is the transfer of funds between trading parties to settle financial obligations arising from international commercial transactions. A payment arrangement is the agreed method and set of conditions under which those funds move.

International payments are more delicate than domestic ones. The parties are located in different countries, often under different legal systems, and may never meet face to face. Pursuing a claim in the other party's country can be slow, expensive, and uncertain. A seller in Tamale knows little about a buyer in Rotterdam, and the reverse is equally true.

This uncertainty creates payment risk, which is the possibility of financial loss arising from delayed, incomplete, or failed payment. Payment risk takes several forms: an exporter ships goods and is never paid in full; an importer pays in advance and the goods are never produced; payment arrives late and disrupts the exporter's cash flow.

Every payment arrangement answers two questions. First, who moves first: does the money travel before the goods, or the goods before the money? Second, what does the first mover rely on: the other party's promise alone, or the documented commitment of a bank? Each method in this lesson is a different answer to those two questions.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The main payment methods and the risk spectrum
BODY:
The major payment methods used in international commercial transactions are advance payment, open account, documentary collection, and the documentary letter of credit. They are best understood as points on a single spectrum, from the methods most favourable to the importer to those most favourable to the exporter.

From the exporter's point of view, the ladder of security looks like this.

1. Open account offers the least protection. The exporter ships and waits, trusting the buyer to pay later.
2. Documentary collections offer more. The exporter keeps control of the documents needed to claim the goods until the buyer pays or promises to pay, although the banks do not promise payment themselves.
3. The letter of credit offers more still. An issuing bank commits to pay when the exporter presents documents that comply with the credit.
4. Advance payment offers the most. The exporter holds the money before the goods leave.

From the importer's point of view, this order is exactly reversed. Advance payment is the most secure method for the exporter and the most exposing for the importer, because the buyer pays before the goods exist. Open account is the reverse: comfortable for the importer and riskiest for the exporter.

This is the central idea of the lesson: risk does not disappear as methods change; it shifts. Whatever protects one side exposes the other, like the two ends of a see-saw.

The bank-intermediated methods, documentary collections and letters of credit, occupy the middle of the spectrum because a neutral third party steps in: as a handler of documents in a collection, or as a bank that commits to pay in a letter of credit. That involvement adds cost and complexity, which generally rise towards the letter of credit end.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Advance payment: cash with order
BODY:
Advance payment is a payment arrangement in which the buyer pays the seller before goods are shipped or delivered. It is also known as cash in advance or cash with order, because the order is paid for when it is placed.

The sequence is straightforward.

1. The parties agree a sales contract requiring payment before shipment.
2. The buyer transfers the agreed funds to the seller.
3. Only when the money has arrived does the seller produce, pack, and ship the goods.

For the exporter, this is the safest arrangement available. Production costs are covered before a single drum leaves the factory, working capital is released rather than tied up, and the question of whether the buyer will pay effectively disappears.

For the importer, it is the most dangerous arrangement on the spectrum. The buyer pays before the goods exist. If the seller never produces the goods, produces them to a poor standard, or ships them late, the buyer has already lost its money and must try to recover it across a national border from the very party that caused the problem.

Advance payment appears mainly in three situations: where the seller's goods are in such demand that buyers accept the terms; where the parties are dealing for the first time and the seller refuses to carry any payment risk; and where the order is a low-value trial shipment where neither party wishes to incur bank charges.

The commercial weakness is obvious: most buyers resist paying a stranger upfront for goods they have not seen, so a seller in a competitive market often cannot demand it. Its value in this lesson is as a benchmark: the exporter's safest extreme, against which every other method is compared.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Open account: goods first, payment later
BODY:
Open account is a payment arrangement in which the seller supplies goods before receiving payment, usually within an agreed credit period. In practice the contract might read: payment 30 days after the invoice date, or 60 days from the bill of lading date. Such periods are common commercial examples; the exact period is what the parties negotiate.

The sequence is the mirror image of advance payment.

1. The parties agree a contract with a stated credit period.
2. The exporter ships the goods and sends the commercial documents to the buyer, or the goods travel with a non-negotiable transport document such as a sea waybill, which releases cargo to the named consignee without presentation of an original bill and suits trusted open-account trade.
3. The importer receives the goods, sells or uses them, and pays within the agreed period.

For the importer, open account is the most comfortable arrangement. Goods arrive before any money leaves, and the buyer may even sell the goods first and pay the seller from the proceeds, with no charges for payment security and minimal administration.

For the exporter, open account is the most exposing arrangement on the spectrum. The seller carries the full payment risk for the whole credit period: the buyer might pay late, pay partially, or not pay at all. The exporter is also, in effect, financing the buyer, because its own money is tied up in the goods until payment arrives.

Open account terms are common where the parties have a long, reliable trading history, where buyer and seller belong to the same corporate group, or where a competitive market forces the seller to offer credit to win the order. Selling on credit is normal domestic business; across borders, distance and weaker practical recourse multiply the risk the exporter accepts.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Documentary collections: how they operate
BODY:
A documentary collection is a payment arrangement in which banks handle commercial or financial documents according to collection instructions, without generally guaranteeing payment. Collections are governed by the International Chamber of Commerce's Uniform Rules for Collections, known as URC 522, in its 1995 revision.

The banks' role is often described with a simple image: under a collection, the banks are a postal service for documents. They pass the papers to the buyer under the exporter's instructions and forward the money back, but they never promise to pay; whether the exporter is paid depends entirely on the buyer's willingness and ability to pay or to accept a promise to pay.

A basic collection follows these steps.

1. The exporter ships the goods and gathers the documents: the commercial invoice, the transport document such as an ocean bill of lading, and any certificates the contract requires.
2. The exporter hands the documents to its own bank with a collection instruction stating exactly what must happen: release the documents only against payment or only against acceptance of a draft, and remit the proceeds.
3. The exporter's bank sends the documents to a bank in the buyer's country.
4. That bank contacts the buyer: the buyer must pay before receiving the documents, or accept a draft payable at a future date, depending on the instruction.
5. Once the buyer pays or accepts, the bank releases the documents.
6. The funds travel back along the banking chain to the exporter.

The power of a collection lies in control of the documents. An original ocean bill of lading is a document of title: whoever holds it controls the right to demand delivery of the goods. While the documents sit with the banks, the buyer cannot collect the cargo; the exporter gives up that control only when the buyer pays or accepts.

A collection therefore offers the exporter more protection than open account, but less than a letter of credit, because the banks supervise the exchange without committing to pay.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Documents against payment and documents against acceptance
BODY:
Two arrangements sit inside every documentary collection: documents against payment and documents against acceptance. Both rest on an instrument called the bill of exchange.

A bill of exchange is a written, unconditional order directing one party to pay a specified sum of money to another party, either on demand or at a determinable future time. In a collection, the exporter draws a bill of exchange on the buyer for the invoice amount. A draft payable on presentation is a sight draft; a draft payable at a fixed future date is a time draft.

Documents against payment (D/P) is a documentary collection arrangement in which specified documents are released to the buyer against payment. The rule is simple: pay to get the documents. The buyer transfers the money, the bank releases the papers, and only then can the buyer claim the goods from the carrier. Within the collection family, this is the safer arrangement for the exporter: cash arrives before the buyer ever touches the cargo.

Documents against acceptance (D/A) is a documentary collection arrangement in which specified documents are released to the buyer against acceptance of a time draft. The rule is: sign a promise to pay later, and take the documents now. The buyer accepts the time draft, a formal undertaking to pay at maturity, and uses the documents to collect the goods immediately, while the exporter waits.

The difference is between keeping the goods as leverage and giving them away on a promise. Under D/A the buyer can sell the cargo and pay from the proceeds, which makes it attractive to buyers and gives sellers a competitive edge. But if the buyer fails to pay at maturity, the goods are already gone, and the exporter is left pursuing an unpaid claim. D/A is therefore always riskier for the exporter than D/P.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The letter of credit: purpose, parties, and operation
BODY:
A letter of credit, often abbreviated to L/C and also called a documentary credit, is a bank's undertaking to honour a complying presentation of documents under the terms and conditions of the credit. Documentary credits are governed by the International Chamber of Commerce's Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits, UCP 600, which has applied since 1 July 2007 and contains 39 articles.

Two ideas in the definition make the letter of credit work.

1. It is an undertaking, not a promise from the buyer. If the beneficiary presents documents that comply with the credit, the issuing bank must pay. Credits are irrevocable, so the issuing bank cannot withdraw its commitment once the credit is issued (UCP 600 articles 2, 3, and 7).
2. It pays against documents, not goods. The credit is independent of the sales contract: the bank is not a party to it and takes no position on the goods (articles 4 and 5).

Four parties appear in a letter of credit transaction.

1. The applicant is the party requesting the issuance of the credit, usually the buyer or importer.
2. The issuing bank is the bank that issues the credit at the request of the applicant or on its own behalf.
3. The beneficiary is the party in whose favour the credit is issued, usually the seller or exporter.
4. The advising bank is the bank that advises the credit to the beneficiary, normally after checking its apparent authenticity.

The operation follows a recognisable sequence.

1. The sales contract states that payment will be by letter of credit.
2. The applicant applies to the issuing bank, which issues the credit in favour of the beneficiary.
3. The advising bank in the beneficiary's country checks the credit's apparent authenticity and advises it to the beneficiary.
4. The beneficiary checks the credit against the contract, produces the goods, and ships them.
5. The beneficiary presents the required documents within the validity of the credit.
6. The banks examine the documents; if the presentation complies, the issuing bank honours the credit and pays.

Letters of credit belong to the wider family of trade finance, which means the financial products and services used to facilitate domestic and international trade transactions.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Sight, usance, and confirmed letters of credit
BODY:
Letters of credit come in variants that change when payment happens and how secure it is.

1. Sight credit. The issuing bank pays as soon as a complying presentation of documents is made. The beneficiary waits only as long as the banks need to examine the documents.
2. Usance credit. Payment comes at a later maturity; usance credits are also called term, deferred-payment, or acceptance credits. A typical arrangement might set maturity 90 days after the bill of lading date, giving the buyer a credit period in which to receive, sell, or process the goods before paying; the figure here is illustrative, and the exact maturity is whatever the credit states. The beneficiary enjoys the same bank undertaking, but the cash arrives later.
3. Confirmed credit. Confirmation answers a different question: can the beneficiary trust the issuing bank itself? A confirmed credit is one where a second bank, usually in the exporter's country, adds its own undertaking to pay against complying documents. If the issuing bank fails to pay, perhaps because of difficulties in the buyer's country, the confirming bank must still honour a complying presentation. Confirmation is used when the buyer's country or bank risk is high; an unconfirmed credit relies only on the issuing bank.
4. Revolving credit. Where the same parties ship regularly, a revolving credit reinstates itself up to a set amount or period instead of being issued fresh for every shipment, saving the cost and delay of repeated issuance.

Each layer of protection carries a price: opening fees, confirmation fees, and the waiting time of usance. The exporter's decision is therefore not only about safety but about whether the order's margin can carry the cost of the protection.

The most protective common arrangement combines these features: a confirmed irrevocable sight credit, where two banks stand behind payment and payment comes as soon as the documents comply.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The roles banks play in international trade payments
BODY:
Banks appear throughout this lesson, and their roles deserve gathering in one place, because the role changes with the method.

1. Banks as payment channels. Under advance payment and open account, the banks simply move the money: they execute the buyer's transfer and credit the seller's account. They take no position on the transaction: if the goods disappoint or the payment never comes, the banks carry no responsibility.
2. Banks as document handlers. Under a documentary collection, the banks follow the collection instruction: they hold the documents, release them only against payment or acceptance, and remit the proceeds. They supervise the exchange of documents for money or promises, but they do not promise to pay.
3. Banks as committers of payment. Under a letter of credit, the issuing bank goes much further: it gives an irrevocable undertaking to honour a complying presentation. A confirming bank, where there is one, adds a second undertaking of its own. Here the bank's commitment is the whole point of the method.
4. Banks as authenticators. The advising bank checks the apparent authenticity of a credit before advising it to the beneficiary, so the exporter can have reasonable confidence that the instrument is genuine.

One principle governs every role: banks deal in documents, not goods. No bank in any of these arrangements inspects the cargo, tests the cocoa, or counts the drums; they check papers, match them against the credit or the collection instruction, and act on what the papers show.

The practical conclusion is to be clear about which role a bank is playing in any transaction. A bank that handles documents is not a bank that has committed money, and confusing the two roles is one of the costliest mistakes a new exporter can make.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Documents, discrepancies, and getting paid
BODY:
Because banks act on documents, the documentary flow is where international payments are won or lost.

The documents banks commonly act on include the following.

1. The commercial invoice, stating the parties, the goods, the price, and the terms of sale.
2. Transport documents, above all the ocean bill of lading. Banks acting under a letter of credit require a clean bill of lading, one without remarks about the condition of the goods; a claused bill showing defects will normally be rejected. The bill must also match the credit's requirements.
3. Insurance documents, where the credit requires them.
4. Certificates: a certificate of origin stating where the goods come from; phytosanitary or health certificates for products of plant or animal origin; and inspection certificates from an independent inspection company attesting quality, quantity, or condition at loading.

When documents are presented under a letter of credit, the banks examine them on their face, meaning on what the documents themselves state, within a maximum of five banking days (article 14). If the presentation does not comply with the credit, the bank is not obliged to pay, and it must give a single notice of refusal listing all the discrepancies (article 16).

A discrepancy is any failure of the documents to match the credit: an invoice amount different from the credit amount, a bill of lading that is not clean, a certificate naming a different party from the one the credit states, or documents presented after the credit has expired.

The consequence is stark: a small clerical error can stop payment even though the goods themselves are perfect. The bank pays documents, not goods, so document accuracy is the price of the undertaking. Experienced exporters read the credit on arrival, produce every document to match its exact wording, and cure any discrepancy while the credit remains valid. Module 6 of this course examines trade documents and document accuracy in detail.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Comparing and choosing a payment method
BODY:
No payment method is best in the abstract. The comparison works through four lenses at once.

1. Buyer risk. Advance payment is riskiest for the buyer and open account safest; collections sit in between, with D/A more exposing than D/P because the buyer receives the goods before paying. Under a letter of credit, the buyer's main exposure is paying against documents that may not reflect the true condition of the goods.
2. Seller risk. The exact mirror image: open account is riskiest for the seller and advance payment safest; collections sit in between; and a confirmed sight letter of credit is the closest a bank-intermediated method comes to eliminating non-payment risk for the exporter, at the cost of fees and strict documentary compliance.
3. Cost. A plain transfer under advance payment or open account is cheapest; a documentary collection carries moderate bank fees; a letter of credit is generally the most expensive to operate, and confirmation adds a further layer.
4. Complexity. Open account and advance payment need little paperwork beyond the commercial documents; collections add the collection instruction and drafts; letters of credit demand the most skill, because every document must match the credit precisely.

The factors that influence the selection of an appropriate method include the following.

1. The history and trust between the parties: a first transaction argues for more protection; years of clean performance argue for simpler terms.
2. Knowledge of the buyer's country and the soundness of its banks, which drives the demand for confirmation.
3. Bargaining power: a seller with scarce goods can insist on advance payment; a buyer with many alternative suppliers can demand open account.
4. The value of the order and the seller's cash-flow needs.
5. Willingness to pay bank fees and to manage documentary detail.

Finally, place payment within the contract as a whole. The previous lesson, Incoterms and Allocation of Responsibilities, introduced Incoterms 2020, the ICC rules stating who arranges carriage, who pays which costs, and where risk passes. Those rules deliberately do not govern payment. One sales contract sets both: it may name the Incoterms 2020 rule CIF and require payment by a confirmed irrevocable letter of credit. The Incoterm answers delivery, costs, and transit risk; the payment method answers when the money moves and on whose promise.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A first-time exporter secures payment with a confirmed letter of credit
BODY:
A company managed by Adwoa produces shea butter near Tamale. A cosmetics ingredients distributor in Rotterdam places a first order: a container of shea butter in drums, shipped from Tema Port, worth 90,000 US dollars. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

The sales contract names the Incoterms 2020 rule CIF Rotterdam and requires payment by a confirmed irrevocable letter of credit payable at sight. Adwoa insists on this because the parties have never traded: the buyer refuses to pay in advance, and she refuses open account with a stranger. The letter of credit is the middle ground where a bank's undertaking replaces trust.

Step 1: The sales contract. The parties agree the goods, price, documents, and payment by confirmed irrevocable sight letter of credit.

Step 2: Issuance. The buyer, as applicant, applies to its bank, which issues the credit in favour of Adwoa's company, the beneficiary.

Step 3: Advice and confirmation. A bank in Ghana checks the credit's apparent authenticity and advises it to Adwoa. Because this is her first export and she knows little about the foreign issuing bank, she has required confirmation: the Ghanaian bank adds its own undertaking to pay against complying documents.

Step 4: Review before production. Adwoa checks every term of the credit against the contract before producing the goods; any term she cannot meet must be corrected now, by amendment, not after shipment.

Step 5: Shipment and documents. The drums travel by road to Tema Port for shipping to Rotterdam. She obtains a clean on-board ocean bill of lading, issues a commercial invoice matching the credit exactly, and obtains a certificate of origin and packing list.

Step 6: Presentation and a discrepancy. Her bank forwards the documents. Within the five banking days allowed for examination, a discrepancy is found: the certificate of origin names the buyer by its trading name, while the credit states its full registered name. A single notice of refusal lists every discrepancy.

Step 7: Correction and payment. While the credit remains valid, Adwoa obtains a corrected certificate and re-presents. The presentation complies, and the issuing bank honours the credit at sight, with the confirming bank standing behind that payment.

What does this example demonstrate?

The credit protected Adwoa because it replaced reliance on an unknown buyer with a bank's undertaking; but it paid only because the documents complied. A certificate with the wrong name nearly stopped a perfect shipment, and the banks never inspected the butter, only the papers.

Key lesson: for a first-time exporter, the letter of credit shifts payment risk away from the buyer; compliant documents are the price of collecting that protection.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Trust grows: from confirmed credit to open account
BODY:
A manufacturer of industrial spare parts in Mumbai has supplied a maintenance company in Hamburg for several years. The same pair of companies can sit at different points of the payment spectrum at different stages of their relationship. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Stage 1: First orders, a confirmed irrevocable sight letter of credit. The parties are strangers. The Hamburg buyer applies to its bank, the credit is issued in favour of the Mumbai manufacturer, and the manufacturer requires confirmation by its own bank in India, because it knows neither the buyer nor the buyer's bank. Each side's risk is contained: the supplier will be paid against complying documents by two banks, and the buyer knows its money leaves only when documents prove shipment.

Stage 2: After years of clean performance, a documentary collection with documents against payment. Letters of credit have cost fees on every order, so the parties move to D/P. The supplier keeps control of the documents, and therefore of the goods, until Hamburg pays. What the supplier gives up is real: the banks promise nothing, so if the buyer refuses to pay, the cargo sits at destination and the supplier must negotiate. What it gains is the saving of credit fees and simpler administration, on the strength of the buyer's now-proven record.

Stage 3: Deeper trust, documents against acceptance. To help the buyer's cash flow, the supplier ships under D/A with a time draft payable 60 days after sight. Hamburg takes the documents, installs the parts, and pays at maturity from its revenues. The supplier now extends credit across a border with no bank undertaking: the buyer's signature on the draft is the security.

Stage 4: Established partnership, open account. Payment is now due 30 days after invoice. There are no collection charges and no drafts. The supplier carries the full payment risk, but carries it knowingly, with the relationship's history as the real protection.

What does this example demonstrate?

At every step, risk moved from one party to the other. The confirmed credit placed almost all payment risk on the buyer's side of the see-saw; open account places almost all of it on the seller's. Each move traded security for lower cost or better competitiveness, and was possible only because performance had been proven.

Key lesson: payment terms are not fixed for life. They are adjusted as trust is earned, and each adjustment deliberately re-divides payment risk between exporter and importer.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: The importer's dilemma: paying before the goods exist
BODY:
A pharmaceutical distributor in Accra needs a consignment of products from a manufacturer near Mumbai with which it has never traded. The quotation carries a condition: full advance payment before production begins, for an order worth 50,000 US dollars. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: The demand. The manufacturer wants cash with order: the distributor is a new customer in another country, and the manufacturer refuses to produce and ship against a stranger's promise to pay.

Step 2: The distributor's fears. From the buyer's chair, advance payment concentrates every risk on one side: the distributor would pay before the goods exist. If the manufacturer never produces, produces the wrong specification, or ships months late, the money is already gone, and recovering it means pursuing a foreign company under foreign procedures, while the advance has drained the distributor's working capital.

Step 3: The counter-proposal. The distributor proposes an irrevocable letter of credit available at sight. Its own bank becomes the issuing bank, undertaking to pay only when the beneficiary presents documents complying with the credit. The credit requires a clean on-board ocean bill of lading, a commercial invoice, a certificate of origin, and a certificate of inspection from an independent inspection company attesting the quality and quantity of the products at loading.

Step 4: Why this answers the fear. The distributor's money does not leave before the goods exist: payment becomes due only against documents proving the goods have been produced, inspected, and shipped on board. Paying before the goods exist has become paying when documents prove shipment.

Step 5: The remaining exposure. The credit does not make the distributor safe from everything. The banks will never open the cartons; they pay documents, not goods. If the manufacturer ships products that satisfy the documents but disappoint in reality, remedies lie in the sales contract, not with any bank. The inspection certificate matters because it places an independent examination between the goods and the payment, reducing, though never eliminating, this risk.

Step 6: Agreement and completion. The manufacturer accepts: an issuing bank's undertaking is stronger security than a new customer's promise. The credit is issued, the goods are produced, inspected, and shipped, the documents comply, and payment follows at sight.

What does this example demonstrate?

Seen from the importer's side, the spectrum inverts: advance payment, the exporter's safest point, is the importer's most dangerous. A letter of credit does not remove the risk but shares it: the buyer pays only against proof of shipment; the seller is paid against documents rather than a promise.

Key lesson: whichever party moves first carries the risk, and the chosen payment method decides who moves first.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: A letter of credit is not a guarantee
BODY:
The most persistent mistake in trade finance is to describe a letter of credit as a guarantee of payment, or worse, as a guarantee of the trade. It is neither.

A letter of credit is an undertaking to pay against a complying presentation of documents. Every word matters. The undertaking belongs to banks, not to the transaction as a whole, and applies only when the documents comply. A letter of credit protects the seller only if the documents are right: the bank pays against documents, not against the actual goods.

It is equally wrong to expect the credit to protect the quality of the trade itself. The banks never inspect the cargo. Documents can comply perfectly while the goods are late, damaged, short-shipped, or not what the buyer imagined. If a beneficiary ships poor goods with impeccable papers, the banks must still pay, and the buyer's remedies lie in the sales contract against the seller, not against the bank. Buyers who need independent assurance arrange inspection certificates, which the credit can require: a protection the parties design into the transaction, not something the credit itself provides.

Do not confuse the documentary credit with the standby letter of credit, a guarantee-like instrument paid only when a stated default or non-performance occurs, against a stated document. The standby is not a payment mechanism for the goods; it sits behind a transaction as backstop security, whereas the documentary credit is the engine that pays for the goods in the normal course of the trade.

The precise vocabulary protects real money: an undertaking against compliant documents, yes; a guarantee of the trade, never.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: A bank in a documentary collection has not promised to pay
BODY:
A new exporter hears that a bank is involved in the payment and concludes the money is safe. Under a documentary collection, that conclusion is wrong.

Under URC 522, the banks in a collection handle commercial or financial documents according to collection instructions without generally guaranteeing payment. They hold the papers, contact the buyer, release documents against payment or acceptance, and remit what they receive. They never step into the buyer's shoes. If the buyer refuses to pay under D/P, the banks will not pay from their own funds; they advise the exporter and act on the collection instruction. If the buyer accepts a time draft under D/A and then fails to pay at maturity, the banks still have not promised anything.

A collection buys supervision and document control, not payment security. Document control is worth having: the buyer cannot collect the goods without paying or accepting. But if the buyer declines, the exporter may face cargo sitting at a destination port far away, and settlement must come from the buyer or from the contract's terms.

This is the clean dividing line of the spectrum. Under a collection, the banks are a postal service for documents and never promise to pay. Under a letter of credit, the issuing bank does commit, but only against documents that comply exactly. Whenever a bank's involvement is described, the practical question is always the same: has this bank undertaken to pay, and against what?

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Payment terms are commercial choices, not customs or legal requirements
BODY:
Learners sometimes assume some authority prescribes how international traders must pay one another. No such rule exists. The payment method is whatever the parties negotiate into their sales contract: advance payment, open account, documentary collection, or letter of credit. The choice is a commercial decision reflecting bargaining power, trust, cost, and risk appetite.

Customs procedures do not choose or police the payment method. Customs authorities concern themselves with the goods crossing the border: declaration, classification, valuation, and applicable controls. How and when the buyer pays the seller is not a customs question.

Nor is any single method superior. Advance payment kills a sale where the buyer holds the bargaining power; open account is reckless with an unknown buyer and routine with a proven one. Parties also blend arrangements: part of the contract value paid in advance and the balance against documents is a common negotiated compromise, because payment terms are flexible commercial material.

Two habits follow. First, write the payment method explicitly into the sales contract, alongside the Incoterms 2020 rule, so that delivery and payment responsibilities are both recorded and do not contradict each other. Second, revisit the method as the relationship develops: the method that suits a first shipment need not survive the tenth. Payment terms are negotiated instruments of risk allocation, and treating them as fixed formalities gives away either protection or competitiveness without noticing.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
An exporter in Tema has agreed to sell machinery parts to a buyer in another country that it has never dealt with before. The parties want a payment method under which the exporter ships the goods before payment, but the exporter's payment is supported by a bank's undertaking rather than by the buyer's promise alone.

Which option best fits this requirement?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Open account terms with payment due 30 days after the invoice date.
2. A documentary collection with documents against acceptance (D/A).
3. A confirmed irrevocable letter of credit payable at sight.
4. Full advance payment before the goods are shipped.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

Under a letter of credit, the issuing bank gives an irrevocable undertaking to honour a complying presentation of documents, and confirmation adds a second bank's undertaking, usually in the exporter's country. The exporter ships before payment and relies on banks, not on the buyer's promise alone.

Option 1 fails the requirement because open account leaves payment resting entirely on the buyer's willingness and ability to pay during the credit period; no bank undertaking exists.

Option 2 fails because the banks in a documentary collection handle documents without generally guaranteeing payment, and under D/A the buyer obtains the documents and the goods merely by accepting a promise to pay at maturity.

Option 4 would be very safe for the exporter, but it contradicts the stated requirement that the exporter ships before payment, and it is the method buyers most often refuse.

The takeaway: when goods must move first and payment must rest on a bank's undertaking, the letter of credit is the method designed for exactly that situation.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
An exporter in Kumasi ships goods to an overseas buyer under a documentary collection with documents against acceptance (D/A). The buyer accepts the time draft, receives the documents from the bank, and collects the goods from the port.

Which statement best describes the exporter's position?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The exporter has been paid and now bears no further payment risk.
2. The banks handling the collection have guaranteed that the draft will be paid at maturity.
3. The buyer can hold the goods before payment is due, and the exporter now relies on the buyer paying at maturity.
4. The buyer will not be able to collect the goods until the draft has actually been paid in cash.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

Under documents against acceptance, the documents are released to the buyer against acceptance of a time draft. Acceptance is a formal promise to pay a stated sum at a determinable future time; it is not money. The buyer can therefore obtain the documents, collect the goods, and even sell them before maturity, while the exporter waits for payment. This is precisely why D/A is riskier for the exporter than D/P, where cash must be paid before the documents are released.

Option 2 fails because under URC 522 the banks in a documentary collection handle documents according to collection instructions without generally guaranteeing payment; no such promise exists.

Option 4 describes documents against payment, not documents against acceptance.

Option 1 ignores the credit period still running; nothing has been paid yet.

The takeaway: D/P means pay to get the documents; D/A means sign a promise to pay later to get the documents, and the exporter carries the risk that the promise is not kept.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A distributor in Accra imports a bottling machine under a letter of credit. The issuing bank pays against a complying presentation of documents. When the machine arrives, it is found to be poorly built, although every document was perfectly in order.

Which statement best explains this outcome?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The issuing bank failed in its duty because it should have inspected the machinery before paying.
2. The letter of credit should have guaranteed the quality of the goods as well as the payment.
3. The payment was invalid because banks are not permitted to pay when the goods are defective.
4. Banks deal in documents, not goods, so the bank's undertaking depended on the presentation of documents and did not extend to the condition of the machinery.
CORRECT: 4
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 4.

A letter of credit is independent of the sales contract (UCP 600 articles 4 and 5). The banks examined the documents on their face, found a complying presentation, and honoured the credit exactly as its terms required. Whether the machinery inside the crate matches the documents is not a question the banks investigate; they never inspect the goods.

Option 1 fails because inspecting cargo is not a bank function under any payment method; banks check papers, not products.

Option 2 fails because a letter of credit is not a guarantee of the trade. It ensures payment against compliant documents and says nothing about quality. A guarantee-like instrument, the standby letter of credit, is a different tool that pays on a stated default and is not a payment mechanism for goods.

Option 3 fails because payment against complying documents is precisely what the credit required; the outcome is frustrating for the buyer, but the payment itself was correct.

The takeaway: document perfection and goods perfection are different things; a buyer protects quality through the sales contract and independent inspection certificates, not through the letter of credit.

=== FACTS & SOURCES ===
- The main international payment methods are advance payment, open account, documentary collection, and documentary credit; advance payment is safest for the exporter and riskiest for the importer, and open account is the reverse — source: research sheet trade-transport-standards.md, section 2 (payment methods and risk allocation).
- Documentary collections are governed by the ICC Uniform Rules for Collections (URC 522, 1995 revision); banks handle documents against payment or acceptance but give no undertaking to pay, and payment depends entirely on the buyer — source: research sheet section 2.
- D/P means documents are released to the buyer against payment, safer for the exporter; D/A means documents are released against acceptance of a time draft, with payment at maturity, riskier for the exporter because the buyer obtains the goods before paying — source: research sheet section 2.
- Letters of credit are governed by ICC UCP 600 (Publication No. 600), effective 1 July 2007, in 39 articles; a credit is an irrevocable undertaking by the issuing bank to pay against a complying presentation (articles 2, 3, 7) — source: research sheet section 2.
- The credit is independent of the sales contract; banks deal in documents, not goods (articles 4 and 5); banks examine documents on their face within a maximum of five banking days (article 14) and must give a single notice of refusal listing all discrepancies (article 16) — source: research sheet section 2.
- A sight credit pays when complying documents are presented; usance/term/deferred-payment/acceptance credits pay at a later maturity (90 days after bill of lading date used as an illustrative example) — source: research sheet section 2.
- A confirmed credit is one where a second bank, usually in the exporter's country, adds its own undertaking, used when the buyer's country/bank risk is high; an unconfirmed credit relies only on the issuing bank — source: research sheet section 2.
- A revolving credit reinstates itself up to a set amount/period instead of being re-issued per shipment — source: research sheet section 2.
- A standby letter of credit is a guarantee-like instrument paid on stated default/non-performance against a stated document, and is not a payment mechanism for the goods themselves — source: research sheet section 2.
- A confirmed sight letter of credit is the closest to eliminating non-payment risk for the exporter, at the cost of bank fees and strict documentary compliance — source: research sheet section 2.
- An ocean bill of lading is a receipt for the goods, evidence of the contract of carriage, and a document of title; possession of a duly endorsed bill controls the right to delivery — source: research sheet section 4 (transport documents).
- Banks under a letter of credit require a clean bill of lading; a claused bill shows defects — source: research sheet section 4.
- A sea waybill is non-negotiable, is not a document of title, releases cargo to the named consignee without presentation of an original, and suits trusted open-account trade — source: research sheet section 4.
- Incoterms 2020 allocate transport tasks, costs and risk only; they do not govern transfer of ownership/title, payment terms, or breach remedies — source: research sheet section 1.
- Non-preferential certificates of origin are traditionally certified by chambers of commerce under ICC World Chambers Federation guidelines — source: research sheet section 5.
- Phytosanitary certificates cover plants and plant products; sanitary/health certificates cover products of animal origin — source: research sheet section 5.
- Third-party or buyer-mandated inspection certificates attesting quality, quantity or condition at loading are common in commodity and bulk trades; their contents are contractual, not standardised — source: research sheet section 5.
- A letter of credit is generally more costly and administratively complex than a documentary collection, which in turn costs more than a plain transfer — source: general business knowledge.
- Credit periods such as 30 or 60 days after invoice (open account) and 60 days after sight (D/A draft) are illustrative commercial examples; actual periods are whatever the parties agree — source: illustrative figures for teaching purposes; open-account credit-period example per research sheet section 2.
- The payment method is a commercial negotiation between the parties and is not prescribed by customs authorities, whose concerns are declaration, classification and valuation of goods — source: general business knowledge.
