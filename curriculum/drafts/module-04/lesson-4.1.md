=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Why countries trade
BODY:
International trade is the exchange of goods and services between individuals, businesses, or organisations located in different countries. Course 1 looked at international logistics, the movement of goods across borders; this lesson turns to the trade that drives those movements: the forms it takes, the parties involved, and the formalities each form requires.

Countries trade with each other for three basic reasons.

1. Specialisation. Every country concentrates on producing the goods it is suited for, by climate, natural resources, skills, and industry. Ghana's position as a producer of cocoa, gold, timber, and shea products, and its reliance on imported rice, machinery, vehicles, and pharmaceuticals, illustrate the pattern; both lists are illustrative examples, not statistics.

2. Availability. Some goods cannot be produced locally in useful quantities, or cannot be produced at all. A country without the climate for a crop, the minerals for a metal, or the plants for a medicine must obtain those goods from abroad or do without them.

3. Price. Even where a good could be produced locally, producing it in another country can be cheaper. Buying from the cheaper source frees money for other uses.

Trade matters at two levels. For national economies, exports earn the income that pays for imports, trade supports employment in farms, factories, ports, and transport, and duties on legitimate trade are a source of government revenue. For businesses, trade widens the market beyond the national border: a seller reaches customers abroad, and a buyer sources better or cheaper inputs.

Every cross-border transaction has two sides at once. From the country receiving the goods, the movement is an import; from the country sending them, it is an export. Some goods cross a country without stopping at all, a third form, transit trade. The next blocks define each form, because the differences decide who does what, where, and at what cost.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Customs territories, country of origin, and country of destination
BODY:
Three terms locate any trade transaction on the map.

A customs territory is a geographical area within which a particular set of customs laws applies. A customs territory usually follows a country's borders, but not always: a group of countries can share one customs territory with a single set of external customs rules, which is why the term exists separately from the country itself.

The country of origin is the country in which goods are considered to have originated under applicable rules of origin. Origin is where the goods were grown, produced, or substantially transformed, not where they were shipped from: rice grown and milled in Thailand keeps its Thai origin even if carried first to Singapore and shipped onward from there. Origin matters because duties, trade statistics, and preferential treatment are all decided against it.

The country of destination is the country to which the goods are intended to be delivered. The documents of every shipment name both: the origin, which the certificates attest, and the destination, where the goods will be entered.

The line that matters most in practice is the boundary of the customs territory. While goods are inside their own territory they move under one set of rules. The moment they cross outward, the exporting territory's formalities apply before they may leave. The moment they cross inward anywhere, they come under the control of that territory's customs authority, and they remain under that control until the territory's rules for them are completed, whether those rules are import, export, transit, or another customs procedure. Which procedure applies is not decided by the goods or by geography alone; it is decided by what the goods are declared for, the subject of the blocks that follow.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Importing and the importer
BODY:
An import is the bringing of goods into a country's customs territory from another country or territory, subject to applicable customs laws. The importer is the person or organisation responsible for bringing goods into a country in accordance with applicable import requirements.

From the importer's perspective, the definition becomes a business activity: importing means buying goods from abroad for use or resale in the importer's own market. A trading company buying rice for its shelves, a construction firm buying excavators for its sites, a pharmacy wholesaler buying medicines for hospitals, all are importing.

The defining feature of an import is entry for home consumption. Goods that import are declared to customs as goods that will stay in the country's economy: they will be sold, used, or processed there. On that entry the country assesses the duties and taxes its law provides, and once released the goods become part of the local market.

The importer carries the responsibilities on the destination side of the transaction.

1. Ensuring the import declaration is accurate: the goods, their value, their origin, and their classification.

2. Paying, or arranging payment of, the duties and taxes assessed on the import, where they apply.

3. Meeting any import requirements that apply to the goods, such as permits, standards, or additional certificates for restricted or regulated products.

4. Organising or paying for arrival handling and inland delivery, according to the trade terms agreed in the sales contract, a subject the next lessons take up.

Importing is not only for traders: manufacturers import raw materials and spare parts, and farms import fertiliser and equipment. Wherever goods are brought into the customs territory to be used or consumed there, the movement is an import.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The import process and customs formalities
BODY:
The previous module traced a journey of goods from order to delivery. This block revisits only the importer's side of that journey, where the trade type shows. The basic process of importing runs as follows.

1. Before ordering. The importer checks what the goods require: whether a permit or certificate is needed, what the classification means for duty, and who, under the agreed trade terms, is responsible for each stage.

2. Shipment and documents. The seller ships, and the commercial and transport documents, invoice, packing list, transport document, and certificates, begin their journey towards the importer's side.

3. Pre-arrival declaration. Modern practice lodges the customs declaration and its data before the goods arrive, letting customs analyse the shipment's risk in advance and release goods much faster on arrival.

4. The customs declaration. A customs declaration is an official statement submitted to customs providing the information required for a particular customs procedure. The import declaration describes the goods, their value, their origin, and their classification, and is the basis on which customs assesses duties and taxes, usually lodged by the importer or a customs representative.

5. Risk assessment and inspection. Customs does not inspect everything: risk-based selection channels declarations, releasing some on the documents alone, sending some to a document check, and physically inspecting only those presenting a risk.

6. Payment and release. When the declaration is in order and the assessed duties and taxes are paid, customs releases the goods from its control.

7. Collection and delivery. The released goods are collected from the port, airport, or border and delivered to the importer's premises.

Formalities are increasingly lodged through a single window: one entry point through which a trader submits standardised information and documents once, to satisfy all import, export, and transit regulatory requirements, instead of repeating them to each agency. In Ghana, the trade single window operates through ICUMS, the national customs management system, which processes declarations electronically at the country's ports of entry.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Exporting and the exporter
BODY:
An export is the sending of goods from one country or customs territory to another. The exporter is the person or organisation that sends or sells goods to a destination outside its country or customs territory.

From the perspective of the exporter, exporting means selling goods abroad: finding a buyer in another country, agreeing terms, and delivering the goods across the border in the condition and quantity the contract promises. The farm cooperative selling pineapples to a European distributor and the processor selling cocoa products to a chocolate manufacturer are both exporting.

An export is the mirror of an import: the same movement is an export from the sending country and an import into the receiving country, and each side runs its own formalities. The exporter's formalities are those of the origin country, completed before the goods leave: an export declaration describing the goods, any certificates the goods require, and compliance with any export controls. In most international sales it is the seller's side that clears the goods for export; the contract's trade terms, commonly expressed using the Incoterms 2020 rules, fix this allocation, and this module returns to them in its lesson on Incoterms.

The exporter's typical responsibilities are these.

1. Preparing goods to the quality, quantity, and packing the contract requires.

2. Lodging an accurate export declaration and obtaining the required certificates, such as proof of origin or health certificates.

3. Handing the goods to the international carrier on time, against the carrier's cut-off.

4. Providing the buyer's side with the documents it needs to clear the goods at destination.

Exporting is how the specialisation described at the start of this lesson becomes income: a country's cocoa, gold, timber, and shea earn nothing abroad until some organisation exports them, correctly documented and on time.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The export process
BODY:
The basic process of exporting runs as follows.

1. Order and terms. A foreign buyer's order is confirmed, and the parties agree the commercial terms: the goods, the price, the delivery arrangements, and who is responsible for each stage. Those terms belong to the next lesson; here, they decide what the exporter must do.

2. Preparation. The goods are produced or processed to the contract's specification, packed for a long international journey, counted, and marked so that every package is identifiable.

3. Certificates. The exporter obtains whatever the goods require: a certificate of origin where the buyer or the destination's rules need one, plant-health or veterinary certificates for products of plant or animal origin, and the quality or inspection certificates common in commodity trades.

4. Export declaration. An export declaration is lodged with the customs authority of the exporting country, usually by the exporter or its freight forwarder, and customs releases the goods for export when it is in order.

5. Handover. The goods reach the port, airport, or border before the carrier's cut-off and are handed to the international carrier. For containers, the shipper's verified gross mass must have been declared before loading; a container without it may not be loaded.

6. Main carriage and documents. The goods travel to the destination country, and the exporter sends the buyer the documents needed there: the transport document, invoice, certificates, and packing list.

7. The buyer's formalities. Import clearance, duties, and taxes at destination are the importing side's work, not the exporter's, unless the agreed terms say otherwise.

Some goods carry extra conditions on the way out. Governments restrict or prohibit the export of certain categories, for reasons of safety, security, or domestic supply, and an exporter must confirm early that its goods may lawfully leave; discovering a restriction after the contract is signed is an expensive way to learn the rule.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Transit trade and why it exists
BODY:
Transit trade is the movement of goods through a country or customs territory under applicable transit arrangements, generally without the goods being entered for domestic consumption. The transit country is neither the origin nor the destination; the goods are passing through.

The need for transit follows from geography. Not every country has a seaport: in West Africa, several countries inland from the coast, such as Burkina Faso, Mali, and Niger, have none of their own, so overseas goods must arrive at a coastal port and continue across one or more neighbouring countries. A container of machinery discharged at Tema and bound for a buyer in Ouagadougou must cross Ghana to reach its market.

That movement creates a question. The goods will never be sold or used in Ghana, so should Ghana treat them as imports and assess duties on them? That would charge duties twice, once in the passing country and again at the real destination, and make coastal ports useless to landlocked neighbours. The answer is the transit arrangement: the goods cross under customs control, without being entered for consumption, and the duties they owe are collected where they will actually be consumed.

Transit trade has four defining characteristics.

1. No entry for home consumption. The goods never join the transit country's market.

2. Duties suspended. Import duties are not paid in the transit country; they are secured by a guarantee and collected at the destination.

3. Control continues. The goods remain under customs control across the whole journey: sealed, documented, and moved along prescribed routes and offices.

4. The procedure closes at the destination. The transit ends where the goods are entered for their real destination, and the guarantee is released there.

Transit still benefits the transit country: its ports, corridors, hauliers, and warehouses earn work from goods passing through, even though its treasury collects no import duties on them.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: How a transit movement is controlled
BODY:
A transit arrangement works because four ingredients travel with the goods.

1. A single transit document. Lodged at the office of entry, it describes the goods, their origin and destination, and the route and offices of the journey, and the driver presents it at each customs office instead of a new declaration being made in every country.

2. A customs seal. The load is sealed at the office of departure, and an intact seal shows every later office that the goods have not been opened along the way.

3. A guarantee. A guarantee covers the duties at risk, so that if the goods disappear into the local market of a passing country, its customs can recover the duty from the guarantor; it is released when the transit is properly closed at destination.

4. Prescribed routes and time limits. The movement follows the approved route and schedule, presenting at the offices named in the document.

West Africa operates its own scheme on these lines: the Inter-State Road Transit Convention, the ISRT, adopted in 1982 by ECOWAS member states. Goods move by road between member states under duty suspension, sealed, covered by a single transit document known as the triptyque, and backed by a guarantee system. Ghana applies the ISRT for transit cargo to and from its landlocked neighbours.

The idea is not unique to West Africa. The international TIR Convention works the same way across Europe, Central Asia, and beyond: goods travel in sealed vehicles or containers under a TIR carnet, duties suspended across multiple borders, backed by an international guarantee chain. Ghana is not a party to the TIR Convention, which is why the regional ISRT is the scheme that matters for Tema's transit corridors.

Charges differ from import in the expected direction: the transit country collects no import duties, but the movement carries administrative requirements, which vary by country: customs processing fees at the offices of entry and exit, escort or convoy charges on supervised corridors, road tolls, and terminal handling charges at the ports and border posts along the way. The responsible party keeps the seal and documents in order until the procedure closes. The fees are usually modest beside the duties suspended, but they belong in a corridor budget.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The major participants in an international trade transaction
BODY:
Course 1 mapped the logistics ecosystem; this block places its participants around one trade transaction, because importing, exporting, and transit all draw on the same cast.

The two principals come first.

1. The exporter or seller, who sends the goods and is normally responsible for clearing them for export in the origin country.

2. The importer or buyer, who receives the goods and is normally responsible for clearing them for import and paying the duties and taxes at destination.

Around them stand the supporting participants.

3. Freight forwarders arrange the shipment on behalf of either principal: booking carriage, organising inland transport and handovers, and preparing documentation.

4. Carriers move the goods: shipping lines, airlines, road hauliers, and rail operators, with port and terminal operators handling cargo between vehicle and vessel.

5. Customs representatives prepare declarations and manage the formalities with the customs authorities of each territory.

6. Banks and insurers support the money and the risk: banks handle payment between the parties, the module's final lesson, and insurers cover goods in transit.

7. Government agencies beyond customs set requirements the goods must meet: plant-protection and veterinary authorities for food and agricultural goods, standards and health authorities for regulated products.

In a transit movement the same cast appears with one addition: the customs administrations of every country crossed, each office checking the seal and the transit document, linked by the guarantee until the destination office closes the procedure.

Two observations follow. First, no participant performs the whole transaction; each is responsible for a stage, and the goods depend on all of them. Second, every participant works from documents and data rather than from the goods themselves: a wrong origin on a certificate can change the duty, and a wrong weight can stop a loading.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Common documents in cross-border trade
BODY:
Course 1 introduced the main shipping documents; this block groups them by what they do, and shows which trade type calls for which.

Commercial documents state what is being sold and shipped.

1. The commercial invoice, from seller to buyer, states the goods, quantities, prices, and terms of sale.

2. The packing list details the packages, weights, and contents, so every party can check what was shipped against what should have been.

Transport documents record the carriage.

3. The ocean bill of lading is the carrier's receipt for the goods, evidence of the contract of carriage, and a document of title: possessing it controls the right to claim delivery.

4. The air waybill plays the same recording role in air cargo but is never negotiable: it is a receipt and evidence of the contract, not a document of title.

5. International road movements in West Africa travel under a national or ECOWAS consignment note; the European CMR note is not the region's document.

Regulatory documents satisfy the authorities.

6. Customs declarations: an import declaration for goods entering for consumption, an export declaration for goods leaving, and, for transit, the single transit document such as the ISRT triptyque.

7. Certificates of origin state where the goods come from. The non-preferential kind is routine paperwork supporting ordinary duty treatment; the preferential kind, such as the ECOWAS certificate under the ETLS or the AfCFTA certificate, unlocks reduced or duty-free entry under a trade agreement.

8. Health and plant-health certificates: a phytosanitary certificate covers plants and plant products, a sanitary certificate products of animal origin, each issued by the exporting country's competent authority.

9. Permits and licences for restricted goods, on either the import or the export side.

Matching documents to trade type is the practical skill: the importer's file centres on the import declaration and duty assessment, the exporter's on the export declaration and certificates, the transit movement's on one transit document, one seal, and one guarantee.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Trade barriers, logistics, and the challenges of cross-border trade
BODY:
A trade barrier is a government-imposed measure, such as a tariff, quota, or regulatory restriction, that affects international trade. Barriers explain why cross-border trade is never just transport: each one is a rule to satisfy and a cost or limit to plan around. The main forms are tariffs, or customs duties, charged at the border on imported goods; quotas, limits on the quantity of a good that may enter or leave during a period; and regulatory restrictions, the standards, licences, and prohibitions that protect health, safety, and public policy.

Duties depend on classification, which is where the Harmonized System enters: the worldwide naming scheme giving every traded product a six-digit code, more than five thousand of them, which countries extend with their own extra digits. Ghana applies the ECOWAS Common External Tariff, under which goods fall into five duty bands: 0, 5, 10, 20, and 35 per cent. The band a product attracts depends on its classification and, for preferential treatment, its origin, which is why origin documents carry financial weight and classification errors surface as money.

Governments impose barriers to protect developing industries, raise revenue, and safeguard health and safety.

Trade and logistics depend on each other in both directions. Trade creates logistics work: imports mean port handling, clearance, and inland delivery; exports mean consolidation, export clearance, and a handover to a carrier; transit movements mean corridor haulage and seal control across several customs offices. Logistics makes trade possible: goods reach foreign buyers competitively only when ports, corridors, and clearance work, so logistics performance is part of a country's competitiveness.

The recurring challenges of international goods movement follow from the lesson.

1. Documentation errors, which stop goods exactly where they are most expensive to hold.

2. Congestion and delay at ports, airports, and border crossings.

3. Trade barriers adding cost, paperwork, and time.

4. Long transit corridors, with seals, schedules, and several administrations to satisfy.

5. Coordination across two principals, several service providers, and two or more governments.

6. Payment risk across borders, the subject of this module's final lesson.

Managing them, through accurate documents, early declarations, and reliable partners, is the work of the rest of this course.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Importing in practice: a container of rice arrives at Tema
BODY:
A food-trading company in Accra buys rice for the Ghanaian market. It orders a full container load of white rice from an exporter in Thailand: about 560 fifty-kilogram bags, roughly 28 tonnes, a load suited to a twenty-foot container. All quantities and figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: The order. The companies agree the goods, the quality, the quantity, the price, and the delivery terms. The trade type is fixed from the start: a sale for import into Ghana, because the rice is bought for resale in the Ghanaian market and will be entered for home consumption there.

Step 2: Origin formalities in Thailand. The exporter packs the bags on pallets, stuffs and seals the container, and completes Thailand's export formalities: the export declaration and the documents the sale needs, including the commercial invoice, packing list, certificate of origin, and the phytosanitary certificate that plant products commonly carry. The container's verified gross mass is declared before loading.

Step 3: Export clearance and loading. Thai customs clears the container for export, and the shipping line loads it within the cargo and documentation cut-offs, issuing a bill of lading.

Step 4: Main carriage. The container sails to Tema Port.

Step 5: Pre-arrival. Before the vessel arrives, the importer's customs representative lodges the import declaration through Ghana's single window, ICUMS, describing the goods, their value, their Thai origin, and their classification. Customs risk-assesses it in advance.

Step 6: Arrival and channel. On arrival the declaration is channelled according to risk: released on the documents, or sent to a document check or physical inspection.

Step 7: Duties and release. Customs assesses import duties and taxes under the ECOWAS Common External Tariff, on the declared classification and origin. Once payment is made and the declaration is in order, customs releases the container.

Step 8: Delivery and entry into the market. The container is collected and delivered to the importer's warehouse. The rice is now on the Ghanaian market: entered for home consumption, duties paid, an import complete.

What does this example demonstrate? Both sides ran formalities. Thailand's ended when the goods left Thai waters; Ghana's, the declaration, the duties, the release, belonged to the importer's side and decided whether the goods ever reached the market.

Key lesson: importing is defined by entry for home consumption, and the importer carries the destination country's formalities and charges.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Exporting in practice: processed cocoa from Tema to Rotterdam
BODY:
A cocoa processing company in Tema produces cocoa butter for industrial buyers. A chocolate manufacturer in the Netherlands orders 20 tonnes of it, packed in 800 twenty-five-kilogram bags on pallets. All quantities are illustrative.

Step 1: The order. The parties agree the grade, the quantity, the price, and the delivery terms, including who arranges and pays for carriage and who insures; those allocations come from the trade terms in the contract.

Step 2: Preparation and certification. The company produces the cocoa butter to the agreed specification, packs it, and arranges a quality and inspection certificate attesting the goods' condition. It also obtains the certificate of origin the buyer will need.

Step 3: Export declaration. The company, or its freight forwarder, lodges the export declaration with Ghana's customs, describing the goods, their value, and their destination.

Step 4: Handover at Tema Port. The pallets are stuffed into a container, which is sealed and delivered to the terminal before the shipping line's cargo cut-off, its verified gross mass having been declared. Customs, the export declaration being in order, releases the goods for export, and the vessel loads them.

Step 5: Main carriage. The container sails to Rotterdam under a bill of lading covering the carriage.

Step 6: Documents to the buyer. The exporter sends the buyer the documents needed at destination: the bill of lading, the commercial invoice, the packing list, and the certificates of origin and quality. With these, the buyer's side in the Netherlands lodges the import declaration, and Dutch import duties and taxes are assessed and paid there.

Step 7: Completion. The buyer takes delivery, and payment follows the method the contract agreed, the subject of this module's final lesson.

Notice where each country's formalities fell. Ghana's were all origin formalities: the export declaration, the release for export, the certificates attesting Ghanaian origin and quality. None of the exporter's work cleared the goods into the Netherlands; that was the buyer's side of the transaction.

What does this example demonstrate? Exporting is sending goods abroad, and the exporter's formalities are completed in the origin country, before the goods leave.

Key lesson: the seller clears the goods out; the buyer, unless the terms say otherwise, clears them in.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Transit in practice: machinery from Tema to Ouagadougou
BODY:
A trading house in Ouagadougou, Burkina Faso, buys a container of agricultural machinery from an overseas supplier. Burkina Faso is landlocked, so the goods arrive by sea at Tema Port in Ghana and continue by road to the border and on to Ouagadougou. They pass through Ghana, but they are not sold or used there. All details are illustrative.

Step 1: Arrival at Tema. The container is discharged at Tema Port. Declared for home consumption in Ghana, it would be an import, and Ghana's import duties would be assessed on it. It is not. Because its destination is Burkina Faso, the movement is declared under transit, under the ECOWAS Inter-State Road Transit Convention, the ISRT, which Ghana applies for transit cargo to and from its landlocked neighbours.

Step 2: Transit formalities at the port. The single transit document, the triptyque, is lodged, describing the goods, their origin, their destination and route. Customs seals the container, and a guarantee covers the duties at risk until the movement reaches its destination.

Step 3: The road leg. A truck carries the sealed container north along the prescribed corridor, the driver presenting the transit document where required; the seal must remain intact for the whole journey.

Step 4: The border. At the border posts, officers check the seal and the transit document and record the passage. No import declaration is lodged in Ghana and no Ghanaian import duties are paid, because the goods were never entered for consumption in Ghana.

Step 5: Arrival and discharge. At the customs office serving Ouagadougou the transit is discharged: the seal is checked and removed, the goods are verified against the transit document, and Burkina Faso's import formalities begin, with duties assessed and paid at the real destination. The guarantee is released.

Step 6: The accounts. Ghana's port, hauliers, and corridor earned their fees for handling and moving the container. Its treasury collected no import duty, correctly, because the machinery never entered Ghana's market; the duty belonged to Burkina Faso, the country of destination.

What does this example demonstrate? Transit is through-movement under control. The goods crossed Ghana's territory without ever entering Ghana's economy.

Key lesson: the goods never import into the transit country. The transit document, the seal, and the guarantee keep the movement under customs control until the duties are collected where the goods are actually consumed.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Transit in another region: a TIR movement from Rotterdam toward Central Asia
BODY:
The same logic works outside West Africa, under different paperwork. Consider a machinery manufacturer in Germany that sells a large industrial generator to a buyer in a landlocked country of Central Asia. The generator travels by truck from Rotterdam, crossing several countries. None of the countries crossed will import it; the buyer's country will. All details are illustrative.

Step 1: Loading and sealing. The generator is loaded onto a truck and the load is sealed, and the driver is issued a TIR carnet, the single transit document of the TIR Convention, with a guarantee behind it from the international guarantee chain.

Step 2: Crossing borders under suspension. At each border, customs offices check the seal and the carnet and record the passage. Import duties are suspended across all the borders crossed: no country along the way assesses duty on a generator merely passing through.

Step 3: Continuing under control. The movement follows the prescribed route and schedule, presenting at the offices named in the document. The single document and the intact seal are what allow the goods to cross so many territories under one procedure.

Step 4: Destination and discharge. At the customs office of the buyer's country, the transit is discharged, the seal is removed, and the import formalities begin, with duties assessed at the country of destination.

The names differ from the West African scheme, the TIR carnet instead of the ISRT triptyque, but the ingredients are identical: one transit document, one seal, one guarantee, duties only at the final destination. A learner who understands one understands the other.

What does this example demonstrate? Transit trade is a worldwide concept, adapted by each region to its own convention, and everywhere it answers the same question: how goods can cross countries that are not their destination without importing anywhere except at the end.

Key lesson: regions differ in paperwork, not in principle. The principle is duties at the destination, control along the way.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Transit is not import
BODY:
The most important distinction in this lesson is also the one beginners lose first: goods that arrive at a country's port are not automatically that country's imports.

The test is the customs procedure the goods are declared under, not the geography. Goods landed at Tema and declared for home consumption are an import: duties are assessed, and after release they join the local market. Goods landed at Tema and declared under transit to Burkina Faso are not a Ghanaian import at any point: they are under customs control, sealed and documented, crossing toward their real destination.

The consequences of the distinction are practical.

1. Charges differ. Imports carry the destination country's duties and taxes. Transit movements carry no import duties in the passing country; the duties are secured by a guarantee and collected at the destination.

2. Control differs. Released imports are in the market and at their owner's disposal. Transit goods remain under customs control throughout: seals, prescribed routes, time limits, and presentation at named offices.

3. Documents differ. An import runs on an import declaration. A transit movement runs on a single transit document, such as the ISRT triptyque, presented along the route.

Mislabelling one as the other creates real failures. Declaring transit goods for import would wrongly charge duties and pull goods into the wrong market; declaring imports as transit would move dutiable goods past the treasury. The declaration decides the treatment, so the trade type of every movement must be fixed, and declared, before the goods arrive.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Import, re-export, and transit are three different things
BODY:
Three patterns are confused constantly, and the difference is worth pinning down.

An import is goods brought into a country and entered for home consumption: they join the local market, duties are assessed, and they are used, sold, or processed there.

A re-export is goods that were imported first and are then sent onward to another country. They entered the market; their onward journey is an export from that country. Imagine a company imports 200 refrigerators into Ghana, sells 160 locally, and later sells the remaining 40 to a buyer in a neighbouring country. The 160 are simply imports consumed locally. The 40, having once been imported and entered for consumption, leave as a re-export, running on Ghana's export formalities on the way out. They were never transit goods, because they did enter Ghana's market first.

Transit is the third pattern: goods that pass through without ever being entered for consumption, under a transit arrangement, sealed, guaranteed, and closed at the real destination.

The practical differences follow the definitions.

1. A re-export has been assessed for the duties of an import, and its onward leg runs on export formalities; a transit movement never attracts the transit country's duties at all.

2. The treatment of goods that leave after import, including any reliefs or repayments of duty, differs from country to country, so the exporter must confirm the rules for its own case rather than assume.

3. The documents tell the three apart: an import declaration for the import, an export declaration for the re-export, a single transit document for the transit.

When a colleague describes a consignment as transit, ask the defining question: were the goods ever entered for consumption in the country they crossed? If yes, the movement was import and then re-export. If no, it was transit.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A container of machinery is discharged at Tema Port. Its buyer is a company in Ouagadougou, Burkina Faso, and the movement is declared under transit to Burkina Faso rather than for home consumption in Ghana.

Which statement best describes how the goods are treated in Ghana?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The goods are an import into Ghana, but the import duties are refunded once the truck leaves the country.
2. The goods are an import into Ghana because they physically arrived at a Ghanaian port, and duties are payable in Ghana first.
3. The goods move under transit arrangements without being entered for consumption in Ghana, with duties secured by a guarantee and collected at the destination country.
4. The goods need no customs treatment at all until they reach Burkina Faso, because Ghana has no interest in them.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

Transit trade is the movement of goods through a country under transit arrangements without the goods being entered for domestic consumption. Here the machinery is declared under the ISRT transit arrangement: a single transit document travels with the driver, customs seals the container, and a guarantee secures the duties until the movement is discharged at the destination office in Burkina Faso, where the import formalities and duties belong.

Answer 1 is wrong because no import arises that could be refunded: the goods were never entered for consumption in Ghana. Answer 2 confuses physical arrival with importation; the test is the customs procedure declared, not the port where the goods happen to land. Answer 4 is wrong because the transit country is not indifferent: Ghana's customs keeps control of the movement through the seal, the transit document, the prescribed routes, and the offices of the corridor.

The takeaway: transit goods cross a country's territory under its control, but they import only where they are actually destined, and the duties belong there.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
An agricultural company in Ghana buys a tractor from a manufacturer in Poland, and in the same season sells a consignment of shea butter to a cosmetics buyer in France.

Which statement correctly assigns the two transactions and their formalities?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Both are exports, because in each case goods leave a port or airport on their way to the other party.
2. The tractor purchase is an import, with Ghana's import declaration and duties on the company's side, and the shea butter sale is an export, with Ghana's export formalities completed at origin.
3. The tractor purchase is an export for Poland and the shea butter sale is an import for France, so all the formalities fall on the foreign parties.
4. The shea butter is an import into Ghana until it is loaded on the vessel, after which it becomes an export.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The direction of the goods relative to the company's own customs territory defines the trade type. Buying the tractor brings goods into Ghana for use in the business, so it is an import: the company, as importer, is responsible for the import declaration, and Ghana's duties and taxes are assessed at destination. Selling the shea butter sends goods from Ghana to a buyer abroad, so it is an export: the company, as exporter, completes the origin country's formalities, the export declaration and the certificates such as proof of origin, before the goods leave.

Answer 1 ignores direction; the tractor leaving Poland for Ghana is Poland's export and Ghana's import, not Ghana's export. Answer 3 reverses responsibility: each country's formalities are normally handled by its own side, with the seller clearing export and the buyer clearing import. Answer 4 confuses preparation with trade type; goods become an export only in the sending country and an import only in the receiving country.

The takeaway: import means goods coming into your customs territory for use or resale; export means goods going out to a buyer abroad, and each side runs its own formalities.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A company in Ghana imports 200 refrigerators and clears them for home consumption. It sells 160 of them in Ghana, and later sells the remaining 40 to a buyer in a neighbouring country, which are trucked across the border under an export declaration.

A colleague describes the whole consignment as transit goods. Which statement is correct?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The colleague is wrong: the 160 sold in Ghana were imports consumed locally, and the 40 sent onward were re-exports after import, not transit goods, because they were entered for consumption in Ghana first.
2. The colleague is right: any goods that arrive at a seaport and later leave the country by road are transit goods.
3. The colleague is right, because paying import duties converts the consignment into transit goods for the portion that later leaves the country.
4. The colleague is wrong, because imported goods can never legally leave the country once they have been entered for home consumption.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

The defining test of transit is whether the goods were ever entered for domestic consumption in the country they crossed. These refrigerators were entered for home consumption in Ghana when they were cleared on import. The 160 sold locally are straightforward imports. The 40 sent onward left Ghana under an export declaration after having been imported, which makes them a re-export: an import followed by an export, not a transit movement. A genuine transit consignment would have crossed Ghana sealed and guaranteed under a single transit document, without any entry for consumption and without Ghanaian import duties.

Answer 2 confuses physical arrival and departure with customs procedure; many imported consignments leave a country later without ever being transit goods. Answer 3 inverts the logic: paying import duties is evidence of entry for consumption, which excludes transit treatment. Answer 4 overstates the rule; re-export after import does happen, under each country's own rules and formalities.

The takeaway: import, re-export, and transit are three different procedures, and the entry for consumption is what separates the first two from the third.
