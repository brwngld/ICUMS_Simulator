=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The carrier's side of the paperwork
BODY:
The previous lesson, Commercial Invoice and Packing List, examined the seller's commercial documents: what they say and why their contents must match the goods. This lesson turns to the other half of the shipment's paperwork, the documents the carrier issues. Lesson 3.4, Introduction to Logistics Documents, met these documents by name; now they are treated in depth.

A bill of lading, usually shortened to B/L, is a maritime transport document issued by or on behalf of a carrier that acknowledges receipt of the goods and provides evidence of the contract of carriage; certain forms of it also function as documents of title. An air waybill, AWB, is a non-negotiable air transport document that provides evidence of the contract of carriage and acknowledges receipt of cargo for air transportation.

Both documents belong to one family: each is the carrier's statement that it has the goods and will carry them on stated terms. The decisive difference sits in that clause about documents of title. An ocean bill of lading, in its negotiable form, is not merely a record; it is an instrument of control over the cargo. An air waybill never is. Sea transport, where voyages take weeks and documents travel faster than vessels, grew a paper instrument powerful enough to be bought, sold, and pledged; air transport, where cargo arrives within hours of its paperwork, never needed one. This single contrast organises the whole lesson: what the bill of lading's three functions mean in practice, how clean and claused bills differ, when a negotiable document earns its administrative weight and when a sea waybill does the job better, how the air waybill differs, how master and house documents work in both modes, and why accuracy in all of them decides whether cargo is released smoothly.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The information a transport document carries
BODY:
Whatever the mode, transport documents answer the same set of questions: who is sending, who is receiving, what the goods are, and where they travel. The essential information falls into four groups.

1. The parties. The shipper, the exporter sending the goods; the consignee, the party to whom the goods are consigned; and the notify party, the person or organisation identified in a transport document to receive notification concerning the shipment's arrival or related events. The notify party is often the consignee itself, sometimes its agent or customs representative; the field exists so that someone reliably learns when the cargo arrives, even when the consignee's own details differ.

2. The journey. The carrier and, at sea, the vessel and voyage number, or the flight details for air; the port of loading, the port where cargo is loaded onto a vessel; and the port of discharge, the port where cargo is unloaded from it. An air waybill names the airports of departure and destination instead. Places of receipt and delivery appear where the carrier's responsibility extends beyond the port or airport itself.

3. The goods. The marks and numbers on the packages, the number and kind of packages, the description of the goods, and the gross weight and measurement. These figures tie the document to the physical cargo and must agree with the commercial invoice and packing list of the previous lesson.

4. The administrative details. Whether freight has been paid at origin or remains payable at destination, the place and date of issue, the signature, and for bills of lading the number of originals issued.

Lesson 5.3, Shipping Instructions and Cargo Consolidation, showed where these details come from: the carrier prepares the document from the shipper's instructions. That mechanism is why document errors are usually instruction errors, a point this lesson returns to at the end.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: First function of the bill of lading: a receipt for the goods
BODY:
The bill of lading's first function is the plainest: it is a receipt for the goods. It records what the carrier, or the terminal acting for it, received: the number of packages, their marks, the description, and their apparent condition, meaning the condition visible on ordinary outward inspection. A document stating that goods were received in apparent good order and condition is accepted at face value as evidence of receipt; a remark the carrier records on it carries corresponding weight.

Timing and wording matter. A bill issued with a shipped on board notation, confirming that the goods have been loaded on board the specified vessel, says more than one that merely acknowledges receipt alongside or before loading: the notation is the carrier's confirmation that the cargo is on its way. Payment terms and letters of credit frequently demand a shipped-on-board document for exactly that reason.

The practical lesson follows from what a receipt is. The document records what the carrier claims to have received, as reported to it, so the shipper's task is to make sure that record matches the shipped reality. If twenty cartons are handed over, the document must say twenty; if a carton arrives at the terminal already crushed, the honest course is to let the damage be recorded, not to argue it away. Once issued, the document freezes its account of the cargo. Everything a bank at the other end of the transaction will later believe about the shipment rests on that frozen account, which is why careful exporters check the draft document line by line before issue, and why the difference between a clean and a claused bill, the subject of a later concept, carries so much commercial weight.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Second function of the bill of lading: evidence of the contract of carriage
BODY:
The second function: the bill of lading is evidence of the contract of carriage between the shipper and the carrier. The contract itself is made earlier, when the carrier accepts the booking on its terms; the bill, issued afterwards, is the document that records and proves it. It names the contracting carrier, identifies the shipper, and states the route from the port of loading to the port of discharge, and it carries the carrier's standard terms, usually printed on its reverse, covering the carrier's responsibilities, its liabilities and their limits, and the freight arrangements.

In practice this function answers a question that arises at every mishap: on what terms is the carrier holding these goods, and what is it answerable for? When containers are lost overboard, when cargo is discharged at the wrong port, when goods are delivered to someone without authority, the answer is sought in the contract of carriage that the bill evidences, including the terms printed on it.

Two boundaries of this function are worth marking now, because beginners routinely blur them. First, the bill is evidence of the contract of carriage, not of the sale: it says nothing about price, payment terms, or ownership; the commercial documents of the previous lesson carry that story. Second, evidence is not the same as the contract itself; that distinction, which has real consequences when a document disagrees with a booking, is drawn carefully in an important note near the end of the lesson. What matters here is the working picture: one document, issued by the carrier, doing duty both as the receipt just described and as the standing proof of the carriage arrangement.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Third function of the bill of lading: document of title
BODY:
The third function is the bill of lading's distinctive power: in its negotiable form it is a document of title. This means that whoever rightfully holds an original, duly endorsed, controls the right to delivery of the cargo. The carrier releases the goods against an original, not against arguments, copies, or invoices. Possession of the paper is possession of the cargo, in law if not in fact.

The consequences reach far beyond the port. Because the document controls the goods, control itself can travel. An exporter in Tema can send the original to its bank; the bank can send it to a partner bank in Rotterdam; the buyer can collect the container only when the paper reaches it. While the document sits in the banking chain, the cargo sits safely at the destination, uncollectable by anyone else. The goods can even be sold in transit: a trader holding the original can endorse and hand it to a new buyer, who steps into control of cargo it has never seen.

This is why the payment methods of Module 4 are built around originals. Lesson 4.4, International Trade Payment Methods, introduced documentary collections, under which the buyer must pay the bank before the bank releases the documents, and letters of credit, under which a bank pays against documents presented exactly as required. Neither mechanism could control a cargo that anyone at the port could claim. The bill of lading's title function is the lock that makes pay-then-collect possible, and it is why the number of originals, their whereabouts, and their safe handling are commercial matters of the first importance, not clerical trivia. Among the transport documents of ordinary sea, air, and road carriage, only the ocean bill of lading carries this power.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Clean and claused bills of lading
BODY:
Because the bill of lading is a receipt of apparent condition, bills divide into two kinds by what their receipts say. A clean bill of lading bears no remarks about the condition of the goods or their packaging: it says the cargo was received in apparent good order and nothing more. A claused bill, known in the trade as a dirty bill, carries a remark recording a defect seen at receipt: two cartons damaged, one pallet torn and re-strapped, drums showing leaks. The clause is the carrier protecting itself, in public, on the face of the document.

The commercial consequences are immediate. Banks handling letters of credit require a clean bill of lading; a claused bill is a discrepancy, and payment under the credit is delayed until the discrepancy is resolved. Buyers dislike claused bills for the same underlying reason: the remark is the carrier's official record made at loading, before the goods ever sailed, and it is far harder to argue with than a buyer's later complaint. A document that begins the journey recording damage invites everyone downstream to expect it.

The professional responses happen before the document issues, not after. Packaging that survives the journey protects the clean bill; agreeing the tally with the carrier's checkers at receipt prevents disputes about the count; genuine damage found at loading belongs on the record, alongside an insurance claim, rather than being pressed off the document, which no exporter should attempt and no honest carrier would accept. Worked Example 3 follows a claused bill into a bank and shows precisely what the clause costs and who decides the outcome.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Negotiable and non-negotiable documents: order bills, straight bills, and the sea waybill
BODY:
The title function exists only where the document allows it, and the main forms are these.

1. The negotiable, or order, bill of lading. A negotiable bill of lading is one issued in a form that allows the rights represented by the document to be transferred through the appropriate endorsement and delivery procedures. It is typically consigned to order rather than to a named party, and control passes by endorsement, a signature on the document's reverse, together with handing over the original. This is the document of title described above, and it is the form that documentary collections and letters of credit can grip.

2. The straight bill of lading. A straight bill is non-negotiable and names a specific consignee: delivery is due to that named party. It still serves as receipt and evidence of the contract of carriage, but it does not transfer control by endorsement, and it cannot be used to sell cargo in transit.

3. The sea waybill. The sea waybill is the non-negotiable maritime transport document that provides evidence of the contract of carriage and receipt of the goods without functioning as a document of title. The carrier releases the cargo to the named consignee on arrival without anyone presenting paper at all. For trusted, open-account trade, where the buyer is known and no document control is needed, the sea waybill removes the handling of originals entirely.

Between the second and third forms sits a convenience worth knowing: telex release, sometimes called express release, under which the shipper surrenders the full set of originals at origin and the carrier authorises electronic release at destination. It keeps bill-of-lading control up to the moment of surrender and waybill-like speed after it. Multimodal journeys can carry the title function across modes too: the negotiable FIATA Multimodal Transport Bill of Lading, issued by freight forwarders acting as multimodal transport operators, is recognised as a document of title acceptable under letters of credit.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The air waybill and its fixed character
BODY:
Now the air side of the contrast. The air waybill is the IATA-standard air transport document, and it is always non-negotiable. It performs several duties at once: it is the receipt for the cargo the airline accepted; it is evidence of the contract of carriage; it is the freight bill showing the charges; and it serves customs as the document describing the consignment that flew. What it never is, under any circumstances, is a document of title. The cargo is released to the named consignee on arrival regardless of who holds any paper. An original waybill in a seller's desk drawer in Accra gives that seller no control over cartons sitting in a warehouse in Dubai.

The document's identity is legible in its number. An air waybill number has eleven digits: a three-digit airline code identifying the carrier whose document it is, a seven-digit serial number, and a final check digit that guards against typing errors. The number appears on every related record, so the whole chain, from acceptance to delivery, can refer to one consignment unambiguously.

The form is changing as well as the content. Under IATA Resolution 672, the electronic air waybill has been the default contract of carriage on enabled trade lanes, with paper the exception. The e-AWB changes the medium, not the legal character: the document remains what it always was, a receipt and contract evidence with no title function.

The commercial consequence deserves its plainest form here. Because air cargo moves as fast as or faster than its paperwork and its payment, control by document is impossible; the seller's protection must be chosen in the sales contract itself. Lesson 4.4's spectrum of payment methods, from advance payment through documentary methods to open account, is not optional theory for air trade; it is the only control mechanism there is. Worked Example 2 shows the release procedure that follows.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Master and house documents in sea and air
BODY:
One pattern repeats across every mode: documents follow contracts, and there are two levels of contract whenever a forwarder stands between shipper and carrier. The document issued by the actual carrier to the party contracting with it is the master document; the document the intermediary issues to its own customer is the house document.

At sea, the master bill of lading, MBL, is generally issued by the ocean carrier to the party contracting with it for transportation, which in a consolidated shipment is the freight forwarder; the house bill of lading, HBL, is issued by a freight forwarder or non-vessel-operating common carrier to its customer. Lesson 5.3 showed the pair operating in a consolidated container: one master document covering the box, one house document per shipper's consignment. The point to add here concerns control. A house bill issued in negotiable form gives the exporter's bank or buyer exactly the title grip a master document would, and an exporter tendering documents under a letter of credit tenders its house bill, not the line's master, because the house document is the one that names the exporter's parties and travels with its own contract.

In the air, the same two levels recur under their own names. The master air waybill, MAWB, is issued by the airline or its authorised agent covering cargo accepted under the airline's contract of carriage; the house air waybill, HAWB, is issued by the freight forwarder to a shipper for an individual consignment. A forwarder consolidating cargo at an air cargo terminal hands each shipper a HAWB and holds the airline's MAWB for the consolidated consignment, exactly as its sea counterpart holds the MBL. In both modes the practical questions for a shipper are the same: which document covers my goods, who issued it, and will my bank or buyer accept it? The answers decide what is presented, to whom, and when.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The road consignment note
BODY:
The third mode in the region's daily work is the road, and its document completes the pattern by absence. The road consignment note is a receipt for the goods and evidence of the contract of road carriage; it is non-negotiable and never a document of title, and it accompanies the goods throughout the journey, travelling with the driver from pickup to delivery, to be checked and signed at each handover.

In Europe and neighbouring regions, the form is standardised by the CMR Convention, whose consignment note follows the same principles, and for which an electronic version, the e-CMR, is permitted. West Africa is different, and the difference matters for anyone documenting a regional movement: Ghana is not a contracting party to the CMR Convention, and West African road movements use a generic national or regional consignment note instead of a CMR note. The generic note does the same two jobs, receipt and evidence of contract, and lacks the same title function.

The road document thus confirms the rule the air waybill already stated: only an ocean bill of lading, among ordinary transport documents, is a document of title. Air waybills, road consignment notes, and sea waybills are receipts and evidence of the contract, never title documents. A driver's paperwork, however carefully completed, will not lock a truckload of goods the way an original bill locks a container, which is why overland regional trade relies on the payment and transit arrangements around the movement rather than on the transport document itself.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Accuracy, cargo release, and common documentation errors
BODY:
Cargo release is where every transport document finally does its work. At the port or airport, the carrier releases cargo according to its document: against an original bill of lading where the document is negotiable, to the named consignee where it is not. The customs declaration lodged for the shipment must agree with the transport document, and the transport document must agree with the commercial invoice and packing list. Accurate transport documentation is therefore not a matter of tidiness; it is the precondition for release, delivery, and payment proceeding on schedule.

The common errors and their effects follow directly from the functions taught in this lesson.

1. The wrong or misspelled consignee. Release follows the document, so a document naming the wrong party holds the cargo while an amendment is arranged through the carrier, with fees and delay.

2. Cargo details disagreeing with the commercial documents. A package count or weight that differs from the invoice and packing list surfaces as a discrepancy at the bank or at customs, freezing payment or clearance until it is corrected.

3. A missing shipped-on-board notation. Where the payment terms demand proof of loading, its absence is a discrepancy in itself.

4. An unexpected clause. Damage recorded at receipt converts a clean bill into a claused one, with the consequences Worked Example 3 shows.

5. Wrong places and parties. An incorrect port of loading or discharge, or notify-party details that reach nobody, delay arrival notices and collection.

Every one of these is cheapest to catch before the document issues, in the check between instructions, invoice, and packing list that earlier lessons built into routine. After issue, correction runs through the carrier; after sailing, it runs through everyone. With the commercial documents of the previous lesson and the transport documents of this lesson in place, the next lesson, Certificates of Origin, Permits, and Supporting Documents, completes the shipment's core paperwork.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Documents against payment by sea: the original as the control instrument
BODY:
A cocoa processing company in Tema has sold 400 bags of processed cocoa, about 24 tonnes in one container, to a chocolate manufacturer in Rotterdam. The parties have traded before but not enough for open credit: the sale is on documents-against-payment terms, D/P, under which the buyer must pay before the bank releases the documents. All quantities and details in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: Shipment and document issue. The container is delivered to the terminal at Tema and loaded aboard the nominated vessel. The shipping line issues a bill of lading showing the exporter as shipper, consigned to order, with a shipped-on-board notation, the port of loading as Tema and the port of discharge as Rotterdam, the buyer's clearing agent named as notify party, and the 400 bags, marks, and gross weight recorded. The bill is issued in a set of originals.

Step 2: The original becomes the control instrument. The exporter endorses an original bill and lodges it, with the commercial invoice and packing list, with its bank, which forwards the documents to its partner bank in Rotterdam. From this moment the cargo sits in the port of Rotterdam, but nobody there can take it. The carrier releases the container only against an original bill of lading, and the only original in the Netherlands is locked inside the banking chain. The unpaid exporter controls the cargo precisely because it controls the paper.

Step 3: Payment. The Rotterdam bank presents the documents to the buyer and demands payment, as documents against payment require. The buyer pays, because it wants the cocoa, and the bank releases the original bill to it.

Step 4: Release. The buyer surrenders the original to the carrier's agent in Rotterdam, and the agent releases the container. The document's journey, from Tema to the bank and finally to the buyer, ends where the cargo's journey ends.

What does this example demonstrate? The bill of lading's title function in operation: the original travelled the banking route instead of the goods being released on trust, and each party's risk was bounded by who held the paper at each moment.

Key lesson: under documents-against-payment, the original bill of lading is the transaction. Guarding it, endorsing it correctly, and sending it by a reliable route are not administrative details; they are the exporter's entire security.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Air waybill release at the airport: no paper, only the named consignee
BODY:
A pineapple exporter near Accra ships fresh pineapples by air to a long-standing customer in Dubai: 40 cartons, about 1,100 kilograms, packed on the morning of departure, because freshness is the product. The parties have traded for years on open-account terms, payment within an agreed period after arrival; no document could ever give the exporter control of perishable air cargo, and the relationship and the payment terms are the security. All details are illustrative.

Step 1: Booking and documents. The exporter's forwarder books the consignment on a flight from Kotoka International Airport to Dubai. Because the cartons travel in a consolidated air consignment with other shippers' goods, two documents issue, exactly as in sea consolidation: the airline issues its master air waybill covering the whole consolidated consignment, and the forwarder issues the exporter a house air waybill for its 40 cartons. The house document shows the exporter as shipper, the Dubai customer as consignee, the customer's produce agent as notify party, the eleven-digit air waybill number, and the carton count, description, and gross weight, agreeing with the invoice and packing list.

Step 2: Acceptance and flight. The forwarder delivers the cartons to the airline's cargo terminal at Kotoka; the airline checks the consignment against the document and accepts it, and the air waybill becomes the receipt for the cargo in apparent good condition. The consignment flies that night and completes arrival formalities in Dubai the next morning; on many lanes this whole exchange runs electronically, the air waybill existing as data under the industry's e-AWB arrangements.

Step 3: Release. The airline's ground handling agent notifies the notify party that the consignment has arrived. The customer, or its agent, presents identification matching the named consignee and collects the 40 cartons. Nobody presents an original document at the counter, because the air waybill is not a document of title; the identity of the named consignee is the release condition.

Step 4: Payment. The customer pays within the agreed period, under the terms agreed before departure. Had the customer not been trusted, the answer would not have been a different document, since no air document controls cargo; it would have been advance payment or another arrangement from Lesson 4.4's spectrum.

What does this example demonstrate? The contrast with the sea case of the previous example: at Rotterdam the cargo waited for a paper original; in Dubai the cargo waited only for its named consignee.

Key lesson: in air trade the document identifies, receipts, and describes; the payment terms, chosen before the goods fly, do the controlling.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A claused bill discovered by the bank
BODY:
A frozen-seafood processor in Mumbai sells 1,200 cartons of fish to an importer in Accra under a letter of credit, the payment method in which a bank undertakes to pay against documents presented in strict compliance with its terms; Lesson 4.4 covered how such credits work, and how banks deal in documents rather than goods. All details are illustrative.

Step 1: Loading and the clause. The reefer container is stuffed and delivered to the terminal in Mumbai. During the carrier's tally at receipt, its checkers find ten cartons with torn, water-stained packaging. The bill of lading issues carrying the carrier's remark: ten cartons torn and stained at receipt. The document is now a claused bill, and nothing the exporter does later can un-clause it.

Step 2: Presentation. After the vessel sails, the exporter presents its documents: the bill of lading, the commercial invoice, the packing list, and the other documents the credit requires. The bank examines the documents on their face, as banks under credits do, and finds the clause.

Step 3: The discrepancy bites. The credit calls for a clean transport document, and the presented bill is not clean. The payment the exporter counted on does not flow; the examination has found a document that does not comply, and the buyer's decision is now required. The buyer in Accra is asked whether it will accept the documents despite the discrepancy.

Step 4: The buyer's decision. The buyer weighs its interests. The fish inside are sound; only the cartons suffered, the Accra market is waiting, and refusing the shipment means cargo stranded far from home. It accepts the documents, the discrepancy is waived on the buyer's instruction, and payment proceeds, days late and after fees and correspondence that nobody needed. Had the buyer refused, the exporter would have faced a shipment arriving to no buyer, with the claused bill as the standing record of why.

What does this example demonstrate? The clean-versus-claused distinction with money attached: the clause was recorded at loading, the bank could only read it, and the decision moved to the buyer. A remark on a transport document is never just a remark.

Key lesson: protect the clean bill at the origin end, with packaging and honest tallies, because at presentation nothing can remove a clause, and the party that decides what a clause is worth is the buyer, not the seller.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Treat original bills of lading like cheques
BODY:
Original bills of lading deserve handling closer to cheques than to filing. Each original is a signed instrument that controls cargo, and a set issued in several originals multiplies the danger, because every piece in the set is capable of releasing the goods until delivery is made against one of them.

The consequences of losing an original follow from the title function. An exporter whose original is lost in transit has not lost a copy; it has lost the key to the container. Release is delayed until the matter is resolved with the carrier, a slow, administrative affair conducted under the carrier's procedures and always at a cost in time, fees, and anxiety, while the cargo accrues whatever waiting charges the port and the line impose. Nobody plans for this, which is exactly why the routine matters: originals go by reliable, trackable delivery; their despatch is recorded; the number of the set is known; and no originals are casually created or left in desk drawers.

The other half of the discipline is choosing when originals should exist at all. Where the trade is trusted and on open account, this lesson's alternatives, the sea waybill or telex release, remove the travelling originals entirely, and with them the risk of loss. Where the payment method needs the title function, the originals must travel, and the exporter should know where they are at every moment, from issue to surrender. A trader who can answer the question, where is the original bill of lading right now, for every live shipment, has understood this lesson.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: The air waybill title trap
BODY:
The most expensive mistake in air documentation is assuming the air waybill behaves like a bill of lading. It does not. The AWB is never a document of title; naming a consignee on it is not a promise to deliver against paper but simply the identification of the party to whom the cargo will be released.

Follow the trap in its commonest form. An exporter airfreights goods to an overseas buyer who has not yet paid, names the buyer as consignee, and keeps the paper waybill at its office in the belief that holding it prevents collection. The goods arrive; the buyer presents itself at the cargo terminal as the named consignee and collects. The paper controls nothing. The exporter's position is that of an unpaid seller of delivered goods, armed with correspondence rather than custody.

Two protections exist, and both operate before departure. The first is the payment arrangement: advance payment from an untrusted buyer, or another method from Lesson 4.4's spectrum, chosen in the knowledge that no document will secure an air shipment. The second is care over the consignee box itself: where the seller is uneasy, goods may be consigned to the buyer's bank or another party at destination able to act, though every party named must be told and agree, and corrections after departure are difficult and slow. The habit to carry forward is to ask, before every air dispatch: who can collect this cargo on arrival, and is the payment position safe if the answer is not me?

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Evidence of the contract, not the contract itself
BODY:
A precision about the bill of lading's second function belongs near the end, because it is a distinction beginners blur in both directions. The bill of lading is evidence of the contract of carriage; it is not the contract itself. The contract of carriage comes into being when the carrier accepts the booking on its terms; the bill issues afterwards and proves that a contract exists and what it was. The terms printed on the bill's reverse form part of that contractual setting, but the document is the contract's witness, not its birth certificate.

The distinction matters whenever paper and reality disagree. If the bill shows a different port of discharge from the booking confirmation, the discrepancy is not two valid truths to be ranked; it is an error to be corrected, usually by amendment with the carrier, and the earlier it is caught the cheaper the correction. The document does not silently rewrite the arrangement, and the arrangement does not excuse the document.

The boundary on the other side is equally practical: the bill of lading is not the sales contract either. Nothing on it records price, payment terms, or trade terms; those live in the commercial documents of the previous lesson, which is why the full documentary set must agree with itself, invoice to packing list to transport document, before any bank or border authority will process it smoothly. One shipment, several documents, each with a limited job: the transport document's jobs are receipt, evidence of the carriage contract, and, at sea only, title. Expecting any single document to carry the whole story is how discrepancies begin.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A trader in Accra airfreights 12 cartons of mobile phone accessories to a buyer in Nairobi who has not yet paid. The trader names the buyer as consignee on the air waybill and keeps the paper waybill at its office in Accra, expecting that holding the document will prevent the buyer from collecting the goods until payment is made.

Which statement best describes the trader's position?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The trader is protected, because the airline will release the cargo only when an original air waybill is presented at the destination counter.
2. The trader is protected, because writing "to order" in the consignee box would have made the air waybill negotiable like a bill of lading.
3. The trader is not protected, because an air waybill is never a document of title and the cargo is released to the named consignee on arrival regardless of who holds the paper, so the trader's security had to come from the payment arrangement agreed before dispatch.
4. The trader is not protected, because airlines hold incoming cargo until the buyer's bank approves the release, which the buyer can arrange without paying the seller.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The air waybill is the IATA-standard air transport document, and it is always non-negotiable: it is a receipt, evidence of the contract of carriage, a freight bill, and a customs document, but never a document of title. Cargo is released to the named consignee on arrival, and the named consignee collects by identification, not by presenting paper. The waybill sitting in the Accra office therefore prevents nothing; by the time the goods land, the seller's only protection is the payment arrangement agreed in the sales contract, which for an untrusted buyer should have meant advance payment or another secure method from Lesson 4.4's spectrum.

Answer 1 fails because it describes bill-of-lading behaviour that an air waybill never has. Answer 2 fails because no wording makes an air waybill negotiable; non-negotiability is a fixed characteristic of the document. Answer 4 invents a bank-approval procedure at the airline that does not exist.

Takeaway: in air trade the document identifies and receipts the cargo; the payment arrangement does the controlling.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
A bank examines documents presented under a letter of credit for a consignment of frozen fish from Mumbai to Accra and finds a remark on the bill of lading reading "ten cartons torn and stained at receipt". The goods themselves are still at sea.

Which statement best describes what follows?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The bank arranges for the container to be inspected on arrival so it can decide whether the ten cartons are actually damaged before releasing payment.
2. The remark makes the bill of lading claused rather than clean; the credit requires a clean transport document, so payment is delayed until the buyer decides whether to accept the documents despite the discrepancy.
3. The remark is immaterial, because the carton count, marks, and weights on the bill of lading agree with the invoice and the packing list.
4. The remark can be removed once the vessel arrives and the carrier confirms that the goods inside the cartons are sound.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

A carrier's remark recording a defect seen at receipt converts a clean bill into a claused, or dirty, bill, and banks handling letters of credit require a clean bill of lading. The clause is a discrepancy, and under a documentary credit the payment the exporter expects is suspended until the discrepancy is resolved, which in practice means the buyer is asked whether it will accept the documents despite the remark, as Worked Example 3 showed.

Answer 1 fails because banks deal in documents, not goods: they examine the documents on their face and have no role inspecting cargo. Answer 3 fails because agreement between the documents cannot cure a clause; the requirement is a clean bill, not a consistent dirty one. Answer 4 fails because the clause records what was seen when the goods were received, and nothing at arrival can un-record it.

Takeaway: the clean-versus-claused line is drawn at loading; the seller pays the price of a clause, and the buyer decides what it is worth.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A buyer in Hamburg has imported shea butter from the same supplier at Tema for years, always on open-account terms with payment made after arrival. For the next container, the forwarder asks whether the seller needs a negotiable bill of lading whose originals must travel, or whether release can be arranged without original documents.

Which option best fits this trade?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. A sea waybill, which is a non-negotiable document evidencing receipt and the contract of carriage, allowing the carrier to release the cargo to the named consignee without any original, which suits trusted open-account trade where document control serves no purpose.
2. A negotiable order bill of lading, because every ocean shipment must be documented in negotiable form for the transport document to be valid.
3. A claused bill of lading, because it records only the receipt of the goods and the agreed consignee without any remark on condition.
4. A master air waybill showing the vessel and voyage number, because it is the standard non-negotiable document for cargo of any mode.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

The sea waybill is the non-negotiable maritime alternative to the bill of lading: it is a receipt and evidence of the contract of carriage, but not a document of title, and the carrier releases the cargo to the named consignee without presenting paper. For parties who trust each other and trade on open account, the title function is a service nobody is buying, so the despatch, tracking, and safeguarding of originals is pure administrative burden, and the sea waybill removes it. Telex release offers a similar simplification where bill-of-lading control is wanted only up to surrender.

Answer 2 fails because negotiability is a choice, not a validity requirement; straight bills and sea waybills are perfectly valid ocean documents. Answer 3 mangles the clean-versus-claused distinction: a claused bill is one that records damage remarks, which no one would choose. Answer 4 mixes up the modes; an air waybill belongs to air carriage, however it is filled in.

Takeaway: choose the transport document by the control the trade actually needs: title instruments for secured payment methods, waybills for trusted parties.
