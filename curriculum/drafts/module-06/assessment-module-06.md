=== ASSESSMENT Q1 | tests lesson 6.1 ===
QUESTION:
An importer in Kumasi receives a sea container said to hold 50 cartons of door hinges from a supplier abroad. The commercial invoice shows 50 cartons and the total invoice value; the packing list shows 50 cartons with shipping marks running AB/ACCRA/1 to AB/ACCRA/50. On unloading, the warehouse counts only 48 cartons, and the importer must lodge a precise claim with the supplier.

Which statement best describes how the documents serve this claim?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The commercial invoice is the decisive document, because the claim is about money, and the invoice's total value tells the supplier exactly how much to refund.
2. The packing list and its shipping marks are decisive: marks numbered in sequence from 1 to 50 identify each package, so the missing cartons can be named precisely, and the claim states which marks never arrived.
3. Neither document helps, because a shortage can only be proved by the carrier's internal loading records, which the importer cannot see.
4. The two documents are interchangeable for this purpose, because both state a quantity of 50, so either can be used to claim for two missing cartons.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Shipping marks exist for exactly this moment. Sequential numbering, AB/ACCRA/1 to AB/ACCRA/50, means a missing package announces itself: the consignee can write, precisely and checkably, that the cartons bearing marks 37 and 41 were never received, and the claim stands on a record both parties can verify. The packing list is the counting sheet for both ends of the journey — the dispatch warehouse counts against it, the receiving warehouse counts in reverse — and the marks tie each physical carton to its line on the list.

The commercial invoice still matters, but for the money side of the claim: its quantity of 50 and its total invoice value establish what was sold and what is at stake. Answer 1 is wrong to reduce the claim to value alone — a refund demand must be tied to identified packages, and an invoice cannot identify them. Answer 3 abandons documents the lesson shows are built for this job; no carrier's internal record is needed to prove which numbered cartons are absent. Answer 4 dissolves the lesson's founding distinction: the two documents agreeing on 50 is what makes the shortage visible, but only the packing list's marks and contents do the identifying work.

The takeaway: the invoice tells what the shipment was worth; the packing list and its shipping marks tell what is physically present, package by package — and a precise claim needs the second story.

=== ASSESSMENT Q2 | tests lesson 6.1 ===
QUESTION:
An exporter in Accra receives an order from a buyer abroad and sends a pro forma invoice, which the buyer uses to confirm the deal and arrange payment. The goods are then produced, packed and shipped. Under time pressure, a clerk sends the buyer the same pro forma invoice as the document accompanying the shipment, and no commercial invoice is issued.

Which statement best describes the problem?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. No problem exists, because a pro forma invoice and a commercial invoice carry the same fields — parties, goods and prices — so a single document can serve both the negotiation and the shipment.
2. The defect is easily cured at destination: once the goods arrive, the buyer can convert the pro forma into a commercial invoice and present it to customs itself.
3. The pro forma is a quotation in invoice form, issued before the sale is final; it is not a demand for payment and not a customs document, so the shipment now lacks the commercial invoice that records the actual sale, demands payment and grounds the customs valuation at import.
4. A commercial invoice must not be issued now, because once a pro forma invoice has been sent for a transaction, the sale may only ever be documented by that document.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The difference between the two documents lies in timing and function, not appearance. A pro forma invoice is issued before the sale is final: a quotation in invoice form, setting out proposed goods, prices and terms so the buyer can confirm the deal, arrange payment, or apply for permits or foreign exchange. It is not a demand for payment and not a customs document. The commercial invoice is issued for the actual shipment: it demands payment for goods actually sold, records the transaction in both parties' books, and grounds the customs valuation at import. By letting the pro forma drift into use as the shipping invoice, the exporter has left customs valuing the goods on a document that was never issued for the shipment at all, and the buyer holds nothing that records the sale.

Answer 1 mistakes shared fields for shared function; the same parties and prices appear on both, yet only one declares a completed sale. Answer 2 is impossible in the direction it proposes: the buyer cannot issue the seller's document of the sale. Answer 4 invents a rule that a pro forma freezes the paperwork forever; the professional habit is the opposite.

The takeaway: a pro forma may start the file, but the commercial invoice, issued for the actual consignment, is the only invoice that travels with the goods — the pro forma proposes, the commercial invoice declares.

=== ASSESSMENT Q3 | tests lesson 6.1 ===
QUESTION:
A sales manager under commercial pressure proposes preparing two invoices for a shipment to a market with high duties: the true invoice for the buyer, showing the real price, and a second copy showing a much lower total, to be presented to customs at destination so the importer pays less duty.

Which statement best describes correct professional practice?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Deliberately declaring a false value on a customs document is misdeclaration and fraud: one sale carries one invoice with one set of true figures, and a genuine error found after issue is corrected openly, by cancelling or crediting the erroneous document and issuing a corrected one.
2. The proposal is acceptable practice, because customs assesses only the document presented to it, and the buyer's true records remain a private matter between the trading parties.
3. The proposal is a legitimate way to keep landed costs competitive, provided the reduced figure still covers the cost of producing the goods.
4. The two documents of a shipment tell different stories by design, so the invoice presented to customs may legitimately state a different value from the invoice on which the buyer pays.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

The lesson's rule is short: one sale, one invoice, one set of true figures, wherever those figures are asked for. Deliberately declaring a false value on a customs document is misdeclaration, and manipulated trade documents are a recognised fraud and compliance exposure in international trade. The consequences reach in every direction: customs that discovers two versions of one invoice can hold or seize the goods and impose penalties; the buyer's accounts, audit trail and three-way match are corrupted by records that contradict the declaration; where banks are paying against documents, the contradictory papers put the payment itself at risk; and the two versions always find each other eventually — a payment for one amount, a declaration for another, an audit comparing files.

Answer 2 assumes customs examines one page in isolation and discovery is unlikely; hope of not being caught is not a control. Answer 3 invents a cost-based defence that exists nowhere in this lesson; a false declaration does not become true because the figure would still cover costs. Answer 4 misapplies the commercial-versus-physical distinction: the invoice and the packing list tell two stories about one set of facts, and neither is permitted to state a value that is not true.

The takeaway: where a genuine error exists, the correction is made openly — cancel or credit the erroneous document and issue a corrected one — never by preparing a second truth.

=== ASSESSMENT Q4 | tests lesson 6.2 ===
QUESTION:
A cocoa trader in Tema ships 600 bags of processed cocoa to a buyer in Antwerp under documents-against-payment terms, and the original bill of lading, consigned to order, travels through the banking chain. While the vessel is still at sea, the buyer finds a second customer in Hamburg who wants the cocoa, and asks whether the cargo can be sold on while it sails.

Which statement best explains what makes an in-transit sale possible here?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The sale is possible because a bill of lading evidences the contract of sale as well as the contract of carriage, so endorsing it transfers the sales contract to the new buyer.
2. The sale is possible because the bill is consigned to order and is a document of title in negotiable form: whoever rightfully holds an original, duly endorsed, controls the right to delivery, so the trader can endorse the original and hand it to the new buyer, who steps into control of cargo it has never seen.
3. The sale is possible because the buyer in Antwerp can simply take delivery at the port and resell there; physical possession of the goods at the port of discharge is what transfers control.
4. The sale is possible only if the trader first asks the shipping line to reissue the bill of lading naming the new buyer as consignee, because a consignment can never change hands while the voyage is under way.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The negotiable, or order, bill of lading is the document of title described in this lesson: consigned to order rather than to a named party, it transfers control by endorsement — a signature on the document's reverse — together with handing over the original. Whoever rightfully holds the original, duly endorsed, controls the right to delivery, because the carrier releases the cargo against an original, not against arguments or copies. That is why the original can travel the banking chain under documents-against-payment while the cargo sits uncollectable at the destination, and it is also why cargo can be sold in transit: a trader holding the original can endorse and hand it to a new buyer, who steps into control of goods it has never seen. No reissue is needed.

Answer 1 confuses the document's stories: the bill is evidence of the contract of carriage, not of the sale, and says nothing about price, payment terms or ownership. Answer 3 inverts the mechanism; possession of the cargo at the port is not the test, presentation of the original is, which is precisely what keeps an unpaid exporter in control. Answer 4 describes amendment thinking; with an order bill the endorsement itself moves the control, and the carrier's obligation to release follows the document.

The takeaway: with a negotiable bill, the original is the cargo in law — endorse it and control moves with it.

=== ASSESSMENT Q5 | tests lesson 6.2 ===
QUESTION:
At the container yard before loading, a shipping line's tally finds two pallets of a machinery consignment with torn and re-strapped packaging. The exporter's clerk asks the line to issue the bill of lading clean anyway, arguing that the machines inside are undamaged and that a remark on the document will only cause trouble at the bank.

Which statement best evaluates the clerk's request?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The request is reasonable, because a bill of lading records only the internal condition of cargo, which the tally at the terminal cannot see.
2. The request is unnecessary, because a remark recorded at receipt can be removed later, once the carrier confirms at destination that the machines inside are undamaged.
3. The request should be refused: the clause is the carrier's receipt record of a defect seen at receipt, the honest course is to let the damage be recorded and pursue it through an insurance claim, and a claused bill presented under a letter of credit is a discrepancy that delays payment — protection happens before issue, through packaging and honest tallies.
4. The request should be refused, but only because machinery may never travel on a claused bill; the consignment must be re-booked on the next vessel.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

A clean bill bears no remarks about the condition of the goods or their packaging; a claused bill carries a remark recording a defect seen at receipt. The clause is the carrier protecting itself, in public, on the face of the document, and the receipt covers apparent condition — the condition visible on ordinary outward inspection — which is exactly what the tally examined. Torn and re-strapped pallets belong on the record, alongside an insurance claim, and pressing the clause off the document is something no exporter should attempt and no honest carrier would accept. The commercial weight is real: banks handling letters of credit require a clean bill of lading, so a claused bill is a discrepancy, and payment under the credit is delayed until the buyer decides whether to accept the documents despite the remark.

Answer 1 misreads what the receipt records: apparent condition is the outside of the packaging, precisely what the checkers saw, and the remark stands whatever is true of the machines inside. Answer 2 hopes for a cure the lesson closes off: the clause is drawn at receipt, and nothing at arrival can un-record it. Answer 4 invents a prohibition on carrying such cargo; a claused bill still documents a real shipment — it simply carries consequences the seller pays for.

The takeaway: the clean-versus-claused line is drawn at receipt; protect the clean bill at the origin end, with packaging and honest tallies.

=== ASSESSMENT Q6 | tests lesson 6.2 ===
QUESTION:
A freight forwarder at Kotoka International Airport consolidates air cargo from several shippers into one consignment to Antwerp. Among them is an exporter of shea butter soap, whose sale is financed by a letter of credit. The exporter asks which document will cover its 25 cartons, who issues it, and what it should present to the bank.

Which statement best answers the exporter?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The airline issues a house air waybill directly to each shipper, because airlines deal with the parties whose goods they carry.
2. The forwarder issues the exporter a house air waybill for its 25 cartons, naming the exporter's parties, while the airline issues its master air waybill to the forwarder covering the whole consolidated consignment; the document the exporter tenders under the letter of credit is its own house waybill.
3. Only one air waybill exists in a consolidation — the airline's master air waybill covering all the cargo — and each shipper receives a copy of it for presentation to the bank.
4. Air cargo needs no transport document for a letter of credit, because the air waybill is not a document of title and therefore plays no part in documentary presentations.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Documents follow contracts, and there are two levels of contract whenever a forwarder stands between the shipper and the carrier. The airline issues its master air waybill covering the cargo accepted under the airline's contract of carriage — here, the whole consolidated consignment — and the forwarder, its customer at that level, issues a house air waybill to each shipper for that shipper's individual consignment, showing the exporter as shipper and the exporter's own parties. The practical questions for a shipper are exactly the ones the exporter is asking: which document covers my goods, who issued it, and will my bank accept it? Under a letter of credit the exporter tenders its house waybill, not the airline's master, because the house document is the one that names the exporter's parties and travels with its own contract. The same two-level pattern governs sea consolidation, with the master and house bills of lading.

Answer 1 reverses the issuers: airlines issue master documents; the house level belongs to the forwarder. Answer 3 denies the house level entirely and hands a shipper a document that neither names its parties nor evidences its own contract. Answer 4 over-extends a true fact — the air waybill is never a document of title — into a false conclusion; a waybill that controls nothing still receipts the cargo, evidences the carriage and is routinely required by credits.

The takeaway: in any consolidation, know which document is yours, who issued it, and what your bank or buyer will accept.

=== ASSESSMENT Q7 | tests lesson 6.3 ===
QUESTION:
A manufacturer near Kumasi makes cocoa beverage mixes using cocoa grown in Ghana and a small proportion of imported flavouring, and its products qualify under a continental trade agreement's rules of origin. To serve a buyer in another participating country duty-free, a clerk proposes to save time by typing the preferential certificate of origin on the company's own letterhead, since the goods genuinely qualify anyway.

Which statement best corrects the clerk?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The proposal is sound, because qualification is the only requirement: once the goods genuinely qualify under the agreement's rules of origin, the format of the certificate and the body behind it do not matter.
2. The certificate should instead be prepared by the buyer, because the party claiming the preference at import owns the document and may create it.
3. The manufacturer should obtain a non-preferential certificate instead, because preferential certificates of origin are issued only by the importing country's customs administration.
4. Preferential certificates are issued through the authorities and procedures each agreement designates, against the exporter's qualifying records — never written up by the trader for the occasion; a self-issued certificate would be a false document even though the goods themselves qualify.
CORRECT: 4
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 4.

A preferential certificate of origin is not stationery; it is a claim with legal consequences — the assertion that the state owes less duty because these goods qualify under a specific agreement. Two disciplines attach. First, the claim must be true before the paper exists: qualification is decided by the exporter's records — what was grown where, what inputs came from abroad, what processing happened in the factory — and this manufacturer's records do support qualification. Second, the paper must come through the proper channel: preferential certificates are issued through the authorities and procedures each agreement designates, never written up by the trader for the occasion. A certificate typed on company letterhead is a false document even where the goods qualify, because the document itself asserts something no authorised body has certified.

Answer 1 treats qualification as the whole test; it is necessary, but the claim must still be made on the proper document through the designated procedures. Answer 2 assigns the document to the wrong side: the certificate is export-side, earned by the exporter's records, not created by the buyer claiming the preference. Answer 3 reaches for the wrong instrument entirely — a non-preferential certificate states origin for ordinary customs treatment and supports no duty-free claim — and misstates who issues preferential certificates.

The takeaway: the certificate follows qualification and the designated procedure; preference is earned by the goods and evidenced by the proper paper, never the reverse.

=== ASSESSMENT Q8 | tests lesson 6.3 ===
QUESTION:
A cosmetics distributor in Accra plans its first import of a new skincare line from a supplier abroad. A vessel sails every week, and the distributor's manager proposes to place the order and ship on Friday's sailing, then apply for the required regulatory authorisation while the goods are on the water, so that the permit is in hand by the time the container reaches Tema.

Which statement best evaluates the plan?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The timing principle is the reverse: the approval must be obtained before the goods are ordered or shipped, not after they arrive — a permit obtained after arrival is a delay at best and may be no use at all, which is why the document list is established while the deal is still being shaped.
2. The plan is efficient, because a permit needs only to exist before the customs declaration is lodged at Tema, and the ocean transit leaves ample time to obtain one.
3. The plan is efficient, because the authorisation is consulted only when a consignment is selected for physical examination, and most containers are never opened.
4. The plan is sound, because the distributor's existing authorisation for its current product lines extends automatically to the new line, since a permit is held by the company rather than by the product.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

The timing principle for permits is absolute in practice: the approval is obtained before the goods are ordered or shipped, not after they arrive, and a permit obtained after arrival is a delay at best and may be no use at all. The manager's plan inverts this: shipping on Friday's sailing and applying during the transit means the consignment will reach Tema supported by nothing, because the declaration file must carry the authorisation, and documents travel before goods. This is why the professional discipline is to establish the complete document list while the deal is being shaped — before prices are quoted and dates fixed — because every certificate and permit on the list takes time to obtain, and its absence costs far more than its presence. The regulator's approval is part of the order, not part of the clearance.

Answer 2 sets the deadline at declaration; the rule is earlier still, and the plan already fails it. Answer 3 ties the permit to physical examination; a document check queries the file too, and a declaration without its required permit is incomplete whatever channel the consignment takes. Answer 4 confuses permit scope: an approval is specific to the product, the holder and its period of validity, so an existing authorisation for one line does not extend to a new one.

The takeaway: the regulatory gate comes first — secure the approval, then order and ship.

=== ASSESSMENT Q9 | tests lesson 6.3 ===
QUESTION:
An agro-exporter in Ghana ships two consignments in the same week: potted ornamental plants to one market and packaged cheese to another. A clerk preparing the files describes both supporting certificates as sanitary certificates and asks the trader's own laboratory to issue them, to save waiting for the authorities.

Which statement best corrects the two errors in the clerk's plan?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Both consignments need phytosanitary certificates, because sanitary and phytosanitary are two names for one certificate, of which plants are simply the commonest case.
2. Certificates of this kind are commercial documents, so any reputable laboratory the parties choose may issue them, and the choice of issuing body is a matter of convenience.
3. The plants need a phytosanitary certificate — phyto means plant — while the cheese, a product of animal origin, needs a health certificate; both are issued by the competent authority of the exporting country, against checks on the actual consignment, before shipment, not by a laboratory the trader hires.
4. Both certificates are obtained on arrival: the destination's authorities inspect the goods and issue them at import, which is why they can safely be left until the vessels sail.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

Two errors are corrected at once. The first is vocabulary: in the language of international health rules, sanitary measures protect human and animal health, while phytosanitary measures protect plant health — phyto means plant. Potted ornamental plants are plant products and travel on a phytosanitary certificate; cheese, a product of animal origin, travels on a health certificate. The second error is the issuing body: these certificates are issued by the competent authority of the exporting country — the plant-protection authority for the phytosanitary certificate, an authorised body for the health certificate — not by a laboratory the trader hires. Both are issued against checks on the actual consignment, and both are obtained before shipment and travel with the file, because the destination checks them as part of import clearance.

Answer 1 erases the distinction the memory rule exists for; one certificate cannot speak for both plant health and food safety. Answer 2 confuses these regulatory certificates with commercial inspection certificates, whose contents are contractual and whose examiners are independent inspectors often appointed by the buyer — a different document answering a different question. Answer 4 inverts the timing: the certificates attest the consignment at export, because that is the only moment the issuing authority can see what it is certifying, and they generally cannot be created after arrival.

The takeaway: match the certificate to what the goods are, and the issuing body to the certificate — earned at export, not repaired at import.

=== ASSESSMENT Q10 | tests lesson 6.4 ===
QUESTION:
A clearing agent in Tema is assembling a file for a letter-of-credit presentation and must confirm, before the documents go to the bank, that the consignment was actually loaded on board the vessel named in the draft bill of lading. On the desk are the commercial invoice, the packing list, the insurance certificate and the draft transport document.

Which record answers the agent's question?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The commercial invoice, because the invoice date records when the goods left the seller's warehouse for the port.
2. The packing list, because the package count proves what was physically placed inside the container.
3. The insurance certificate, because cover beginning on the sailing date implies that the goods sailed.
4. The bill of lading itself, specifically the shipped-on-board notation and its date, which is the carrier's confirmation that the goods were loaded on board the named vessel.
CORRECT: 4
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 4.

The set-check discipline begins with knowing which document can answer which question, and loading is the bill of lading's own fact. A document bearing a shipped-on-board notation, confirming that the goods have been loaded on board the specified vessel, says more than one that merely acknowledges receipt alongside or before loading: the notation is the carrier's confirmation that the cargo is on its way, and the notation's date fixes the loading. This is exactly why payment terms and letters of credit frequently demand a shipped-on-board document, and why the agent can resolve the bank's question from the transport document alone.

Answer 1 reaches for the invoice date, which fixes the sale's timeline — payment periods are counted from it and accounting records anchored on it — and says nothing about carriage. Answer 2 takes the packing list's package count for a loading record; the list is the physical script of what should be inside each package, and it is silent on when, or whether, the container was stowed. Answer 3 offers an inference where evidence is required: an insurance certificate evidences cover, not carriage, and a cover date implies nothing about the vessel.

The takeaway: in a set-check, first ask which document is even capable of answering the question — receipt, value, cover or loading — and then read that entry, rather than guessing across the file.

=== ASSESSMENT Q11 | tests lesson 6.4 ===
QUESTION:
At Tema, a customs document check finds that the import permit annexed to a declaration names Meridian Foods Ltd, a trading name the company has stopped using, while the invoice, the bill of lading and the declaration name the company's registered name, Meridian Foods Ghana Ltd. The importer urges the clearing agent to correct the name on the permit in the file by hand, initialling the change, so the container can release the same day.

Which statement best describes the correct course?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The agent should make the hand correction and initial it, because the change is factually accurate: a correction that states the company's true registered name cannot mislead anyone.
2. The mismatch is a discrepancy to be reported to the issuer: the authority that granted the permit reissues or formally amends it through its proper channels, and no trader or agent alters another party's document by hand, because an unauthorised alteration destroys the document's authenticity and casts doubt on the whole file.
3. The agent should type a corrected permit for the file in its own office, because the agent prepared the declaration and is therefore entitled to maintain the documents annexed to it.
4. The discrepancy is immaterial, because regulators match permits to the products they cover rather than to the traders named on them.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The permit is the authority's document, and the remedy follows the amendment rule: the issuer fixes it. A document amendment is an authorised correction made by the party entitled to make it, through proper channels — the carrier's agent for transport documents, the authority for permits, the exporter for invoices — so the importer applies to the authority that granted the permit for a corrected or formally amended document in the registered name. A hand correction by anyone else is not an amendment but an unauthorised alteration, and it damages the document twice: it destroys authenticity, because the page no longer proves the issuer said what it contains, and it raises the worse question of what else in the file was altered. Documents functioning as official certificates can be invalidated outright by apparent alteration.

Answer 1 is the pen trap in its plainest form; factual accuracy is not the test, authority is — the change may be true, but no one is entitled to make it by hand. Answer 3 is the same trap retyped: the agent prepares and lodges the declaration, but a permit annexed to it is nobody's to reissue except its issuer's. Answer 4 dismisses identity, yet the documentary system runs on it: regulators match permits to the trader they name, and the mismatch between permit holder and declared consignee is precisely what the officer's query flagged.

The takeaway: report to the issuer, let the issuer fix it, keep both versions — and run the set-check on receipt, while the mismatch was still free to repair.

=== ASSESSMENT Q12 | tests lesson 6.4 ===
QUESTION:
A documentation clerk in Accra receives an export file in stages: the invoice in the first week; the packing list in the second, showing a total gross weight of 6,900 kilograms; and the draft bill of lading returned by the shipping line in the third, showing 9,600 kilograms for the same consignment. Each document is internally faultless: the invoice arithmetic checks out, the packing list totals agree with its own per-carton column, and the draft bill is complete and correctly worded. Reading each document only on its own, the clerk approves the draft bill, and the container sails. All figures are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Which statement best identifies what the clerk's routine missed?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Nothing was missed: a document that passes its own internal checks is correct, and documents prepared by different hands may legitimately differ.
2. The weights cannot be compared until the goods reach the destination port, so no verification of weights is possible before sailing.
3. Verification has two stages, and the clerk ran only one: each document must be correct in itself, and the set must then be compared element by element — parties, descriptions, quantities, weights, marks, ports and dates — because a file is verified only when it agrees with itself; documents arrive in stages, so the set is re-checked the moment it completes.
4. The clerk should have verified the file only once, after the vessel sailed, because that is the first moment all the facts are final.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

Verification asks two questions of every file, and the clerk ran only the first. The single-document check — blank fields, names, dates, arithmetic — passed, because a transposed total can be internally flawless on every page it lands on. The second stage is the set check: corresponding details are compared element by element — parties, descriptions of goods, quantities and weights, marks and numbers, ports and places, dates — because the test is data consistency, the agreement of corresponding information across related documents. Weights in particular propagate: one wrong figure travels from the packing list into the transport document, the customs declaration and any weight certificate, so the set must be re-checked the moment it completes, while a correction is still a reprint and a corrected instruction rather than a refusal notice at the bank or a query at the border.

Answer 1 states the exact misconception: internal correctness is half the examination, agreement is the other half, and corresponding figures differing between documents is a discrepancy, not a licence. Answers 2 and 4 invert the timing economics in opposite directions: verification is possible and cheapest before issue and while the goods are at sea, and it does not wait for arrival or sailing to make the facts final — a discrepancy found early is fixed by the issuer in minutes, while the same finding later costs days, fees and goodwill.

The takeaway: check each page, then check the set — the file you release must agree with itself.
