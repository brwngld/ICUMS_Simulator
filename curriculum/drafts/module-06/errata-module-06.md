## CHANGE MADE (1)

### C1. Lesson 6.2, Worked Example 1 — impossible container weight

- Was: "800 bags of processed cocoa, about 52 tonnes in one container"
  (and "the 800 bags" in Step 1).
- Now: "400 bags of processed cocoa, about 24 tonnes in one container"
  (and "the 400 bags").
- Why: a single container cannot legally carry 52 tonnes. The research sheet
  (trade-transport-standards.md, section 6) gives payload of about 28,250 kg
  for a 20ft and about 26,700 kg for a 40ft (30,480 kg max gross minus tare);
  even the ISO 668:2020 36,000 kg rating leaves roughly 33 t. The lesson's own
  module-mate (6.1, "Common errors and omissions", item 4 and MCQ 3) teaches
  that implausible weights must be caught before issue — the worked example
  cannot itself carry an impossible weight on a bill of lading. 400 bags at
  about 60 kg per bag (realistic cocoa bag weight) gives about 24 tonnes,
  inside every standard rating. Narrative, arithmetic and all other steps are
  unaffected; no other file references the 800-bag figure.
- The "all figures illustrative" disclaimer covers prices and counts, not
  physical impossibilities; corrected under the conservative-correction rule.

### M2. "Certificates generally not issuable retrospectively" (lessons 6.3 and 6.4) — STANDS, hedge judged adequate

Verified by WebSearch against IPPC ISPM 12 (phytosanitary certificates are
based on inspection/verification of the consignment before export) and national
NPPO practice (USDA APHIS export certification; CFIA export/re-export policy;
Minnesota Dept. of Agriculture timing rules): once a consignment has been
shipped, the exporting NPPO generally cannot certify it retroactively, with
only narrow national windows (e.g. limited post-inspection validity) and
re-export certification as the exception path. The drafts hedge exactly this
way ("generally not possible", "at best re-inspection, at worst refusal") and
assert no country-specific procedure. Accepted as written.
Sources: https://www.ippc.int/static/media/files/publication/en/2016/01/ISPM_12_2014_WithAppendix1_En_2015-04-21.pdf ;

- A1. Bills of lading issued in sets of originals, every original capable of
  releasing the goods until delivery is made against one. Verified by WebSearch:
  standard carrier terms (Ocean Network Express B/L terms: one original duly
  endorsed must be surrendered), the customary clause "one of which being
  accomplished, the other(s) to be void", and trade press.
  Sources: https://www.one-line.com/terms-and-rules/bill-of-lading-terms ;
  https://maritime-executive.com/editorials/why-are-bills-of-lading-issued-in-sets-of-three
- A2. AWB cargo released to the named consignee on identification, no paper
  presented — consistent with the sheet's safe phrasing ("the cargo goes to the
  named consignee, full stop").
- A3. Consigning air cargo to the buyer's bank or another reliable party at
  destination as an unpaid-seller precaution, with all named parties informed —
  standard freight practice, already flagged in the draft's own sources.
- A4. Loss of an original B/L delaying release under the carrier's procedures
  (letter-of-indemnity territory), taught qualitatively with no amounts or
  procedures — matches A1's sources.
- A5. Invoice total exceeding the credit amount being a presentation
  discrepancy with remedies of correction, waiver, or credit amendment —
  standard UCP 600 documentary-credit practice (invoice may not exceed the
  amount available under the credit); taught with no article numbers.
- A6. "MAWB issued by the airline or its authorised agent" (A-item above) —
  uncontentious; kept.
- A7. B/L as "evidence of the contract of carriage, not the contract itself",
  the contract forming at booking — the orthodox textbook position, consistent
  with the sheet's own labelling of function 2.
