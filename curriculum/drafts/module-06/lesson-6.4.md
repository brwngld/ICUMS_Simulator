=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: What document verification is and why it matters
BODY:
The previous lessons of this module built a shipment file piece by piece: the commercial invoice and packing list, the transport documents that carry the goods, and the certificates and permits that satisfy regulators. This closing lesson adds the discipline that holds the file together: document verification.

Document verification is the process of examining documents to confirm their accuracy, completeness, consistency, authenticity, and compliance with applicable requirements. Its opposite is the document discrepancy: an inconsistency, omission, or error found within a document or between related documents. Verification is the systematic search for discrepancies before anyone else finds them.

Why does it matter so much? Because in international trade, documents do work that goods cannot do.

1. Payment
Under documentary payment arrangements, banks pay against documents, not against goods. A bank in the buyer's country never sees the cargo; it reads the file.

2. Clearance
Customs assesses and releases a consignment against the declaration and its supporting documents. Errors in the file follow the goods to the border.

3. Delivery and control
Carriers release cargo to the party their documents name, and buyers verify what they receive against the file. The documents are the operational record of the transaction.

A single discrepancy can therefore stop payment, hold a consignment, or strand cargo, even when the goods themselves are perfect. Verification reduces delays, prevents financial losses, and supports compliance with contractual and regulatory requirements. It is not clerical decoration; it is loss prevention.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The shipment file as a set: what to review
BODY:
Verification begins with knowing what to review. For a typical international shipment, the file to be checked contains four groups.

1. Commercial documents
The commercial invoice and the packing list taught in the lesson on the commercial invoice and packing list. The invoice states the transaction; the packing list states how the goods are packed.

2. Transport documents
The bill of lading, sea waybill, air waybill or road consignment note introduced in the lesson on the bill of lading and air waybill, together with booking confirmations and related carrier documents.

3. Supporting documents
The certificates of origin, permits, health and phytosanitary certificates, inspection and insurance documents, and dangerous goods declarations covered in the lesson on certificates of origin, permits, and supporting documents.

4. Instructions and internal records
The purchase order or sales contract, the customer's or supplier's shipping instructions, and internal records such as the order file. These are the standard against which the documents themselves are checked.

The essential habit is to treat these as one set, not as separate pieces of paper. An invoice can be internally flawless and still be wrong, because the bill of lading describes the goods differently, or a permit names a different buyer. Verification asks two questions of every file: is each document correct in itself, and does the set agree with itself and with the transaction? The rest of the lesson turns those two questions into a working procedure.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Checking each document on its face
BODY:
The first procedure is the single-document check: read each document on its own, before any comparison, and look for four kinds of fault.

1. Blank mandatory fields
Every field the document requires must be completed: shipper, consignee, vessel or flight, ports, package counts, weights, dates, signatures. A blank is not neutral; a bill of lading without a stated port of discharge, or an insurance certificate without the insured sum, is an incomplete document that someone will eventually refuse.

2. Names and spellings
Names of parties must be checked letter by letter. A consignee typed with a missing or transposed letter looks harmless on screen and becomes a real problem at the bank, at the airport, or at customs, where every party acts on the name the document carries. The lesson on transport documents showed that cargo is released to the named party; identity is not a detail.

3. Dates
Every date must be present and sensible: issue dates, shipped-on-board dates, validity periods. A date in the wrong order, or a validity that expires before use, is a discrepancy the next stage will catch — better that this stage does.

4. Arithmetic
Recompute the numbers. An invoice total must equal unit price multiplied by quantity, and line totals must add up to the grand total. Package counts on a packing list must agree between the summary and the item list, and the weights must add to the stated total. Arithmetic is the one check that needs no telephone call to anyone: the numbers prove or disprove themselves.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Comparing across the set: data consistency
BODY:
The second procedure is the set check: comparing corresponding details across documents. What you are testing is data consistency: the agreement of corresponding information across related documents and records. The lesson on the commercial invoice and packing list began this discipline for that pair of documents; verification extends it to the whole file. Work through the set element by element.

1. Parties
The seller, buyer, consignee and notifying party must appear identically on the invoice, packing list, transport documents, permits and certificates — names and addresses alike.

2. Description of goods
The goods must be described consistently everywhere: not necessarily worded identically, but never contradicting one another.

3. Quantities and weights
Carton counts, unit counts and weights must agree between the invoice, the packing list, the transport document and any inspection or weight certificate.

4. Marks and numbers
The shipping marks and package numbers that identify the cargo physically must be the same on the packing list and the transport document, or the link between paper and cargo breaks.

5. Ports and places
Port of loading, port of discharge, place of delivery, place of issue.

6. Dates and deadlines
Shipment dates, issue dates, expiry and presentation deadlines, permit validity.

The logic is the same control that procurement has applied for decades under the name three-way match: the purchase order, the goods receipt note and the supplier invoice must agree before payment is released. Document verification applies that discipline to the trade file. No document is trusted on its own; each is trusted because the set agrees.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Authenticity, validity and completeness
BODY:
A set can agree internally and still fail, if the documents themselves are not what they claim to be. Three further properties must be checked.

1. Authenticity
Document authentication is the process of confirming that a document is genuine or has been issued or endorsed by an authorised party. Is the issuer who it claims to be? Is the document signed or stamped by the body entitled to issue it? A certificate of origin is worth what the certifying body behind it is worth; an endorsement must come from the party entitled to endorse. Missing stamps, photocopied signatures, or a certificate whose reference cannot be traced to the issuing body are authenticity warnings.

2. Validity
Document validation is the process of checking whether a document satisfies specified rules, formats, or acceptance requirements. The right kind of document, in the form the requirement demands, with dates still live: a permit consulted after expiry is, as the previous lesson put it, simply a missing permit.

3. Completeness
The file must contain everything the transaction requires: every document, every original where originals are demanded, every page. A set of three original bills of lading is not complete at two.

The overall examination — are these the right documents, genuine, current, complete, and do they satisfy the legal, regulatory and contractual requirements that apply — is called a compliance review. Verification without it checks the arithmetic and misses the expired page and the forgery.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Discrepancies of names, addresses and descriptions
BODY:
The common discrepancies cluster into families, and the first family concerns identity and description.

1. Party errors
A misspelled consignee; a buyer's address carried over from an old order; a notifying party missing altogether. Under a documentary collection the bank hands documents to the buyer it can identify; a carrier releases cargo against its own documents; customs matches permits to traders. In every case the check is against the name on the page.

2. Address and reference errors
Wrong addresses, post-office boxes from a previous year, container or booking numbers keyed incorrectly. These break the link between document and shipment even when the goods are correct.

3. Cargo description mismatches
A cargo description mismatch is a discrepancy arising when the description of goods differs between documents or does not accurately reflect the cargo. The classic case: the invoice says frozen shrimp, 500 cartons, and the bill of lading says only seafood. The carrier may accept a general description on its document; customs at destination cannot classify seafood, and a bank comparing documents literally will treat the difference as a discrepancy. Descriptions must be consistent across the set and accurate to the cargo — specific enough for classification, identical in substance everywhere they appear.

The defining feature of this family is that the goods are usually fine. The paper disagrees about who is involved or what is being moved, and the disagreement alone is enough to stop payment or clearance.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Discrepancies of quantities, weights and arithmetic
BODY:
The second family concerns numbers.

1. Quantity discrepancies
A quantity discrepancy is a difference between the quantity of goods stated in documents and the quantity recorded elsewhere or physically verified. The invoice is drawn for 600 cartons; the packing list and the bill of lading both show 620. Which is right? The set does not say, and until it is resolved, no downstream party can rely on it. Quantities must also be checked against physical reality: the count taken at packing or loading is the fact the documents must match.

2. Weight discrepancies
A weight discrepancy is a difference between declared and recorded or measured cargo weights. The most common cause is transposition: 18,600 kilograms keyed as 16,800, or a per-carton weight changed without the total being updated. Weights propagate — from the packing list into the transport document, the customs declaration and any weight certificate — so one wrong figure multiplies as it travels.

3. Values and arithmetic errors
The invoice value must agree with the contract and, under documentary payment, with the amount of the credit. Totals must equal unit price multiplied by quantity, line totals must sum to the grand total, and package counts must agree within the same document. These are the cheapest discrepancies to find and the most embarrassing to present to a bank.

All figures in this lesson are illustrative for teaching purposes. The habit to build is mechanical: recompute every total, and compare every number against the same number in the other documents, before the file leaves your hands.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Discrepancies of timing and omission
BODY:
The third family concerns when documents exist, and whether they exist at all.

1. Late issuance
A transport document must reflect the shipment it records: a shipped-on-board date earlier than the document's issue, a certificate dated long after the consignment it certifies. Payment arrangements built on documents set deadlines around the shipment date, and a document issued late, or presented late, can be refused on timing alone. The previous lesson made the parallel point for certificates: they attest the consignment at export, and issuing them retrospectively afterwards is generally not possible.

2. Missing documents
The required certificate never obtained; the permit still in process when the goods sail; one original of a three-original set lost. An omission is a discrepancy of the whole file, and it is usually discovered at the worst moment: when the file is presented and something is not there.

3. Informal fixes
When an error is found, the temptation is to reach for a pen. Resist it. A hand-corrected document — a figure crossed out and re-written, a name initialled in the margin — is not a document amendment. A document amendment is an authorised correction or modification made to a previously issued document: made by the issuer, through proper channels, recorded as a change. Anything else is alteration, and an apparently altered document invites the suspicion that other parts were altered too.

Timing and omission discrepancies are the family verification is best at preventing, because both are visible early: the diary shows the deadline, and the checklist shows the gap.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: What discrepancies cost: payment and clearance
BODY:
Discrepancies are not aesthetic problems. They have price tags, paid in two currencies: money and time.

1. Payment consequences
The lesson on international trade payment methods explained that under documentary collections and letters of credit, banks work with documents: in a documentary collection the banks pass documents against payment or acceptance without any undertaking to pay, and under a letter of credit the issuing bank pays only against complying documents. Because banks deal in documents and not in goods, a discrepancy lets the buyer refuse or delay payment even where the goods are exactly as ordered. A seller may be shipping sound cargo while its paperwork gives the buyer a lawful reason to withhold money.

2. Clearance consequences
At customs, documents that disagree with the declaration or with each other trigger queries, document checks and inspection. A consignment whose permit does not match its declaration, or whose weights differ between documents, waits while the file is repaired — and while it waits, time-based storage charges accumulate and downstream customers wait with it.

3. Compliance consequences
Documents that are altered, back-dated or false do not merely delay; they create compliance exposure, and false declarations are treated as fraud. A verification culture protects a business from its own paper.

The pattern across all three is the same: the cost of a discrepancy grows at every stage it survives. Caught at preparation it costs minutes; caught at payment or the border, it costs days, fees and goodwill.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Correcting a discrepancy: the amendment rule
BODY:
When verification finds a discrepancy, the remedy follows one rule: the issuer fixes it. The procedure has four steps.

1. Report immediately
Discrepancies are reported to the party who issued the document — the exporter for an invoice, the carrier or its agent for a transport document, the authority for a permit or certificate — the moment they are found. Delay shrinks the options.

2. The issuer reissues or amends
A corrected invoice is reissued by the exporter. A transport document is amended or reissued by the carrier through its agent. A permit is reissued by the authority that granted it. A document amendment is an authorised correction made by the party entitled to make it, through proper channels.

3. Never alter a document you did not issue
No trader, agent or bank corrects another party's document by hand, however small the error appears. An unauthorised alteration can invalidate the document and raise questions about everything else in the file.

4. Keep the trail
Changes are recorded. An audit trail is a chronological record of activities or changes that allows transactions and document modifications to be traced. Keep the superseded version and the replacement, and note who requested the change and when. If a dispute follows, the trail shows the file was corrected, not doctored.

Amendment is cheapest before the document is used: before the bill of lading is issued, before presentation to the bank, before the declaration is lodged. After use, every correction costs more.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: A working verification routine
BODY:
Verification becomes reliable when it stops depending on memory. The tool is the document checklist: a structured list used to verify the availability, completeness, and accuracy of required documents. A workable routine runs as follows.

1. Build the list early
At contract or booking stage, list every document the transaction requires. The lesson on supporting documents showed how the goods, their origin, the destination and the regulations in between determine that list.

2. Check before issue
Whoever prepares the documents verifies the set as it is assembled: invoice against order, packing list against the physical count, draft transport document against both, certificates and permits against the goods and parties. This is the moment errors are cheap.

3. Check again on receipt
The moment documents reach you — from a supplier, an agent or a branch — check them again. Never assume the sender verified. Early detection is cheap detection: a discrepancy found while the goods are still at sea can be fixed before arrival.

4. Run the checks in order
Internal completeness and arithmetic first; then the set comparison — parties, descriptions, quantities, weights, marks, ports, dates; then authenticity, validity and completeness, the compliance review.

5. Report and track discrepancies
Any finding is reported immediately to whoever can fix it, and followed until the reissued document arrives.

Behind the routine sits document control: the systematic management of document preparation, review, approval, storage, access, and revision. Version control, central files and clear responsibility are what make the checklist trustworthy — so that the file you check is the file that travels.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: The weight that almost sailed: a pre-issuance set-check at Tema
BODY:
A frozen-food exporter in Tema is dispatching 1,200 cartons of fish fillets to a buyer in Antwerp, by sea. The shipment file is nearly complete. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: Assemble the set
The clerk lays out the commercial invoice (1,200 cartons at 20 US dollars per carton), the packing list (1,200 cartons, 15 kilograms gross per carton), the verified gross mass declaration for the container, and the draft bill of lading the shipping line has returned for approval ahead of the documentation cut-off.

Step 2: Check each document
The invoice arithmetic passes: 1,200 multiplied by 20 gives 24,000 dollars. The packing list fails: its summary line reads 10,800 kilograms gross in total, although the per-carton column works out to 18,000. The total was keyed with its digits re-ordered.

Step 3: Check the set
The draft bill of lading repeats 10,800 kilograms — the line typed its document from the same wrong figure. The verified gross mass declaration, obtained by weighing the packed container, shows the true 18,000 kilograms. The set disagrees with itself.

Step 4: Report and correct
The clerk reports the discrepancy the same morning. The exporter reissues the packing list and sends corrected shipping instructions to the line before the cut-off. The issued bill of lading shows 18,000 kilograms, agreeing with every other document in the file.

What does this example demonstrate?

The error cost one reissued page and one corrected instruction — minutes of work, before the bill of lading existed. Caught after issue, it would have required a formal amendment through the line. Caught after sailing, it would have travelled into a bank presentation and a customs declaration as a weight discrepancy, forcing corrections in three directions at once while time-based charges ran at destination.

Key lesson: a set-check before issue converts an amendment into a reprint.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A discrepancy at the bank: the invoice and the credit disagree
BODY:
An engineering exporter in Mumbai ships machine components to a buyer in Hamburg under a letter of credit. The credit, as the lesson on international trade payment methods described, is the issuing bank's undertaking to pay against complying documents. All figures are illustrative.

Step 1: The terms
The credit is for 250,000 US dollars, covering 5,000 components at 50 dollars each, with documents to be presented within the stated time after shipment.

Step 2: The shipment
The exporter ships exactly 5,000 components. The bill of lading is issued and shows the correct consignment.

Step 3: The error
Preparing the invoice, the clerk keys the unit price as 55 instead of 50. The invoice total reads 275,000 dollars. Nobody recomputes it: 5,000 at 50 is 250,000, the amount of the credit.

Step 4: Presentation and refusal
The bank examines the documents on their face. It does not ask whether the goods are sound — banks deal in documents, not goods. The invoice total exceeds the amount of the credit, so the presentation does not comply, and the bank gives notice of refusal listing the discrepancy. Payment stops, in Mumbai and Hamburg alike, over one keyed digit.

Step 5: The remedies
The exporter has the classic options. It corrects the documents while time remains: a reissued invoice for 250,000 dollars is presented within the validity of the credit, the presentation now complies, and payment proceeds. Had the correction missed the deadline, the remaining options would have been to ask the buyer to accept the discrepancy or to have the credit amended — both cost days and goodwill — and failing those, the exporter would face delayed or refused payment on a shipment that was physically perfect.

What does this example demonstrate?

A discrepancy does not have to be large to be serious; it has to exist. The bank paid nothing against intentions or good goods, only against documents, and one unverified total nearly redirected the payment through refusal, waiver or delay.

Key lesson: under documentary payment, verify the invoice against the credit before presentation, not after the refusal notice.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: The permit that did not match: a query at Tema
BODY:
A beverage company in Accra imports 40,000 litres of fruit juice concentrate by sea to Tema. The products require authorisation from the Food and Drugs Authority, obtained before shipment as the previous lesson required. The company and figures are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: The permit
Months before shipping, the company secures the FDA authorisation. It is issued in the name on the company's registration: Sunlab Foods Ltd — a name the company has since shortened informally to Sunlab in its day-to-day correspondence.

Step 2: The documents
The supplier abroad prepares the invoice, packing list and bill of lading from the order correspondence — and types the buyer as Sunlab Beverages Ltd, a trading name picked up from an email signature. Nobody compares the new documents against the permit on file.

Step 3: The declaration
The vessel berths at Tema. The clearing agent lodges the customs declaration through ICUMS, Ghana's single-window e-customs platform, annexing the invoice, the transport document and the permit. The file is selected for a document check, and the officer's query is immediate: the permit holder does not match the declared consignee.

Step 4: The cost
Release stops. The importer must apply to the authority for a corrected permit in the proper name — an issuer's act, which takes days. While the consignment waits, time-based storage charges accumulate, and the production plan built on the concentrate slips. The agent's explanation is the weakest sentence in trade: the goods are right, only the paper disagrees.

Step 5: The prevention
The mismatch was visible weeks earlier. The moment the supplier's documents arrived — long before the vessel sailed — a set-check comparing the permit's name against the invoice and the bill of lading would have caught it, and the corrected paperwork would have been ready before the goods were.

What does this example demonstrate?

Customs does not clear intentions; it clears files. A permit valid in every other respect fails when the name on it is not the name in the declaration.

Key lesson: documents reach you long before the goods do; the window between receipt and arrival is when verification is cheapest.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Verify before issue, not after
BODY:
The most expensive verification habit is checking late. Documents move faster than corrections: once a document is issued it begins travelling — to the bank, into the customs system, to the buyer — and a correction cannot overtake it. A wrong figure caught before issue costs a reprint; the same figure caught after presentation costs a refusal notice and a race against a deadline; caught at the border, it costs queries, days of delay and time-based charges while the consignment sits.

The operational habit follows. No document should be issued, and no set released, without verification — the check is part of preparation, not a favour done afterwards. And because documents arrive in stages over weeks, the set is re-checked the moment it completes: an invoice verified in isolation means little until it can be compared against the transport document that finally names the vessel.

We will fix it later is the sentence that precedes most documentation losses. Later is when the document is in three other hands, when the correction needs an issuer on another continent, and when the goods have arrived without their paperwork.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: It is never only a spelling
BODY:
Beginners dismiss name errors as cosmetic. Professionals do not, because the documentary system runs on identity. Banks pay the party named in the credit; carriers release cargo to the consignee their document names — and for a negotiable bill of lading, to whoever holds it properly endorsed; customs and regulators match permits, declarations and certificates to the trader they name. Each of these parties checks the name on the page, not the intention in the trader's head.

A consignee typed with one letter missing, a permit in the company's old name, a notifying party left blank — each looks like only a spelling, and each is a real discrepancy: enough for a bank to refuse a presentation, an airline to withhold freight, or an officer to raise a query that holds the goods.

The standard is exact agreement across the set: names spelled identically, addresses current, and legal names used where legal names are required — as they are on permits and declarations. Where a trading name and a registered name differ, the registered name appears on the documents that carry legal consequences, and the whole set uses it consistently.

It is only a spelling is how costly delays begin.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Never correct another party's document by hand
BODY:
An error is found on a document someone else issued — a weight on the bill of lading, a description on a certificate, a name on a permit. The fastest-looking fix is a pen: strike the error, write the correction, initial the margin. It is also the one fix that makes everything worse.

A hand-corrected document is not an amendment. An amendment is the issuer's act: an authorised correction made through proper channels, recorded and traceable. A pen change by anyone else is an unauthorised alteration, and it damages the document twice. First, it destroys authenticity: the document no longer proves the issuer said what it contains, and for documents that function as documents of title or official certificates, apparent alteration can invalidate them outright. Second, it raises the worse question: if this page was changed by hand, what else in the file was?

The correct sequence is always the same. Report the error to the issuer. Let the issuer reissue the document or formally amend it through its channels — the carrier's agent for transport documents, the authority for permits, the exporter for invoices. Keep both versions so the change is traceable. Even for documents you issued yourself, reissue rather than overwrite: a clean paper trail is worth more than a tidy-looking page.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A freight forwarder in Tema is checking a shipment file before the documentation cut-off. The commercial invoice describes 500 cartons of frozen shrimp; the draft bill of lading returned by the shipping line reads only "seafood".

Which action is the correct next step?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Ask the carrier's agent to amend the draft bill of lading so that it agrees with the invoice before the bill is issued.
2. Leave the bill of lading as it is, because customs at destination does not read cargo descriptions.
3. Correct the wording by hand on the printed draft and initial the change before the bill is issued.
4. Wait until the goods arrive at destination and let the buyer request the correction from the carrier.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

The draft bill of lading and the invoice disagree about the cargo — a cargo description mismatch, one of the common discrepancy families this lesson identifies. The discrepancy is cheapest now: the bill has not yet been issued and the documentation cut-off has not passed, so the right action is to report it to the carrier's agent and have the document amended through proper channels before issuance. Descriptions must be consistent across the set and accurate to the cargo, because customs classifies goods by description and banks compare documents literally.

Answer 3 is the trap the amendment rule closes: a hand correction on a carrier's document is an unauthorised alteration, not an amendment, and it can invalidate the document and cast doubt on the whole file. Answer 2 misjudges the consequence — descriptions drive classification and document checks, and a vague mismatch surfaces as a query exactly when it costs most. Answer 4 postpones the fix to the most expensive moment, after the document has travelled.

The takeaway: find it in the draft, fix it through the issuer, and the error never sails.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
An exporter presents documents under a letter of credit. The bank finds that the invoice total does not agree with the amount stated in the credit, although the goods were shipped exactly as ordered.

Which statement best describes the position?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The bank must pay, because payment depends on the goods and the goods were shipped correctly.
2. The bank may refuse payment until the documents comply or the buyer accepts the discrepancy, because banks deal in documents rather than goods.
3. The bank must pay, because a small difference between the invoice total and the credit amount is always tolerated.
4. The bank must arrange for the goods to be returned to the exporter at the buyer's cost.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The lesson on international trade payment methods established that under a letter of credit the issuing bank's undertaking is to pay against complying documents, and this lesson adds the operating rule behind it: banks deal in documents, not goods. The goods were shipped exactly as ordered, but the bank never sees the goods — it examines the documents on their face, and an invoice total that does not agree with the credit is a discrepancy. The bank may refuse the non-complying presentation, giving notice of the discrepancy, and payment waits until the documents are corrected in time, the buyer accepts the discrepancy, or the credit is amended.

Answer 1 is precisely the misconception the documents-not-goods rule removes: payment does not follow the goods. Answer 3 invents a tolerance that no principle in this lesson supports; a discrepancy exists or it does not. Answer 4 confuses the bank's documentary role with physical cargo handling the bank does not perform.

The takeaway: under documentary payment, the file is the payment — verify it against the credit before presentation.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
An importer in Accra receives the document set for a consignment of detergent. The commercial invoice is drawn for 600 cartons. The packing list and the bill of lading both show 620 packages.

Which statement best describes what the importer should do?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Accept the invoice figure, because the invoice is the most important document in the set.
2. Correct the invoice by hand to read 620 cartons, so that all documents show the same figure.
3. Query the discrepancy with the exporter immediately and have the responsible document reissued before the set is used for payment or clearance.
4. Ignore the difference, because a packing list figure against an invoice figure has no effect on duty or payment.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

Six hundred against 620 is a quantity discrepancy: a difference between the quantity stated in one document and the quantity recorded elsewhere in the set. The set check exists because no document can be trusted alone — until the discrepancy is resolved, no downstream party can rely on the file for payment, duty assessment or release. The remedy follows the amendment rule: query immediately with the exporter, establish which figure is wrong against the physical count, and have the responsible document reissued before the set is used.

Answer 1 inverts the logic of verification: the invoice's importance does not make it correct, and the set must agree before any part of it is used. Answer 2 is the hand-correction trap: altering an exporter's document is not an amendment, and it damages the document's authenticity. Answer 4 underestimates the consequences: quantities feed duty assessment, payment under documentary arrangements and cargo examination, so an unreconciled count is a query waiting for the worst moment.

The takeaway: when documents disagree about a number, stop the file and let the issuer fix the wrong document.
