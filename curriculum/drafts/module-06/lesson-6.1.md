=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Two documents, two stories
BODY:
Lesson 3.4, Introduction to Logistics Documents, gave you the map: five families of documents, each answering one question about a shipment. Module 6 now walks deep into that map, beginning with the two commercial documents that head almost every shipment file.

The commercial invoice is a document issued by a seller to a buyer that provides details of goods sold, their prices, and the terms of the commercial transaction. It is the document of the sale. Everything financial about the transaction lives on it: who sold, who bought, what was sold, at what price, in which currency, and on what conditions.

The packing list is a document describing the contents, quantities, packaging, weights, and dimensions of goods included in a shipment. It is the document of the consignment, the physical parcel of goods that actually travels. Nothing about prices appears on it; everything about the packages does.

Beginners often treat the two as one bundle of paperwork; they answer different questions. The commercial invoice answers: what was agreed between seller and buyer, and what is it worth? The packing list answers: what is physically inside this shipment, and how is it packed? Every shipment therefore tells two stories that must never contradict each other.

Between them they support shipment identification, cargo verification, customs procedures, and transportation planning, which is why customs officers, bank staff, carriers, warehouse checkers and consignees all read one or both.

This lesson teaches each document field by field, shows who reads which, and confronts the discipline holding the file together: the details must agree from document to document, and both must agree with the actual cargo.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The commercial invoice: identifying the transaction
BODY:
A commercial invoice begins by establishing who the transaction is between and giving itself a unique name in the seller's records. These identity fields look administrative, yet they are the first examined whenever anything goes wrong, and a missing or duplicated number is among the commonest errors in commercial documents.

1. Seller and buyer details
The invoice states the full legal names and addresses of the seller and the buyer. The details should match the sales contract or purchase order that authorised the transaction, the agreement Lesson 4.2, Buyers, Sellers, and International Trade Agreements, placed at the centre of international trade; a customs officer or bank clerk must see at a glance which two organisations the transaction belongs to.

2. Invoice number
The invoice number is a unique reference assigned to an invoice for identification and recordkeeping. No two invoices from the same seller should share a number, and well-run businesses issue them in one continuous sequence. The number is how everyone points to this document: a payment, a bank's letter and a customs declaration all reference an invoice number, never a phrase like the invoice for the cream.

3. Invoice date
The date establishes the timeline: payment periods are counted from it, banks compare it with the transport document dates, and it fixes the invoice in the accounting records.

4. Reference to the sales contract or purchase order
Quoting the buyer's purchase order number, or the sales contract reference, ties the invoice to the agreement it carries out. This habit later allows the buyer to match order, receipt and invoice before releasing payment, a check this lesson returns to.

5. Currency of transaction
The currency of transaction is the currency in which the commercial transaction is priced or payable. It may be neither party's local currency: a Ghanaian seller and a British buyer commonly agree to invoice in United States dollars. State the currency clearly once, and keep every figure on the invoice in that same currency.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The commercial invoice: describing and pricing the goods
BODY:
With the parties identified, the invoice turns to the goods themselves: the heart of the document, and the field where quality is most visible.

1. Goods description
The goods description is the information identifying the nature, type, and characteristics of the goods being sold. The professional test is simple: could a stranger, a customs officer who has never seen the shipment, tell from the description alone what is in the boxes? A description reading spare parts fails that test. Spare parts for what? Vehicles, textile machines, or computers? A description reading shea butter body cream, 500 millilitre bottles, passes it.

The description matters because customs decisions hang on it. Authorities worldwide classify goods under the Harmonized System, the World Customs Organization's international product nomenclature, and duty treatment, permits and risk screening flow from that classification. No correct classification can be built on a description that says nothing. The description must also agree, in substance, with the packing list and the transport document.

2. Quantities and unit price
Quantities are stated in clear units: bottles, pieces, pairs, kilograms. The unit price is the price charged for one unit, and each invoice line multiplies quantity by unit price to give a line total any reader can reconstruct.

3. Total invoice value
The total invoice value is the total monetary value of the goods and any charges stated on the invoice. It is the most consequential number on the document: the customs assessment is built upon it, and the seller demands payment against it. An arithmetic error here is not a small fault; it is a wrong declaration.

4. Country of origin
The invoice states the country where the goods were produced or manufactured. Origin drives customs treatment, and it must agree with any certificate of origin presented with the shipment; certificates receive their own lesson later in this module.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The commercial invoice: conditions and uses
BODY:
The final invoice fields record the commercial conditions agreed between the parties and, with the price fields, give the document its three great uses.

1. Incoterms rule and named place
The invoice normally restates the agreed Incoterms 2020 rule together with its named place, for example CIF Tema, Incoterms 2020. Lesson 4.3, Incoterms and Allocation of Responsibilities, explained what the rules do: allocate between seller and buyer the tasks, costs and risks of delivery, from carriage and insurance to customs clearance. Stating rule, named place and year lets every downstream reader see at a glance who is responsible for what. Naming the rule without the year, or without the place, is an incomplete entry that professionals query.

2. Payment terms
The invoice states how and when payment is to be made: in advance, on open account such as 30 days from invoice date, through a documentary collection, or under a letter of credit. Lesson 4.4, International Trade Payment Methods, covered the methods; the invoice carries whichever was agreed into daily operation.

3. The three uses of the invoice
First, it is the customs valuation basis at import: customs begins valuing goods from the price actually paid or payable for them, the transaction value, and the commercial invoice is where that price is declared. Second, it is the payment support under the agreed method: where banks are involved, banks deal in documents rather than goods, so the invoice must be exact. Third, it is the record of the sale: in any disagreement over what was sold, at what price and on what terms, the invoice is the central evidence both parties hold.

An invoice serving three audiences at once is no casual paperwork: a financial instrument, a customs declaration's foundation, and a legal record in one page.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The packing list: describing the consignment
BODY:
Defined at the start of this lesson as the document of the consignment, the packing list now yields its components, each answering one physical question about the goods.

1. Package count
The package count is the total number of packages or handling units in the shipment. State the unit precisely: 50 cartons on 5 pallets says more than 50 packages, because it shows how the cartons are grouped for handling.

2. Contents per package
The list states what each package holds and how many units it contains. This lets a checker know what should be inside each numbered carton and find a specific article without opening everything.

3. Gross weight
The gross weight is the total weight of the goods including their packaging, but excluding transport equipment unless otherwise specified. Carriers plan loads in gross tonnes, so this is the figure transport lives by.

4. Net weight
The net weight is the weight of the goods themselves, excluding the packaging. The difference between gross and net is the packaging, and the relationship is fixed: gross weight can never be less than net weight.

5. Dimensions
The list gives the size, and sometimes the volume, of each package. Dimensions decide stowage: how many cartons fit a container or aircraft hold, whether pallets stack, and what handling they require.

6. Shipping marks and numbers
Shipping marks are identification symbols, numbers, or other markings placed on packages to assist with shipment identification and handling. A typical mark names the consignment and numbers each package in sequence, for example KFT/LONDON/1 through KFT/LONDON/50. Sequential numbering means a missing package announces itself: if carton 37 never arrives, no one has to argue about a total count.

Notice what the packing list does not carry: prices. It is a physical record, and every figure on it serves someone who must count, weigh, load or inspect. Together they let anyone take any single carton and know where it belongs.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Who uses the packing list, and for what
BODY:
The packing list earns its place in the file by being useful to at least four different readers, each for a different reason.

1. The warehouse checker
At dispatch, warehouse staff pick and count against it: 50 cartons means 50 cartons physically loaded, each bearing its mark. At receipt, the receiving warehouse counts in reverse. The packing list is the counting sheet for both ends of the journey, and it lets a shortage be discovered while the truck or container still stands at the dock, where it can be investigated.

2. Carriers and terminal planners
Weights and dimensions drive load planning, stowage and the choice of equipment. In air transport the figures are not merely informative: air cargo is charged on the higher of gross weight and a volumetric weight computed from the dimensions, so the packing list affects the freight bill itself. In sea transport the shipper must provide the verified gross mass of a packed container before it may be loaded, and verified package weights of this kind are where that declaration comes from.

3. The consignee
The buyer verifies receipt package by package against the marks. A consignment claimed complete becomes a claim, honestly and precisely, when the consignee can write: cartons 37 and 41 not received. Vague complaints lose; marked and numbered records stand.

4. Customs examiners
When a consignment is selected for examination, the packing list is the script for the search. It tells the officer what should be inside each package, so the physical goods can be compared against the paperwork package by package.

One document, four readers, four different jobs: accuracy matters more than elegance, and a beautifully formatted but wrongly counted list is a loaded weapon in a logistics file.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Commercial information versus physical information
BODY:
The deepest idea in this lesson is that the two documents embody two kinds of information about one shipment. Sorting any detail into its correct home is a working skill.

1. Commercial information
The parties to the sale, the prices, the currency, the total invoice value, the payment terms and the Incoterms 2020 rule answer the question: what was this worth, and on what terms? It lives on the commercial invoice, read by accounts teams who must pay, banks who must settle and customs officers who must value the goods.

2. Physical information
The package count, the contents of each package, the gross and net weights, the dimensions and the shipping marks answer the question: what is travelling, and how is it packed? It lives on the packing list, read by warehouse staff who must count, carriers who must load, consignees who must verify and inspectors who must examine.

3. The shared core
Some details appear on both documents: the parties, the goods description, the total quantities and the shipment references. Wherever a detail appears twice, it must be identical both times. Fifty cartons is fifty cartons on the invoice, on the packing list, and on the transport document, or the file is broken.

Why two documents rather than one? Because the readers make different decisions: a forklift operator needs the weight of a pallet, not its price; a bank needs the total value, not the dimensions of a carton. One document serving both would bury what each reader needs under what the other needs.

The working habit follows: before looking for a detail, know which question you are asking. Prices and terms belong to the invoice. Packages, weights and marks belong to the packing list.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: How the two documents support logistics and customs
BODY:
Objectives matter only in operations. This block traces where the two documents actually do their work.

1. At the border
The import declaration is prepared from the two documents: the value is declared from the invoice, the quantities, weights and package details from the packing list, and the classification from the goods description. Many customs administrations, Ghana's included, risk-assess declarations and channel them to release without inspection, document check, or physical examination. Whatever the channel, the officer works from these documents, so their quality decides whether a consignment moves or is held. In Ghana the declaration is lodged electronically in ICUMS, the Integrated Customs Management System, normally by a licensed clearing agent acting for the importer; the documents behind it must still be right.

2. In payment
The commercial invoice drives whichever payment method was agreed. Where a bank is involved, the bank examines documents, not goods, so an exporter presenting papers under a letter of credit is paid against documents that must be exact. An invoice error in this world can pause payment for the whole shipment.

3. In the buyer's own control
Before paying, a careful buyer matches three records: the purchase order, the goods receipt and the supplier's invoice. This is the three-way match, and it works only when the receipt was counted against a packing list and the invoice states the same goods at the same prices. The two documents of this lesson are two of its three legs.

4. In transport operations
Package counts and weights flow into bookings, space planning and the manifests carriers declare to authorities. A carrier's plan for a container, an aircraft hold or a truck is only as good as the weights and dimensions the documents gave it.

Four operations, one conclusion: these documents are the working tools of every participant the goods will meet, not paperwork trailing behind them.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: One source of truth: consistency across the file
BODY:
Now the discipline that holds everything together. The quantities and descriptions in a shipment must agree between the commercial invoice, the packing list and the transport documents, and all of them must agree with the actual cargo.

Agreement should be natural, yet often is not, because of how the documents are born. Lesson 5.3, Shipping Instructions and Cargo Consolidation, taught that the shipping instructions are the single authoritative source from which the carrier prepares the transport document. The invoice and the packing list draw on the same facts, the order and the physical dispatch, but are usually prepared by different hands, in different offices, and often retyped from different records. Every retyping is an opportunity for divergence.

Consider what full agreement looks like. Fifty cartons appears as 50 on the invoice, 50 on the packing list, and 50 on the bill of lading or air waybill, and the marks on the boxes run 1 to 50 with every carton present. One number, five appearances, no contradiction.

Now consider what disagreement does. A customs officer faced with an invoice for 50 cartons and a transport document for 48 cannot verify the declaration, so the consignment waits. A bank faced with contradicting documents stops payment. A consignee facing 48 physical cartons against papers claiming 50 cannot sign a clean receipt. And a discrepancy rarely announces its own cause: typing error, miscount at packing, or two cartons genuinely missing; nobody can tell until someone traces it, while the goods stand still.

The professional habit follows: check documents against each other, not against memory, and finally against the goods themselves. Consistency lets every participant act on the file without seeing what is inside the boxes.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Common errors and omissions
BODY:
Because invoice and packing list details are copied, retyped and exchanged between organisations, the same errors recur across the industry. Recognising them is half of preventing them.

1. Missing or duplicated invoice numbers
A payment or a declaration cannot reference the document cleanly, and duplicate numbers corrupt both parties' records.

2. Vague goods descriptions
General goods, spare parts, samples, accessories. Classification cannot start, value cannot be sanity-checked against known products, and verification has nothing to compare the goods against.

3. Quantity mismatches
The invoice says one count, the packing list another, the transport document a third. Every reader is stopped until the truth is traced.

4. Implausible weights
Net weight greater than gross weight is impossible, since gross includes the packaging. Per-carton weights no worker could lift, or totals that disagree with the carrier's weighed figures, flag the same carelessness.

5. Missing or inconsistent currency
A total without a stated currency, or lines priced in different currencies, leaves the value unverifiable.

6. Arithmetic errors
A line total that does not equal quantity multiplied by unit price, or lines that do not sum to the total invoice value, undermines the number the assessment rests on.

7. Missing country of origin
Origin drives customs treatment, and its absence invites queries that a single field would have prevented.

8. Incomplete delivery terms
An Incoterms 2020 rule without its named place or its year tells readers nothing reliable about who bears which cost and risk.

9. Missing or inconsistent shipping marks
Numbers on the list that appear on no box, or boxes bearing marks on no list, defeat the purpose of marking.

10. Missing dates or references
No invoice date, no purchase order reference, no link between the documents, and the file fragments into unconnected papers.

None of these errors requires bad faith. They are prevention problems, not accidents, and the review method in the next block catches nearly all of them in minutes.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Reviewing an invoice and packing list before issue
BODY:
One objective of this lesson is a skill: reviewing an invoice and packing list for accuracy. Professionals use a fixed method, so nothing depends on mood or memory. The method below takes minutes and repays them on every shipment.

1. Check the identity fields
Seller and buyer named in full, with addresses. An invoice number, unique and in sequence. An invoice date. A purchase order or contract reference. A currency, stated once and used throughout. Any missing field is a query before anything else is checked.

2. Read the description as a stranger
Ask: could someone who has never seen the shipment tell from the description what is in the boxes and how to classify it? If the honest answer is no, the description is rewritten, not excused. Spare parts becomes brake pads for light commercial vehicles, with part numbers.

3. Verify the arithmetic
Multiply each line's quantity by its unit price and confirm the line total. Add the line totals and compare the result with the total invoice value. Systems do this arithmetic too, but someone must notice when the input was typed wrongly.

4. Test the physical figures for plausibility
Gross weight at least equal to net weight. Per-carton weights a person or machine could handle. Totals consistent with the nature of the goods. Package counts matching the mark sequence: marks numbered 1 to 50 mean 50 packages, no more and no less.

5. Cross-check the documents against each other
Descriptions, total quantities, parties and marks identical across the invoice, the packing list and the carrier's details. Where a detail appears twice, it must be the same twice.

6. Finally, check paper against goods
The decisive step: the physical dispatch is counted against the packing list before the container is sealed or the truck leaves. No document is issued, and none signed, until its claims are verified against reality.

A review of this kind is the cheapest insurance in logistics: minutes spent at dispatch against days lost at the border.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A complete pair: cosmetics from Accra to London
BODY:
A cosmetics producer in Accra receives a purchase order from a beauty retailer in London for shea butter body cream. The terms are confirmed, the goods packed, and the producer prepares the two documents of this lesson. All figures are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: The commercial invoice, field by field
The invoice names the seller, the Accra producer, and the buyer, the London retailer, with addresses matching the purchase order. It carries invoice number INV-2026-0417, an invoice date, and a reference to PO-8841. The goods description reads: shea butter body cream, 500 millilitre bottles, product code SB-500. Not cosmetics, and certainly not general goods; a customs officer in either country can tell what is being sold. The quantity line shows 50 cartons of 24 bottles each, 1,200 bottles in total, at a unit price of 4.50 US dollars per bottle. The total invoice value is 5,400 US dollars, in the currency of transaction, United States dollars, which neither party uses at home but both agreed upon. The country of origin is Ghana. The delivery terms read CIP London, Incoterms 2020: under this rule the seller arranges and pays for carriage and insurance to London, while risk passes to the buyer when the goods are handed to the first carrier in Accra. The payment terms are open account, 30 days from invoice date.

Step 2: The packing list, field by field
The packing list records a package count of 50 cartons, 24 bottles per package. Weights per carton are 12 kilograms net and 13.5 kilograms gross, giving consignment totals of 600 and 675 kilograms. Each carton measures 60 by 40 by 40 centimetres. The shipping marks run KFT/LONDON/1 through KFT/LONDON/50, one mark per carton. No price appears anywhere on the document.

Step 3: Who reads which
The airline's cargo staff in Accra read the packing list: chargeable weight is the higher of gross and volumetric weight, so the dimensions affect the freight bill. The retailer's warehouse counts the 50 cartons against the marks on arrival. United Kingdom customs reads the invoice for the value behind the assessment and the description behind classification, with the packing list as the script for any examination. The retailer's accounts team pays against the invoice only when purchase order, goods receipt and invoice agree.

What does this example demonstrate?
Two documents, two stories, one set of facts. Every reader, planner, counter and officer finds the same story in both documents.

Key lesson
Prepare both documents from the same verified facts, and everyone downstream reads one consistent shipment.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Fifty cartons on paper, forty-eight in the container
BODY:
A hardware importer in Tema buys 50 cartons of door hinges from a manufacturer in Shenzhen, shipped in one loaded container by sea. The story of what went wrong, and when, is a lesson in itself. All figures are illustrative.

Step 1: The documents are prepared
The manufacturer issues a commercial invoice for 50 cartons and a packing list showing 50 cartons, marked SZ/TEMA/1 through SZ/TEMA/50. The shipping instructions sent to the forwarder, the single authoritative source taught in Lesson 5.3, also say 50 cartons.

Step 2: The handover at origin
The goods reach the carrier's container yard, where staff count what is actually handed over: 48 cartons. The carrier is honest and issues the bill of lading, its receipt for the goods, for 48 cartons. Nobody stops to ask why the receipt disagrees with the commercial documents. The discrepancy was born at this handover, and here it should have died: the shipper should have reconciled its dispatch, found that two cartons were left behind at its warehouse, and corrected all documents before the container sailed.

Step 3: Arrival at Tema
The importer's clearing agent lodges the import declaration in ICUMS, presenting the invoice for 50 cartons, the packing list for 50, and the transport document for 48. The officer sees three documents telling two different stories and queries the declaration. The container is held, and a physical check confirms 48 cartons on the ground.

Step 4: Tracing the cost
The agent writes to the supplier, who investigates and finds the truth: two cartons were short-loaded at its warehouse. The declaration must be amended to 48 cartons before release, and the importer pursues the missing cartons as a separate claim. Through all of this the container sits at the port while time-based charges accumulate. A two-carton error has become a customs delay, an amendment exercise, a claim, and a cost.

Step 5: When it should have been caught
At the origin handover, before the transport document was issued. A reconciliation of the shipper's count against the carrier's count takes minutes while the goods stand in front of both parties. Discovered at destination, it costs days, because the goods, the documents and the people are now on two sides of an ocean.

What does this example demonstrate?
Consistency failures do not announce their cause: fifty against forty-eight could be a typing error or a shortage, and nobody can know until it is traced, so the file is frozen until someone does.

Key lesson
The cheapest place to catch a discrepancy is where the goods change hands. Count at handover, reconcile then, and never let a document sail with a question mark inside it.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: General goods: a description that answers nothing
BODY:
An exporter in Shenzhen loads 800 cartons of mixed household products for a buyer in Hamburg, to travel by sea. To save time, a clerk describes the goods on the invoice and the packing list with two words: general goods. Everything else on the documents is in order. All figures are illustrative.

Step 1: The documents are issued
The invoice carries a correct invoice number, a stated currency, quantities and a total value. The packing list shows 800 cartons, weights, dimensions and marks numbered 1 to 800. The goods description on both documents reads: general goods.

Step 2: Customs asks its questions
When the declaration is lodged, the customs officer at the European port has a job to do and a description that helps with none of it. What are the goods? Classification under the Harmonized System cannot even begin. Is the declared value plausible? For what products, exactly? Could the consignment contain items needing permits or certification, electrical goods, toys, anything with safety rules? The description answers none of these questions, and each unanswered question is a reason to look closer.

Step 3: The cost of the silence
The consignment cannot be processed cleanly on the papers, so it is channelled to queries and physical examination. The container is moved to an inspection bay and opened, and officers count cartons against a packing list just as silent as the invoice. Days pass. Storage and handling costs accumulate, the buyer's shelves in Hamburg wait empty, and the exporter answers awkward emails. The two words saved at dispatch have bought all of this.

Step 4: The same shipment, described honestly
Now replay the story with the description the goods deserved: kitchen scales, digital, plastic housing, model KS-12, 800 grams each. An officer reading that line can classify the product, weigh the declared value against a known product category, check whether any control applies, and release the consignment. If an examination is chosen anyway, the packing list showing 24 scales per carton turns the search into a quick confirmation.

What does this example demonstrate?
The vague description did not merely slow one officer's morning. It blocked classification, made valuation checks impossible, raised suspicion where none was earned, and forced physical handling the shipment did not need.

Key lesson
General goods is not a description; it is an absence. Every question customs asks begins from what the goods are, so say what they are, specifically, on every document that describes them.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: One sale, one invoice
BODY:
There is a version of this lesson's documents that must never be made: a second commercial invoice for the same shipment. It usually takes the form of a reduced-value copy prepared for the customs authorities, showing a lower total so that less duty and tax are paid, while the true invoice records the real price for the buyer's payment.

Say it plainly: that is fraud. Deliberately declaring a false value on a customs document is misdeclaration, and manipulated trade documents are a recognised fraud and compliance exposure in international trade.

The consequences reach in every direction. Customs discovering two versions of one invoice can hold or seize the goods and impose penalties, and the exposure follows the trader for years. The buyer's records now reference a document that contradicts the one presented at the border, so the buyer's accounts, audit trail and three-way match are all corrupted. Where banks are paying against documents, the contradictory papers put the payment itself at risk. And the two versions always find each other eventually: a payment for one amount, a declaration for another, an audit comparing files.

Integrity is also simpler than the fraud. If a genuine error is discovered after an invoice is issued, the correction is made openly: the erroneous document is cancelled or credited on the record and a corrected one is issued, so that both parties, and where necessary the authorities, see one consistent truth.

The rule for professional life is short. One sale, one invoice, one set of true figures, wherever those figures are asked for.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Pro forma and commercial invoices: different documents at different moments
BODY:
Two documents share the word invoice, look almost identical, and are easily confused: the pro forma invoice and the commercial invoice.

A pro forma invoice is issued before the sale is final. It is a quotation in invoice form: it sets out the proposed goods, prices and terms so that the buyer can confirm the deal, arrange payment, or apply for permits or foreign exchange at home. It is not a demand for payment, and it is not a customs document. Lesson 4.2 showed where it fits: the pro forma helps the parties form the agreement that sits at the centre of international trade.

The commercial invoice is issued for the actual shipment. It demands payment for goods actually sold, records the transaction in both parties' books, and grounds the customs valuation at import. It is the document of record this lesson has taught throughout.

The confusion is understandable because the two documents can carry the same fields: the same parties, goods and prices. The difference lies in timing and function, not appearance. The pro forma proposes; the commercial invoice declares.

Two malpractices follow from blurring them. A buyer who treats a pro forma as final and pays against it has paid against a quotation that may still change. A seller who lets the pro forma drift into use as the shipping invoice leaves customs valuing the goods on a document that was never issued for the shipment at all.

The habit to keep: a pro forma may start the file, but the commercial invoice, issued for the actual consignment, is the only invoice that travels with the goods.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: The packing list is not optional paperwork
BODY:
Because the packing list carries no prices, a beginner sometimes concludes that it is a formality, an extra copy for the file that could be skipped when time is short. This is exactly backwards: the packing list is the counting sheet for everyone downstream, and skipping it moves the work to people who must do it blind.

Without a packing list, the receiving warehouse counts packages with no record to count against, and shortages surface as arguments instead of facts. The consignee signs for goods it could not verify, and inherits any discrepancy silently. The carrier plans loading without reliable weights and dimensions, which is how containers end up overweight and aircraft space is misbooked. The customs examiner who must inspect a consignment has no script, so more packages are opened than the shipment ever needed to have opened.

Notice also that the absence of prices is design, not omission. The packing list is a physical document for physical hands, and the people counting cartons in a warehouse, or planning stowage on a quayside, have no need of the transaction's commercial terms. Keeping prices off it keeps the commercial story where it belongs, on the invoice, and the physical story where it belongs, on the packing list.

So treat the packing list as what it is: the consignment's own identity document, the record that lets every counter, planner and examiner work from the same facts. Preparing it carefully is not paperwork. It is the work.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A new colleague in a forwarding office is preparing the documents for an export of 40 cartons of floor tiles. He suggests putting the unit prices on the packing list as well, so that the buyer's warehouse can check the bill while counting the cartons.

Which statement best describes the roles of the two documents?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The packing list should carry the prices, because every document in a shipment file must show the transaction value for the file to be complete.
2. The commercial invoice records the sale, its prices and its terms, while the packing list describes the physical consignment, its packages, weights and marks, and normally carries no prices.
3. The commercial invoice is read only by the buyer's accounts team, while the packing list is the document customs relies on to value the goods.
4. Either document may carry the prices, provided the totals agree, because the two documents are interchangeable records of the same sale.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The lesson's founding distinction is that every shipment tells two stories. The commercial invoice is the document of the sale: parties, goods description, quantities, unit prices, total invoice value, currency, origin and terms. The packing list is the document of the consignment: package count, contents per package, gross and net weights, dimensions and shipping marks. Its readers are counting, loading and inspecting, so prices would serve no purpose there, and standard practice leaves them off.

Answer 1 turns a design feature into a defect, claiming completeness requires prices everywhere; in truth the file is complete when each document carries what its readers need. Answer 3 gets both halves wrong: customs valuation is built on the commercial invoice, which banks as well as accounts teams read, not on the priceless packing list. Answer 4 dissolves the distinction the lesson exists to teach; the documents are complementary, not interchangeable, and each answers a different question.

The takeaway: prices and terms live on the invoice; packages, weights and marks live on the packing list. Knowing which document answers which question is the working skill of this lesson.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
A forwarder in Hamburg receives shipping instructions describing 300 cartons of goods simply as spare parts, and must prepare the export invoice and packing list from them.

Which option best explains why this description is a problem?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The description is too short to fit the standard space provided for goods descriptions on commercial invoice forms.
2. Spare parts is an acceptable description, provided the quantities, weights and total invoice value have all been calculated correctly.
3. Customs classification, valuation checks and cargo verification all hang on the goods description, so it must state what the goods actually are; a vague phrase like spare parts invites queries, delays and possible inspection.
4. The description matters only on the packing list, because customs reads weights and package counts rather than product names when processing a declaration.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

Customs decisions hang on the description. Classification under the Harmonized System begins from what the goods are, the declared value can only be sanity-checked against identifiable products, and any physical verification compares goods against a description that says what to expect. Spare parts for what? Vehicles, textile machines or computers? No officer can begin to answer, so the consignment attracts the queries, document checks and physical examinations that a specific description, such as brake pads for light commercial vehicles with part numbers, would never have earned.

Answer 2 accepts the vagueness as long as the arithmetic is right, but correct totals cannot rescue a description that blocks classification, as the general goods example in this lesson showed. Answer 4 shrugs the problem onto the packing list, yet customs reads the description on the invoice and the declaration together, and product names are precisely what drive classification. Answer 1 invents a formatting rule that has nothing to do with the real issue.

The takeaway: write every description so a stranger at the border can tell what is in the boxes; if it cannot survive that test, rewrite it before the documents are issued.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
While reviewing a packing list for 20 pallets of bottled water before dispatch, a logistics officer finds the net weight stated as 15,400 kilograms and the gross weight as 14,900 kilograms.

Which conclusion best describes correct professional practice?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The figures are acceptable, because packaging is deducted from the gross weight before the goods are shipped.
2. The figures reveal an error: gross weight includes the packaging, so it can never be less than net weight, and the packing list must be corrected before issue because carriers and customs rely on its weights.
3. The figures are acceptable, because net weight may legitimately exceed gross weight when goods such as bottled water are dense and their packaging is light.
4. The figures are a matter for customs at destination, so the officer should issue the documents unchanged and let the authorities raise any query.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Gross weight is the total weight of the goods including their packaging; net weight is the goods alone. Gross can therefore equal net only in the impossible case of weightless packaging, and it can never fall below it. Net 15,400 kilograms against gross 14,900 kilograms is a demonstrated error, and the review stage before dispatch is exactly where it must be caught and corrected, because carriers plan loads and verify declared weights against these figures, and customs compares documents with goods using them.

Answer 1 misstates the relationship: packaging is included in gross, not deducted from it. Answer 3 invents a density exception that does not exist; density changes how much volume a weight fills, never which of the two weights is larger. Answer 4 abandons the reviewer's duty taught in this lesson: issuing a document knowing it contains an impossible figure passes a preventable error downstream, where it will surface as queries, delays and a corrected filing at the worst possible moment.

The takeaway: plausibility checks are part of every document review, and a figure that cannot be physically true must be corrected before issue, never shipped.
