=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The Participants Behind Every Available Product
BODY:
Module 1 introduced logistics as the activity of planning, moving, and storing goods so that they become available where and when they are needed. Logistics, however, does not act on its own. It serves a network of participants: the businesses and organisations that produce goods, supply them, buy them, and finally use them. This network is often called the logistics ecosystem. In nature, an ecosystem is a community of living things that depend on one another. The logistics ecosystem works in a similar way: a shop cannot sell what no factory has produced, a factory cannot produce what no supplier has delivered, and a supplier cannot survive without customers who buy.

Consider an ordinary bottle of drinking water bought at a kiosk in Accra. Someone drew and treated the water. Someone manufactured the bottle and the cap. Someone filled, sealed, and packed the bottles. Someone transported them to a store and onward to the kiosk. Someone finally bought the bottle to drink. Behind that single purchase stands a chain of participants, each with a distinct role.

The main roles examined in this lesson are the producer, the supplier, the manufacturer, the buyer, the wholesaler, the retailer, and the consumer. Together these roles form what is called a supply chain: the complete set of participants and activities that carry a product from its raw materials to its final user. The lesson defines each role, explains the responsibilities that belong to it, and shows how the roles connect so that goods become available. By the end, you should be able to take any everyday product and trace it through these roles.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Producers, Where the Chain Begins
BODY:
Every supply chain begins with a producer. A producer is an individual or organisation involved in creating or producing goods or services. The word is deliberately broad. It covers anyone whose work brings a product into existence, whether or not that product is made in a factory.

Producers of goods include the following.

1. Farmers and fishers. A cocoa farmer, a tomato grower, and an artisanal fishing crew all produce goods. Their output is grown, caught, or harvested rather than made.

2. Miners and extractors. Businesses and individuals who extract minerals, stone, salt, or sand produce the raw materials on which many industries depend.

3. Manufacturers. Factories are producers too, because they produce goods by transforming inputs. Manufacturing is treated as a role of its own in the next section.

The word producer can also describe creators of services, such as an electricity company or a bank. In logistics training it is most often used for those who produce physical goods, because physical goods are what must be stored, moved, and delivered.

A producer's main responsibilities, as they concern logistics, are to produce in the quantities required, at an acceptable quality, and at times that fit the plans of those who will buy the output. A harvest must be ready for collection; a quarry must have stone ready when construction customers order. Producers rarely deliver their goods far by themselves, which is one reason the supplier role exists.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Manufacturers, Transforming Inputs into Finished Goods
BODY:
A manufacturer is a business or organisation that transforms raw materials or components into finished or intermediate products. Three ideas in this definition deserve attention.

First, transformation. Manufacturing means changing the form of things: roasting and grinding cocoa beans, sawing logs into boards, mixing flour and water into biscuits, assembling parts into a telephone. The manufacturer adds value by turning inputs into something more useful.

Second, raw materials and components. Raw materials are unprocessed inputs such as cocoa beans, timber, or grain. Components are parts that have already been made elsewhere, such as bottle caps, zips, buttons, or screens, which the manufacturer builds into its own product.

Third, finished and intermediate products. A finished product is ready for its final user with no further processing: a chocolate bar, a bottle of water, a school desk. An intermediate product is output that becomes the input of another manufacturer. Flour is an intermediate product: the mill produces it, and the bakery transforms it again. Steel sheets, cocoa butter, and woven fabric are intermediate products in the same way. Supply chains often contain several manufacturers in a row, each transforming the previous one's output.

The responsibilities of manufacturers include the following.

1. Production planning, deciding what to make, in what quantities, and when, so that goods are available when customers need them.

2. Quality control, checking that outputs are fit for their purpose before they leave the factory.

3. Packaging, protecting goods and presenting them in units suitable for transport and sale.

4. Holding finished-goods stock, storing output safely until it is dispatched.

5. Supplying customers, accepting orders, preparing goods, and delivering them. Every manufacturer that sells its output is therefore also acting as a supplier, a point examined later in this lesson.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Suppliers, Providing What Other Participants Need
BODY:
A supplier is an individual or organisation that provides goods, materials, or services to another party. The defining feature of the supplier is provision: the supplier stands on the providing side of a transaction, and the party that receives is its customer.

Suppliers provide many different things.

1. Raw materials. A quarry that sells gravel and sand to construction companies is a supplier of raw materials.

2. Components and packaging. A company that sells bottle caps to a bottling plant supplies components the plant needs for production.

3. Finished goods for resale. A wholesaler that sells cartons of soap to shops is the shops' supplier, even though it did not make the soap.

4. Services. A haulage company supplies transport; a cleaning company supplies janitorial services; a security firm supplies guarding services. Suppliers are not limited to physical goods.

The important insight is that supplier describes a role in a transaction rather than a type of business. Almost any participant becomes a supplier at the moment it provides goods or services to another party.

A dependable supplier's responsibilities include the following.

1. Delivering the correct goods, in the agreed quantities and quality.

2. Delivering on time, because late inputs can interrupt a buyer's production or empty a buyer's shelves.

3. Communicating clearly, confirming orders, warning of delays, and providing documents such as delivery notes and invoices.

4. Maintaining dependable capacity, keeping enough stock or production capability to meet repeated orders, not only the first one.

Buyers depend on suppliers for everything they sell or use, which is why the relationship between these two roles sits at the centre of the logistics ecosystem.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Tiers of Suppliers, Who Supplies the Supplier
BODY:
The word tier means level. Supply chains describe suppliers in levels, according to how close each one stands to the buyer it serves directly. For a manufacturer, the usual picture is as follows.

1. Tier 1 suppliers sell directly to the manufacturer. If a biscuit factory buys flour from a mill and printed wrappers from a packaging printer, the mill and the printer are the factory's tier 1 suppliers.

2. Tier 2 suppliers sell to tier 1 suppliers. The wheat merchants who supply the mill with grain, and the ink and film producers who supply the packaging printer, are tier 2 suppliers.

3. Tier 3 suppliers stand one level further back. The fertiliser producer who sells to the wheat farmers, or the chemical maker who supplies the ink producer, operates at tier 3.

The tier concept teaches two lessons. First, every participant in the chain is somebody's supplier, and most suppliers are also somebody else's customers. Second, problems travel down the tiers. If a tier 2 supplier fails to deliver, the tier 1 supplier may be unable to complete its own orders, and the manufacturer feels the shortage even though it never contracted with the tier 2 business. For this reason, businesses that depend heavily on critical inputs try to know who their suppliers' suppliers are, and how a failure at a lower tier could reach them.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Buyers, Purchasing for Use, Resale, or Operations
BODY:
A buyer is an individual or organisation that purchases goods or services for personal use, resale, or business operations. The definition names three distinct reasons for buying, and each creates a different kind of buyer.

1. Buying for personal use. A household buying foodstuff, clothing, or fuel purchases for its own use. Such a buyer is usually also the final user, a point the lesson returns to shortly.

2. Buying for resale. Wholesalers and retailers buy goods in order to sell them again. They purchase large shares of many products, yet they never intend to use the goods themselves.

3. Buying for business operations. A factory buys raw materials to produce with; an office buys paper and printer cartridges; an airline buys fuel and spare parts. These buyers consume their purchases inside their own operations.

In a business, buying is usually organised as a formal process called procurement. A simple buying cycle runs as follows.

1. The buying organisation recognises a need and raises a purchase requisition, an internal request for approval to buy.

2. Once approved, a purchase order is sent to the chosen supplier, stating the goods, quantities, agreed prices, and delivery requirements.

3. The supplier delivers, and the buyer checks the delivery against the purchase order. This check is called the goods receipt.

4. The supplier sends an invoice requesting payment.

5. Payment is released only when the purchase order, the goods receipt, and the invoice agree with one another. This check is known as the three-way match, and it protects the buyer from paying for goods it did not order or did not receive.

A buyer's responsibilities include defining needs clearly, choosing suitable suppliers, ordering accurately, checking deliveries, keeping records, and paying on time. Buying decisions also drive logistics: every order tells the ecosystem what must be produced, moved, and stored next.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Consumers, the Final Users
BODY:
A consumer is the individual or organisation that ultimately uses or consumes a product or service. A closely related term, the end user, means the person or organisation that ultimately uses a product. The two terms point to the same idea: the participant standing at the end of the chain, for whose sake the whole movement of goods takes place.

Consumers do not resell what they consume. The child who wears the school uniform and the family that drinks the bottled water are consumers. So is the mason who uses a bag of cement in a wall: the cement is used up in his work and never resold.

Consumers may therefore be households or organisations. When a bakery turns flour into bread, the bakery consumes the flour, because it is used up in production and never resold as flour. An office consumes its printer paper in the same way. Consumption means use, whether at home or inside a business.

Final consumers matter to the whole chain because their demand is the original source of every order above them. Shops order stock because shoppers buy. Factories produce because wholesalers and shops order. Suppliers deliver because factories order. Changes in what consumers want and buy eventually travel back up the chain, even though most consumers never see or think about the participants standing behind their purchases.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Wholesalers and Retailers, Selling Goods Onward
BODY:
Between the manufacturer and the final consumer stand two selling roles that make small purchases possible.

A wholesaler is a business that purchases goods in relatively large quantities and resells them, usually to retailers or other businesses. A retailer is a business that sells goods directly to final consumers.

Manufacturers produce in large units: pallets, cartons, sacks, and tonnes. Consumers buy in small units: one loaf, one bar, one bottle. Very few consumers need a pallet of soap at a time, and most manufacturers would find it impractical to sell single bars to thousands of individual buyers. Wholesalers and retailers bridge this gap.

The wholesaler buys full consignments from manufacturers or importers, stores them in warehouses, and sells convenient intermediate quantities, such as cartons and cases, to shops, hotels, schools, and other businesses. The retailer buys those cartons, holds the goods close to its customers in kiosks, market stalls, supermarkets, or pharmacies, and sells the final small units to whoever walks in.

Their contribution to availability is easy to state: wholesalers and retailers hold goods near the market and sell them in quantities that ordinary buyers can afford and carry. Without them, goods would sit in factories in bulk instead of standing ready on shelves in towns and villages.

Both roles are buyers and suppliers at once. The wholesaler is a buyer when it purchases from the factory and a supplier when it serves the shops. The retailer is a buyer when it stocks its shelves and the last seller in the chain when it serves the final consumer.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Business-to-Business and Business-to-Consumer Trade
BODY:
Transactions in a supply chain are often grouped by who is selling to whom.

Business-to-business trade, usually shortened to B2B, covers sales in which a business sells to another business. The farmer selling cocoa to a buying company, the miller selling flour to a bakery, the factory selling soap to a wholesaler, and the wholesaler selling to shops are all B2B transactions.

Business-to-consumer trade, shortened to B2C, covers sales in which a business sells directly to the final consumer. The shop selling a bar of soap to a shopper is a B2C transaction.

One observation follows immediately. In a supply chain of any length, every sale is business-to-business except the last one. The journey from cocoa farm to chocolate bar may pass through five or six sales, and only the final sale, to the person who eats the bar, is B2C.

The two kinds of trade commonly differ in ways that matter for logistics.

1. Order size. B2B orders are usually large, measured in pallets and cartons; B2C purchases are usually single units.

2. Number of customers. A business serves relatively few business customers; a retailer may serve hundreds of individual consumers in a day.

3. Planning and documentation. B2B orders are commonly placed in advance, negotiated, and supported by purchase orders and invoices; B2C sales are usually immediate, small, and documented by nothing more than a receipt.

4. Delivery pattern. B2B goods often move in full loads to a few business addresses; B2C goods are bought at shops or delivered in small parcels to many homes.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: One Organisation, Several Roles
BODY:
A role describes what an organisation does in a particular transaction, not what the organisation is. This distinction matters because one organisation commonly holds several roles at the same time.

Consider a bakery.

1. When it buys flour from a miller, the bakery is a buyer, and the miller is its supplier.

2. When it mixes and bakes, the bakery is a manufacturer transforming inputs into a finished product.

3. When it delivers loaves to a supermarket, the bakery is a supplier providing goods to another party.

4. When its flour is used up in production, the bakery is a consumer of flour.

The same four sentences could be written about a cocoa farmer, who produces beans, supplies them to a buying company, buys fertiliser and sprayers, and consumes fuel in his tractor. A supermarket is a buyer when it places orders with its suppliers, a retailer when it sells to shoppers, and a consumer when it uses electricity and carrier bags in its own operations.

The practical lesson is to judge roles transaction by transaction. Whenever you read about a shipment or a business relationship, ask two questions: what is being provided, and who stands on each side of this particular transaction? The answers, not the company's name or its size, determine which role each party is playing. A large company can be a supplier on one contract, a buyer on the next, and a consumer in its own canteen, all within a single day.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: How the Participants Keep Goods Available
BODY:
The previous module described logistics as the activity that makes goods available. This lesson can now state plainly who makes goods available: manufacturers and suppliers, working together, supported by the buyers and sellers around them.

Suppliers contribute availability at the input end. A factory without raw materials and components is silent; a shop without stock is empty. Every product a business sells or uses begins as somebody's delivery, so dependable suppliers are the foundation of everything that follows.

Manufacturers contribute availability at the transformation end. Consumers cannot drink cocoa beans or wear cotton fibre, and shops cannot sell half-made goods. Manufacturers convert inputs into the finished forms that buyers and consumers actually want, in quantities large enough to serve whole markets.

Together, the participants form a sequence of customer-supplier relationships. Every participant is the customer of the participant behind it and the supplier to the participant ahead of it. Producers feed suppliers; suppliers feed manufacturers; manufacturers feed wholesalers; wholesalers feed retailers; retailers serve consumers. When each link performs reliably, goods flow and shelves stay stocked.

When one link fails, the effects travel forward. A late raw-material delivery can stop a production line. A stopped line shortchanges the wholesaler's next order. A short order empties shop shelves, and consumers find the product unavailable, which is the exact outcome logistics exists to prevent. This is why businesses strengthen the chain in simple, practical ways: they buy critical items from more than one supplier, and they hold buffer stock to cover short interruptions, both ideas met in the previous module's treatment of inventory. Availability is not the achievement of any single participant. It is the joint result of many organisations performing dependably, one link after another.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: From Cocoa Beans to a Chocolate Bar
BODY:
A supply chain is easiest to understand by walking along it. The following example follows cocoa from a farm in Ghana to a chocolate bar bought by a shopper. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: Production. A cocoa farmer in a cocoa-growing region of Ghana tends his trees, harvests the pods, and ferments and dries the beans. At this point he holds 20 bags of dried cocoa beans. The farmer is the producer, the person whose work brought the goods into existence.

Step 2: The first sale. A local buying company that purchases cocoa from farmers in the district buys the farmer's 20 bags and collects beans from many other farms until it has a full lorry load. In this transaction the farmer is the supplier and the buying company is the buyer.

Step 3: Supply to the processor. The buying company delivers the beans to a cocoa processing factory located near Tema. The buying company is now the supplier, and the factory is the buyer.

Step 4: Manufacture. The factory cleans, roasts, and grinds the beans and presses them into cocoa butter and cocoa powder, which are intermediate products, and uses them to produce finished chocolate bars packed in cartons. In this stage the factory is the manufacturer, and when it sells its output it acts as a supplier.

Step 5: Sale to a distributor. The factory sells 500 cartons of chocolate bars to a distributor in Accra that supplies retail shops across the city. The factory is the supplier; the distributor is a buyer purchasing for resale.

Step 6: Breaking bulk. The distributor stores the cartons in its warehouse and sells them in smaller lots to kiosks, mini-markets, and supermarkets. In each sale the distributor is the supplier, and each shop is a buyer and a retailer.

Step 7: The final sale. A shopper buys one bar from a kiosk and eats it that evening. The kiosk is the last seller; the shopper is the final consumer, the only participant who uses the product.

What does this example demonstrate? The goods changed hands many times but were used up only once, at the very end. Every sale from the farm to the kiosk was a business-to-business transaction; only the last sale was business-to-consumer. Each participant had a defined role in each transaction, and several participants changed roles as the goods moved along. The availability of that chocolate bar in the kiosk was the joint achievement of producer, suppliers, manufacturer, distributor, and retailer.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A Smartphone's Component Chain Across Continents
BODY:
The second example leaves Ghana to show how long and international a modern supply chain can be. Consider an ordinary smartphone sold in an Accra phone shop.

Step 1: Raw materials. Mines in several countries extract the metals and minerals from which electronic parts are made. These mining businesses are producers and early-tier suppliers in the chain: their output reaches the telephone only after further processing by others.

Step 2: Component manufacture. Factories, many of them in East Asia, transform the refined materials into components: display screens, camera lenses, batteries, memory chips, and small parts. These component makers are usually tier 2 suppliers, because most of them sell to other suppliers rather than to the phone company itself.

Step 3: Module supply. Other specialist businesses assemble components into complete modules, such as a finished camera module or a combined screen assembly, and sell these directly to the phone manufacturer. They are the phone company's tier 1 suppliers.

Step 4: Final assembly. The phone brand assembles and tests its telephones at plants in manufacturing centres such as Shenzhen in China, a city known worldwide for electronics production. The brand is the manufacturer: it transforms hundreds of purchased components into a finished product. When the plant packs, say, 5,000 telephones into a container for an importer, the brand acts as supplier to its business customers.

Step 5: Distribution. The container travels by sea to West Africa, where an importing distributor clears the goods and sells them in bulk to retailers, including phone shops in Accra. Each of these sales is a business-to-business transaction between a supplier and a buyer.

Step 6: The final sale. A customer in Accra buys one telephone from a shop for personal use. The shop is the retailer; the customer is the end user, and this single sale is the business-to-consumer transaction the whole chain existed to serve.

What does this example demonstrate? One product may cross several countries, several manufacturers, and several tiers of suppliers before it reaches a shop, and most of its transactions were between businesses: supplier after supplier feeding the manufacturer. The example also shows why the tier concept matters. A fire at a tier 2 lens factory in Asia can delay a telephone in an Accra shop months later, because shortages travel down the tiers and interrupt availability at the end of the chain.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: One Product, Many Roles: Bottled Water in Kumasi
BODY:
The final example traces a single product through the roles and watches the roles change hands. It shows why roles must never be treated as fixed labels attached to particular companies.

Step 1: The packaging suppliers. A bottling company near Kumasi buys its empty bottles and caps from a packaging manufacturer, and the packaging manufacturer buys plastic resin from a petrochemical producer. The bottling company is a buyer; the packaging maker is a tier 1 supplier; the resin producer is a tier 2 supplier.

Step 2: Production. The company draws water from its boreholes, treats and filters it, and fills and caps 1,500 cartons of bottled water in a production run. In this activity the company is a manufacturer of a finished product, and also a producer of the water itself.

Step 3: Sale to the wholesaler. The bottling company sells 200 cartons to a wholesaler in Kumasi, which stores them in its warehouse. The bottling company is now a supplier, and the wholesaler is a buyer purchasing for resale.

Step 4: Sale to the shops. The wholesaler sells the cartons in lots of ten to kiosks and mini-markets across the city. The wholesaler is the supplier in these sales; the shops are buyers and retailers.

Step 5: Consumption. A teacher buys two bottles from a kiosk on her way home and shares them with her family. She and her family are the final consumers, the end users of the product.

Now follow one company through the week. The bottling company was a buyer when it purchased bottles, a manufacturer and producer when it filled them, and a supplier when it sold the cartons onward. One organisation, several roles, all in a single week, without the company ever changing what it is.

What does this example demonstrate? Roles are positions in transactions, and the same organisation moves between them constantly. The example also shows the value of the intermediary sellers: the wholesaler and the shops stood between a factory producing 1,500 cartons at a time and a family needing two bottles at a time, converting one large quantity into many small ones.

Lessons from the example: identify the transaction first, name the roles second, and remember that every business in the chain is simultaneously somebody's customer and somebody else's supplier.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: A Buyer Is Not Always the Consumer
BODY:
Beginners often assume that whoever pays for goods is also the person who uses them. The two roles coincide for the household shopper, which makes the assumption natural, but in most supply chains they are different participants.

Consider these situations.

1. A mother buys medicine for her child. The mother is the buyer; the child is the consumer.

2. A school buys 40 textbooks. The school is the buyer; the students who study the books are the consumers.

3. A shop buys 200 umbrellas. The shop is the buyer, purchasing for resale; the consumers are the people who later buy the umbrellas and use them. The shop itself never uses a single one.

4. A company buys laptops for its staff. The company is the buyer; the employees who type on the machines are the end users.

The distinction matters in logistics for practical reasons. Sales to business buyers usually involve large quantities, bulk packaging, deliveries to business addresses, and documents such as purchase orders and invoices. Sales to final consumers involve single units, small packaging, and thousands of separate purchase points. A business that confuses the two will package, price, transport, and document its goods for the wrong kind of customer.

There is also a service lesson. The buyer chooses the product, but the consumer experiences it. When the end user's needs are ignored because everyone in the chain paid attention only to the immediate buyer, the product may be ordered and delivered correctly yet still fail in the market.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Every Manufacturer Is a Supplier, but Not Every Supplier Is a Manufacturer
BODY:
Manufacturer and supplier are sometimes used as if they described the same set of businesses. They do not. The manufacturer is defined by what it does: it transforms raw materials or components into products. The supplier is defined by its position in a transaction: it provides goods, materials, or services to another party.

Two statements put the relationship in order.

First, every manufacturer that sells its output is a supplier to its customers. The flour mill supplying the bakery and the bottling plant supplying the wholesaler are, in each sale, occupying the supplier role.

Second, not every supplier is a manufacturer. A wholesaler supplies shops with goods it did not make. An importer supplies local retailers with goods produced abroad. A haulage company supplies transport, a service with no goods at all. A trader supplying sand and stones may simply buy and resell what others produced. Supply describes the relationship, not the making.

A simple test keeps the two roles straight. Ask what the business does with the goods it handles. If it transforms them into something new, it is manufacturing. If it passes them on, or provides a service, it is supplying. Then ask who stands on the other side of the transaction, because the same business will often manufacture at its factory gate and merely resell at its trade store.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Supply Chains Are Not All the Same Length
BODY:
The examples in this lesson showed chains of five, six, and seven links, and it is tempting to conclude that every product travels such a long road. It does not. Supply chains vary enormously in length, and a learner who memorises one fixed diagram has missed the point.

At the long end stands the imported smartphone: mines, refiners, component factories, module makers, assembly plants, ocean transport, an importing distributor, retailers, and finally the consumer. At the short end stands the mango farmer who harvests a basket of mangoes and sells them directly to households at a market stall. In that chain there is no manufacturer, no wholesaler, no retailer, and no import stage: the producer sells straight to the consumer. A baker who sells bread across the counter of her own bakery runs a chain of barely two steps.

What decides the length? Roughly four things: how much transformation the product needs, how far it must travel, whether it is produced locally or imported, and how the market for it is organised.

The important constant is that the roles remain the same even when they are combined or skipped. The mango farmer is producer, wholesaler, and retailer at once. When a chain has fewer participants, some participants are simply performing more roles, not performing different ones. Learn the roles well, and any chain, short or long, can be read at sight.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A bakery in Kumasi buys 50 bags of flour from a miller every week, bakes bread in its ovens, and delivers loaves to a supermarket, where shoppers buy them.

Which statement best describes the participants in these transactions?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The bakery is only a manufacturer, the miller is only a supplier, and the supermarket is the final consumer of the bread.
2. The bakery is a buyer when purchasing flour, a manufacturer when baking bread, and a supplier when delivering loaves to the supermarket.
3. The bakery is a supplier in every transaction because it is the bakery that ultimately provides bread to shoppers.
4. The miller is the buyer in the flour transaction because the bakery pays money to the miller.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Roles are judged transaction by transaction, and one organisation commonly holds several roles at the same time. In the flour purchase, the bakery receives goods for use in its operations, so the bakery is the buyer and the miller is the supplier. In the baking stage, the bakery transforms raw materials into a finished product, so it acts as a manufacturer. In the delivery to the supermarket, the bakery provides goods to another party, so it acts as a supplier.

Answer 1 fails because it treats roles as fixed labels attached to companies, and because the supermarket buys bread for resale, which makes it a retailer and a business buyer rather than the final consumer; the shoppers are the consumers. Answer 3 fails because in the flour transaction the bakery receives goods rather than providing them. Answer 4 reverses the roles: the buyer is the party that purchases goods, and the party that receives payment for providing them is the supplier.

The takeaway is that roles belong to transactions, not to organisations.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
A company that assembles smartphones buys its camera modules from a module maker. The module maker, in turn, buys its lenses from a glass company. The assembly company learns that a glass shortage will delay the camera modules, and therefore delay finished telephones.

Which statement best describes the suppliers in this situation?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The module maker is a tier 1 supplier to the assembly company, and the glass company is a tier 2 supplier.
2. Both the module maker and the glass company are tier 1 suppliers because both supply parts used in the telephone.
3. The glass company is a tier 1 supplier because raw materials and basic parts always stand at tier 1.
4. The glass company is not part of the supply chain because it never sells anything directly to the assembly company.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

Suppliers are arranged in tiers according to how close each one stands to the buyer it serves directly. Tier 1 suppliers sell directly to the manufacturer, so the module maker is the assembly company's tier 1 supplier. Tier 2 suppliers sell to tier 1 suppliers, so the glass company, which supplies the module maker, stands at tier 2.

Answer 2 fails because tiers describe position in the chain, not the importance of the part supplied. Answer 3 reverses the logic: raw materials and basic parts usually stand at the far tiers, because they reach the manufacturer only after other businesses have processed them. Answer 4 fails because a business that stands in the chain of supply is part of the supply chain even where it never contracts with the manufacturer directly.

The situation also shows why tiers matter for availability: a shortage at tier 2 stops the tier 1 supplier's production, and the shortage reaches the manufacturer and its customers even though the manufacturer never ordered from the tier 2 business.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A factory sells 1,000 cartons of soap to a wholesaler. The wholesaler splits the consignment into 10-carton lots and sells them to neighbourhood shops, and the shops sell single bars to households.

Which option correctly identifies the final consumer of the soap?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The wholesaler, because it purchases the largest quantity of soap in the chain.
2. The shops, because they hold the soap immediately before it is sold.
3. The household member who ultimately uses the soap.
4. The factory, because it created the soap and is therefore the first user of it.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The consumer is the individual or organisation that ultimately uses or consumes a product. The households that buy single bars and use them for washing are the final consumers, and their purchase from the shops is the only business-to-consumer transaction in the chain.

Answer 1 fails because buying in large quantities is the wholesaler's commercial function, not consumption; the wholesaler purchases for resale and does not use the soap. Answer 2 fails because position near the end of the chain does not make a business the final user; the shops also buy for resale. Answer 4 confuses producing with consuming: the factory transforms inputs into soap, which makes it the manufacturer, the very start of the chain rather than the end of it.

Every sale before the household purchase, from factory to wholesaler and wholesaler to shops, was a business-to-business transaction between a supplier and a buyer purchasing for resale. The takeaway is simple: follow the soap to whoever finally uses it, and you have found the consumer.

- Wholesalers purchase in relatively large quantities and resell to retailers and other businesses; retailers sell directly to final consumers; their shared function is bridging bulk production and small-unit consumption. — source: lesson brief key terms plus general business knowledge (bulk-breaking function).
- In a multi-step supply chain, every sale except the last is a business-to-business transaction; only the final sale to the end user is business-to-consumer. — source: general business knowledge.
- Cocoa farming is a major agricultural activity in Ghana, and cocoa moves from farms through buying companies to processing factories; the example chain in this lesson is illustrative and names no specific company or institution. — source: general business knowledge (chain described generically for teaching).
- Shenzhen in China is a major world centre of electronics manufacturing, and smartphone supply chains typically involve component and module suppliers in several countries feeding final assembly plants. — source: general business knowledge (qualitative statement only; no company, volume, or percentage claimed).
- One organisation can hold several participant roles at once; roles belong to transactions rather than to organisations. — source: general business knowledge (conceptual framing).
