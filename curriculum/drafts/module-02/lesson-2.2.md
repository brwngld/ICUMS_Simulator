=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The Parties and the Goods in Every Shipment
BODY:
Every shipment involves parties who send goods and parties who receive them. The previous lesson introduced manufacturers, suppliers, buyers, and consumers in the supply chain. This lesson begins by naming those same parties as they appear in transport, because the intermediaries studied here all work for them.

Freight is the general term for goods transported commercially by road, rail, sea, or air. While such goods are being handled at a terminal or carried in a container, they are also commonly called cargo.

The shipper is the party that tenders goods for transportation. In an export, the shipper is usually the seller or exporter, and in most shipping documents the shipper is identified as the consignor.

The consignee is the person or organisation designated to receive goods under a transport document. In an import, the consignee is usually the buyer or the receiver named at the destination.

Consider a company in Accra that ships 300 cartons of soap to a distributor in Cotonou. The Accra company is the shipper. The Cotonou distributor is the consignee. The cartons of soap are the freight.

Between the moment goods are tendered and the moment they are received, they normally pass through the hands of several specialist organisations. A logistics intermediary is a service provider that facilitates logistics transactions between cargo owners, transport operators, and other participants. The most important intermediaries for this lesson are freight forwarders, carriers, shipping lines, and agents. The rest of this lesson defines each role, explains what each one does, and shows how they work together.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: What a Freight Forwarder Is
BODY:
A freight forwarder is an individual or company that organises and coordinates the transportation of goods on behalf of shippers. The definition contains three ideas worth separating.

First, the forwarder acts on behalf of the shipper. The exporter or consignor engages the forwarder as its transport organiser, and the forwarder works in the shipper's interest.

Second, the forwarder organises and coordinates. It plans the journey, selects suitable transport services, and connects them into one complete movement. A useful comparison is the travel agent: a travel agent books flights with airlines for a traveller, but the travel agent does not fly the aircraft. In the same way, a freight forwarder arranges carriage with transport companies without usually operating the vessels, aircraft, or truck fleets itself. Forwarders of this kind are often described as non-asset-based, meaning they do not own the main transport equipment; they buy transport space from those who do.

Third, the forwarder's product is organisation. Its value lies in its knowledge of routes, schedules, documents, and requirements, and in its working relationships with carriers.

Freight forwarders handle domestic and international shipments. In an international movement, a forwarder often manages the shipment from the shipper's premises to the consignee's door, coordinating road transport, ocean or air carriage, and the formalities along the way. For example, a manufacturer in Kumasi exporting 500 cartons of processed food may appoint one forwarder to arrange collection, transport to a seaport, the ocean booking, and the export paperwork, rather than performing each of those steps separately.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The Responsibilities of a Freight Forwarder
BODY:
The responsibilities of a freight forwarder cover the whole organisation of a shipment. The main duties include the following.

1. Advising and planning. The forwarder advises the shipper on suitable routes, transport modes, transit times, packing requirements, and likely costs, and then plans the movement stage by stage.

2. Booking carriage. The forwarder reserves space with carriers on the shipper's behalf, for example booking a container slot with a shipping line, space on an aircraft, or a truck from a haulage company.

3. Arranging collection and delivery. The forwarder organises the transport of goods from the shipper's premises to the port or airport, and corresponding delivery arrangements at the destination.

4. Preparing documentation. International shipments require transport documents and supporting documents. The forwarder prepares or checks these documents so that names, quantities, and descriptions are accurate and consistent.

5. Consolidating cargo. The forwarder may combine several customers' smaller shipments into one full container or one vehicle load. This practice, called consolidation, allows each shipper to pay only for the space it uses.

6. Arranging cargo insurance. Where a shipper wants its goods insured in transit, the forwarder can arrange appropriate cover with insurers.

7. Tracking and communicating. The forwarder follows the shipment's progress, keeps the shipper informed, and passes instructions between the parties.

Not every shipment needs every service. A simple domestic move may require only booking and collection, while an international consignment may use all seven. The pattern to remember is that the forwarder is responsible for arranging the complete transport on the customer's behalf, whether it performs each activity itself or coordinates other providers to perform it.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: What a Carrier Is
BODY:
A carrier is a person or company responsible for transporting goods or passengers under a transport agreement. The carrier is the operator that actually performs the carriage: it owns or controls the vehicles, vessels, or aircraft, employs the drivers, crews, or pilots, and moves the goods physically from one point to another.

Carriers are usually identified by the transport mode they operate.

1. A trucking or haulage company is a road carrier. An example is a company whose articulated trucks carry containers between Tema Port and Kumasi.

2. An airline is an air carrier. Airlines carry cargo in the holds of passenger aircraft and in dedicated freighter aircraft.

3. A shipping line is a sea carrier. Because ocean transport is central to international trade, the next section examines shipping lines separately.

4. A rail operator is a rail carrier, moving wagons over a rail network.

A carrier normally receives the goods into its care and issues a transport document for them. A shipping line issues a bill of lading for sea carriage, an airline issues an air waybill, and a road carrier issues a consignment note. These documents record what the carrier received and on what terms it agreed to carry the goods; they are studied in detail in later modules.

The essential point is physical performance. When goods are on the carrier's truck, aircraft, or vessel, the carrier is responsible for carrying them safely to the agreed destination under the transport agreement.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Distinguishing the Freight Forwarder from the Carrier
BODY:
Beginners often use freight forwarder and carrier as if they meant the same thing. The distinction between them is one of the most useful in logistics.

The carrier moves the goods. It is the transport operator whose equipment performs the carriage, and it is responsible for its transport leg under the transport agreement.

The freight forwarder organises the movement. It plans the shipment, books space with carriers, prepares documentation, and advises the shipper. In most cases it owns no vessels, aircraft, or trucks; its contribution is expertise and coordination rather than transport equipment.

Both roles appear in the same shipment. An exporter in Takoradi may appoint a forwarder to arrange a consignment to a buyer in Europe. The forwarder books space with a shipping line, which carries the container across the sea. A trucking company, another carrier, delivers the container to the port. Three organisations, two functions: the shipping line and the trucking company are carriers performing carriage, while the forwarder is the intermediary arranging and coordinating both.

Responsibility follows the role. The carrier answers for performing its leg of the transport properly. The forwarder answers to its customer for organising the shipment correctly: choosing suitable carriers, meeting deadlines, and keeping documents accurate.

The distinction is a matter of function. Some large companies do both, operating trucks while also arranging shipments for customers. What decides the label in any particular transaction is the function the company is performing there, a point taken up again in an important note near the end of this lesson.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Shipping Lines and International Maritime Transport
BODY:
A shipping line is a company that operates or commercially manages vessels providing maritime cargo transportation services. In other words, a shipping line is a sea carrier: it puts vessels on the water, or manages vessels commercially, and carries freight for customers. Some lines own and crew their vessels; others charter vessels from owners and manage the commercial service. In both cases, the company whose service the cargo travels on is the line.

Most container traffic moves through liner services. A liner service runs published routes on published schedules between named ports, in the way a bus service follows a timetable rather than a taxi that goes wherever a single passenger directs. The liner publishes its port rotation and sailing frequency, and cargo is booked onto particular sailings.

The role of the shipping line in an international movement is normally the ocean leg, port to port. The typical sequence is as follows.

1. The shipper or its forwarder books space on a sailing, and the line confirms the booking.

2. The container is delivered to the port before the cut-off time for the sailing.

3. The line loads the container onto the vessel and issues a bill of lading for the goods received.

4. The vessel sails on the published route and discharges the container at the destination port.

A single large container vessel can carry thousands of containers, and vessel capacity is commonly counted in twenty-foot equivalent units, called TEU. Shipping lines generally do not deliver cargo inland to doors themselves; inland legs are performed by other carriers, coordinated by the shipper or its forwarder.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Shipping Agents
BODY:
A shipping agent is a person or company appointed to represent a shipping line or vessel operator at a port or within a designated area. The definition turns on representation: the agent acts in the name of the line, in a place where the line itself has no presence.

The need is practical. A vessel may sail under a line whose head office is on another continent, yet during a port call it needs documents signed, services arranged, and problems solved on the ground. The line therefore appoints an agent at each port it serves. Typical duties of a vessel's agent include the following.

1. Handling arrival and departure formalities, including notifying the authorities and arranging berthing arrangements with the terminal.

2. Arranging services and supplies for the vessel, such as fuel, water, food provisions, and minor repairs.

3. Handling documents on the line's behalf, including the cargo documents connected with the port call.

4. Attending to crew matters, such as crew changes and medical or travel arrangements.

5. Coordinating cargo operations with the terminal so that loading and discharge proceed on schedule.

Two related uses of the word agent are common in trade. A destination agent is a company at the port of discharge that handles arrival matters and assists in arranging release and delivery of cargo to consignees, often working as the local partner of the forwarder or the line at origin. A booking agent is a company that sells cargo space on behalf of shipping lines, in effect brokering space between the lines and shippers or forwarders.

In every form, the agent's defining feature is the same: it represents a principal, such as a line or a vessel, and acts for that principal locally.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Other Logistics Intermediaries
BODY:
Freight forwarders, carriers, shipping lines, and agents are the roles this lesson focuses on, but several other intermediaries appear regularly in cargo movement. A logistics intermediary, as introduced earlier, is any service provider that facilitates logistics transactions between cargo owners, transport operators, and other participants. The main additional examples are the following.

1. Customs brokers. In many countries, a customs broker is a licensed agent who transacts customs clearance business on behalf of importers and exporters, preparing and submitting declarations, classifying goods, and arranging payment of duties. Licensing requirements vary from country to country. Customs procedures themselves are treated in later modules; at this stage the point is the brokering role between the trader and the customs authority.

2. Third-party logistics providers. A third-party logistics provider, usually abbreviated 3PL, is an external company to which a business outsources logistics functions such as transport, warehousing, or freight forwarding. Many businesses that own no transport equipment at all still run complete distribution operations by combining 3PL services.

3. Warehouse operators and terminal operators. These companies receive, store, and handle cargo at facilities along the route. Their facilities and functions are the subject of the next lesson, Ports, Terminals, Warehouses, and Inland Depots.

4. Courier and parcel companies. For small, urgent, or document shipments, courier services organise collection, carriage, and delivery, performing a coordinating role similar to the forwarder's, but for small parcels.

What unites all intermediaries is that they facilitate movement without usually owning the goods. They exist because cargo owners need specialist services, and because connecting those services efficiently is itself a skilled business.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Why a Small Exporter Uses a Freight Forwarder
BODY:
Consider a small exporter who ships occasionally. Perhaps a processor in Tamale sends a container of shea butter abroad three or four times a year. Why should it appoint a freight forwarder rather than deal with each carrier itself?

Dealing directly would mean doing all of the following without help: identifying which shipping lines call at which ports and on what schedules; negotiating rates with each carrier; understanding the transport documents and getting them right; arranging a truck to meet the sailing deadline; and solving problems at a foreign port of destination. Each step is a specialist task, and an occasional shipper has no reason to maintain that specialist knowledge for a handful of shipments a year.

The forwarder exists precisely to supply that knowledge as a service.

1. Expertise. The forwarder handles such shipments every day and knows the routes, the schedules, the documents, and the requirements.

2. One point of contact. The exporter manages one relationship instead of many, and the forwarder coordinates everyone else.

3. Buying power and consolidation. Because the forwarder combines many customers' cargo, it can often obtain better rates than a single small shipper, and a small consignment can share container space with others instead of paying for an empty half-container.

4. Documentation support. Experienced preparation reduces the errors that delay cargo.

5. A network at destination. Forwarders work with partners and agents in foreign ports, so the shipment has a local handler at the far end.

The forwarder's fee is the price of these services. For an occasional shipper it is usually money well spent: the cost of a missed sailing, a rejected document, or a stranded container can quickly exceed it.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: How Logistics Service Providers Coordinate
BODY:
An international shipment is a relay. No single organisation normally carries the goods from the producer's door to the buyer's door. Instead, several specialised providers each perform one part, and the shipment advances by repeated handover. Coordination is what turns separate services into one journey.

A typical export sequence shows the pattern.

1. The shipper engages a freight forwarder and provides details of the goods, the destination, and the required timing.

2. The forwarder plans the movement and books the ocean carriage with a shipping line, which confirms the booking.

3. The forwarder arranges a trucking carrier to collect the goods or container and deliver it to the port in time for the vessel.

4. The line loads the container and carries it to the destination port, where its agent assists the vessel during the port call.

5. At the destination, the forwarder's partner or a destination agent handles arrival matters, and the consignee is notified to take delivery.

Each handover carries two requirements. The schedules must align: if the truck arrives at the port after the container cut-off, the container may miss its vessel and roll to a later sailing, delaying the whole consignment. The information must also pass: every party that receives the goods must already know what they are, where they are going, and what must happen next.

This is why coordination is a genuine skill. A forwarder's daily work consists largely of managing these handovers: matching trucks to sailings, documents to cargo, and arrival arrangements to the consignee's readiness to receive.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Communication and Documentation Between Stakeholders
BODY:
While goods move physically, information moves in parallel, and the physical movement depends on it. Every handover in the relay described earlier is accompanied by communication and, almost always, by a document.

The documents record the transactions that make up the shipment. The line's booking confirmation records the space reserved. The carrier's transport document, whether the bill of lading at sea, the air waybill in air carriage, or the consignment note on the road, records what the carrier received, from whom, and for delivery to whom. Supporting documents describe and certify the goods. Each document allows the next party in the chain to verify what it is receiving and to perform its own part correctly.

Communication keeps the parties aligned as the shipment progresses. The forwarder tells the shipper when the container was booked and when it sailed. The line tells its agent the vessel's expected arrival. The agent informs the terminal, and the consignee is notified when the cargo is ready for collection. A delay at one stage must be communicated promptly, because every later stage is planning around the original schedule.

Accuracy matters as much as speed. A misspelled consignee name on a transport document can hold cargo at the destination until the error is corrected. A wrong container number can associate documents with the wrong box. Missing a documentation deadline can leave booked cargo behind. Documents and instructions are therefore checked carefully before they are issued, and changes are confirmed in writing.

In short, the movement of goods is only as reliable as the movement of the information about the goods.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A Ghanaian Exporter Ships Shea Butter to Rotterdam
BODY:
A company in Tamale produces shea butter for export. A cosmetics buyer in Rotterdam, in the Netherlands, orders 600 cartons. This is the company's first international shipment, and it appoints a freight forwarder to organise the movement. The following steps show who does what.

Step 1: The shipment is agreed. The exporter, as shipper, and the buyer, as consignee, agree the sale. The exporter engages a freight forwarder and provides details of the goods, the destination, and the required delivery period.

Step 2: The forwarder plans and books. The forwarder advises on packing and timing, plans the route from Tamale to Tema Port and onward by sea to Rotterdam, and books space for one container with a shipping line. The line confirms the booking.

Step 3: A trucking carrier delivers the container. The forwarder arranges for a container to be trucked to Tamale, where the 600 cartons are loaded into it and the container is sealed. A trucking company, acting as road carrier, then delivers the sealed container to Tema Port in time for the sailing cut-off.

Step 4: Documents are prepared. The forwarder prepares the export paperwork together with the exporter. The shipping line receives the container at the port and issues a bill of lading recording the goods received for carriage to Rotterdam.

Step 5: The ocean leg. The line loads the container onto its vessel, which sails from Tema to Rotterdam on its published route. The line's shipping agent at Rotterdam assists the vessel during its port call.

Step 6: Arrival and delivery. At Rotterdam, the forwarder's destination partner handles arrival matters and notifies the consignee. The buyer completes the local requirements and takes delivery of the 600 cartons.

What does this example demonstrate? At least six participants performed distinct functions: shipper, freight forwarder, trucking carrier, shipping line, port agents, and consignee. The exporter dealt with one company, the forwarder, yet specialists moved the goods at every stage.

Key lesson: the freight forwarder organises the specialists, the carriers move the goods, the agents represent the lines locally, and each handover is recorded in documents.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Consolidation in Hamburg: Four Shippers, One Container
BODY:
A freight forwarder in Hamburg, Germany, serves small exporters. In one week, four customers each ask to ship goods to buyers in Dubai: a spice merchant with 8 pallets, a stationery maker with 6 pallets, a tool supplier with 9 pallets, and a candle maker with 5 pallets. None of the four loads is large enough to justify a full container on its own. The forwarder consolidates them.

Step 1: Separate bookings. Each shipper books its own consignment with the forwarder and receives a price based on the space its goods occupy, rather than the cost of a whole container.

Step 2: Collection to the forwarder's warehouse. The forwarder collects all four loads and receives them at its warehouse, checking each consignment against its documents.

Step 3: Building one container. Warehouse staff load the 28 pallets together into a single container, planning the loading so that each consignment can be separated again at the far end. The forwarder prepares documentation that identifies each shipper's goods within the shared container.

Step 4: One ocean booking. The forwarder books the container with a shipping line as one shipment. The cost of the container is shared among the four shippers in proportion to the space each uses.

Step 5: Separation at destination. The forwarder's partner at Dubai receives the container, separates the four consignments, and arranges release of each one to its consignee.

What does this example demonstrate? Consolidation turns four uneconomic part-loads into one efficient full container. Each small shipper obtains ocean transport at a cost it could never achieve alone, and the buyers in Dubai each receive their consignment as though it had travelled separately.

Lessons from the example: consolidation is one of the freight forwarder's core services; the forwarder's organisation, not ownership of ships, is what creates the saving; and the shipping line carries one container while four separate trades travel inside it.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A Vessel Call at Tema: The Shipping Agent at Work
BODY:
A container vessel operated by an international shipping line is scheduled to call at Tema Port as part of its route. The line's commercial head office is on another continent, thousands of kilometres away, yet the port call requires many local arrangements. The line therefore works through its appointed shipping agent in Ghana.

Step 1: Appointment and pre-arrival information. Well before arrival, the line sends its agent the vessel's schedule and the cargo details for the call. The agent is the line's representative at the port for this visit.

Step 2: Arrival arrangements. The agent handles the required notifications to port authorities and arranges berthing arrangements with the terminal so that the vessel can berth and begin work without delay.

Step 3: Services and supplies. While the vessel is alongside, the agent arranges what the ship needs: food provisions, fresh water, fuel services, and any repairs or crew requirements, including crew changes.

Step 4: Cargo coordination. The agent coordinates with the terminal so that discharge of import containers and loading of export containers proceed according to plan. For cargo consigned to Ghanaian consignees, the agent handles release documents on the line's behalf and directs consignees, or the forwarders representing them, on collecting their cargo.

Step 5: Departure and reporting. When operations are complete, the agent completes the departure formalities, settles local charges on the line's behalf, and reports the call to the line. The vessel sails for its next port.

What does this example demonstrate? A shipping line can operate a global service without maintaining an office in every port, because a local agent represents it. The agent acts for the line and the vessel: its duties are vessel services, formalities, documents, and coordination, not the ownership of cargo.

Key lesson: the agent is the line's local hands, voice, and signature. Shippers and consignees usually encounter the agent's work through their forwarders rather than directly.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: The Forwarder Organises; It Does Not Usually Own the Vehicles
BODY:
A common beginner misconception is that a freight forwarder must own ships or trucks, because it moves goods. In most cases it does not. The typical forwarder is non-asset-based: it buys transport space from carriers and organises its customers' shipments around that space. Its warehouse may be rented, its trucks subcontracted, and its ocean capacity purchased from shipping lines.

Two refinements keep this accurate. First, some large companies do own vehicles or warehouses as well as arranging shipments for customers, so the boundary in practice is not absolute. Second, in certain arrangements a forwarder may take on carrier responsibilities itself, contracting as the carrier for the whole journey and then engaging the actual operators behind the scenes. What defines the role in any given shipment is the function performed: organising, or carrying.

Do not confuse ownership with responsibility. Even without owning a single vehicle, a forwarder is accountable to its customer for the arrangement: selecting suitable carriers, meeting deadlines, and keeping documents accurate. When a shipment goes wrong, the shipper's first call is the forwarder it appointed, and the forwarder then pursues the matter with the carrier responsible.

Remember: the forwarder organises the movement, the carrier performs it, and either may be answerable to you, depending on what you engaged it to do.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: One Company Can Play Several Roles
BODY:
The four roles in this lesson, freight forwarder, carrier, shipping line, and agent, are functions, not fixed identities. Real companies often combine several.

A shipping line is itself a carrier; the name shipping line simply identifies a carrier that operates by sea. A large logistics group may act as a freight forwarder, a customs broker, a trucking operator, and a warehouse operator at the same time, sometimes within one group of companies. The shipping agent at a port is frequently a separate local company whose entire business is representing visiting lines.

Because titles alone cannot be trusted, judge a company by the function it performs in your transaction. Ask four questions. Is this company physically carrying my goods on its own equipment? Then it is acting as a carrier. Is it arranging and coordinating the movement on my behalf? Then it is acting as a forwarder. Is it representing a line or vessel at the port? Then it is acting as a shipping agent. Is it preparing customs declarations for me? Then it is acting as a broker.

The practical value of identifying roles is knowing whom to contact for what: booking questions to the forwarder, the ocean leg to the carrying line, arrival arrangements to the destination agent, and customs declarations to the broker. In a well-run shipment, every participant's role is clear.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Information Errors Become Physical Problems
BODY:
Intermediaries coordinate goods partly by coordinating information, which means an information error soon becomes a physical problem. Several examples follow directly from this lesson's material.

A consignee name misspelled on a transport document can delay release of the cargo at destination until the document is corrected, and corrections cost time and money. A container booked for a sailing but delivered to the port after the cut-off rolls to a later vessel, and every arrangement at the destination that was planned around the original arrival slips with it. A truck dispatched with an incorrect delivery address carries correctly packed goods to the wrong place. Documents that disagree with one another invite questions from every party that must check them.

Three habits reduce these risks.

1. Verify before issuing. Names, quantities, weights, container and seal numbers, and delivery details should be checked against the underlying instructions before documents are issued or instructions confirmed.

2. Confirm changes in writing. A change agreed by telephone should be recorded in a written instruction, so that every party works from the same version.

3. Communicate delays immediately. A party that learns of a delay early can adjust; a party that conceals it multiplies the cost.

Every stakeholder in the chain depends on the accuracy of the previous stakeholder's information. The shipment is only as strong as its documents.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A businesswoman in Kumasi exports 400 cartons of groundnut paste to a buyer in London. She has never exported before and asks a freight forwarder to handle the shipment instead of contacting shipping lines, airlines, and trucking companies herself.

Which option best explains what the freight forwarder will do for her?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Operate the vessel that carries the goods to London and employ its crew.
2. Organise the shipment on her behalf by booking carriage with carriers, arranging collection, preparing documentation, and advising her throughout.
3. Purchase the groundnut paste from her and resell it to the London buyer at a profit.
4. Store the cartons in its own warehouse until the buyer travels to Ghana to collect them.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

A freight forwarder's function is to organise and coordinate transportation on behalf of the shipper. Booking carriage with carriers, arranging collection of the goods, preparing and checking documentation, advising on routes and requirements, and keeping the customer informed are the core responsibilities introduced in this lesson.

Answer 1 fails because operating a vessel is the function of a shipping line, which is a carrier; the forwarder usually owns no transport equipment at all. Answer 3 confuses the forwarder with a trader: the forwarder does not buy the exporter's goods, it works on her behalf for a service fee, and the goods remain her consignment throughout. Answer 4 describes simple storage, which is a warehouse function and not the reason she engaged a forwarder.

The central point of the lesson is that the exporter supplies the goods and the instruction, while the forwarder supplies the organisation, so that she deals with one company instead of negotiating separately with a shipping line, an airline, and a trucking company.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
At Tema Port, a container of frozen fish is delivered to the terminal by a trucking company and then loaded onto a vessel operated by a shipping line. A separate company, which owns no trucks and no vessels, organised the ocean booking and prepared the shipping documents for the exporter.

Which statement best describes the roles of these companies?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The trucking company and the shipping line are freight forwarders because they handle the goods physically.
2. The company that organised the booking is the carrier because it manages the whole shipment.
3. The trucking company and the shipping line are carriers because they physically transport the goods, while the company that organised the booking and documents is a freight forwarder.
4. All three companies are shipping agents because each provides a service for payment.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The lesson's core distinction is function: a carrier physically transports goods under a transport agreement, while a freight forwarder organises the movement on the shipper's behalf. The trucking company performs road carriage with its own vehicle, and the shipping line performs the ocean carriage with its vessel; both are therefore carriers, in different modes. The company that booked the space and prepared the documents owns no ship and no truck; it performed the organising function, which is exactly what defines a freight forwarder.

Answer 1 fails because physical handling is the carrier's characteristic, not the forwarder's; handling goods does not make a company a forwarder. Answer 2 fails because organising the booking is the forwarder's function, and managing a booking does not make a company the carrier. Answer 4 fails because being paid for a service does not make every provider a shipping agent; the agent role means representing a shipping line or vessel at a port.

The takeaway: ask whether the company carried the goods or organised the carriage, and the correct label follows.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A shipping line whose head office is overseas sends a vessel to Tema Port. While the vessel is in port, a local company arranges its berthing arrangements and supplies, attends to its crew requirements, and handles documents on the line's behalf.

Which statement best describes the role of this local company?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. It is a shipping agent appointed to represent the shipping line and its vessel at the port.
2. It is the consignee of the cargo carried on the vessel.
3. It is a freight forwarder taking ownership of all cargo on board the vessel.
4. It is a trucking company hired to discharge the containers from the vessel.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

A shipping agent is a person or company appointed to represent a shipping line or vessel operator at a port or within a designated area. Arranging berthing, supplies, and crew requirements, handling documents, and coordinating the port call are the classic duties of a vessel's agent. The line appoints the agent precisely because its head office is far away and the vessel needs a local representative on the ground.

Answer 2 fails because the consignee is the party designated to receive goods under a transport document, not the line's local representative at the port. Answer 3 fails on two counts: a freight forwarder organises shipments for shippers, and none of the roles in this lesson involves taking ownership of cargo. Answer 4 fails because discharging containers is cargo-handling work performed at the terminal, not the representative function described in the question.

The takeaway is representation: the defining feature of the shipping agent is that it acts in the line's name at the port, locally and on the line's behalf.
