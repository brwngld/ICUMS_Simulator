=== ASSESSMENT Q1 | tests lesson 2.1 ===
QUESTION:
Three businesses operate in one town. A mill buys wheat and grinds it into flour, which it sells to bakeries. A wholesaler buys cartons of soap from a factory and resells them to neighbourhood shops. A haulage company owns trucks and carries goods for other businesses for a fee.

Which statement best describes the supplier roles in these businesses?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Only the mill is a supplier, because only the mill sells a physical product that it has itself processed.
2. The mill and the wholesaler are suppliers, but the haulage company is not, because a supplier must provide goods rather than services.
3. None of the three is a supplier, because none of them manufactures everything that it sells.
4. All three act as suppliers, because each provides goods or services that another party needs, and supplying does not require manufacturing.
CORRECT: 4
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 4.

A supplier is defined by its position in a transaction: it provides goods, materials, or services to another party. Supplying therefore does not require manufacturing. The mill supplies flour to the bakeries, and because it also transforms wheat into flour it is a manufacturer that acts as a supplier whenever it sells its output. The wholesaler supplies the shops with soap it did not make, which the lesson treats as a fully valid supplying role. The haulage company supplies transport, a service with no goods at all, and the lesson names haulage companies among suppliers of services.

Answer 1 fails because supplying is not reserved for businesses that process what they sell; the wholesaler and the haulage company supply without transforming anything. Answer 2 fails because suppliers are not limited to physical goods: services such as transport, cleaning, and security are supplied exactly as goods are. Answer 3 fails because supply describes a relationship, not the making of the product; a business does not need to manufacture everything it provides.

The takeaway is the lesson's simple test: if a business transforms inputs, it is manufacturing; if it passes goods on or provides a service, it is supplying.

=== ASSESSMENT Q2 | tests lesson 2.1 ===
QUESTION:
A clinic in Tamale buys medicines from a pharmaceutical distributor and administers them to its patients. The distributor sources the medicines from a pharmaceutical factory.

Which option correctly identifies the buyer and the final consumer of the medicines in the transaction between the distributor and the clinic?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The distributor is the buyer, and the clinic is the final consumer of the medicines.
2. The clinic is the buyer, and the patients who ultimately receive the medicines are the final consumers.
3. The distributor is both the buyer and the final consumer, because the medicines pass through its warehouse.
4. The clinic is the buyer and also the final consumer, because the medicines are held on its shelves.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Roles are judged transaction by transaction. In the transaction between the distributor and the clinic, the clinic purchases the medicines for its business operations, so the clinic is the buyer and the distributor is its supplier. A consumer is the participant that ultimately uses or consumes the product, and the medicines are finally used on the patients, so the patients are the final consumers, just as the child who receives medicine bought by a parent is the consumer in the lesson's example.

Answer 1 names the wrong transaction: in its purchase from the factory, the distributor is indeed a buyer, but in the sale to the clinic it stands on the providing side, and the clinic is not the final user of the medicines in any case. Answer 3 fails because passage through a warehouse is not consumption; the distributor buys for resale and never uses the medicines it stocks. Answer 4 fails for the same reason: holding medicines on shelves is not using them, exactly as the shop in the lesson that stocks umbrellas never uses a single one.

The takeaway: the buyer chooses and pays, the consumer finally uses, and in most supply chains the two are different participants.

=== ASSESSMENT Q3 | tests lesson 2.1 ===
QUESTION:
A biscuit factory stops production because its flour mill failed to deliver. The mill explains that its own wheat merchant missed a supply. Within days, some shops in the city report that the biscuits are out of stock, although the shops never traded with either the mill or the wheat merchant.

Which statement best explains this situation?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The participants form a chain of customer-supplier relationships, so a failure at a lower tier travels forward: it stops production, shortchanges later orders, and finally empties shop shelves.
2. Availability depends only on the contracts a business signs directly; a failure at a business two steps away cannot affect it.
3. The shops are the consumers of the wheat, so the shortage is a consumer problem rather than a supplier problem.
4. Nothing could have been done differently, because the availability of goods is never within any participant's control.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

The participants form a sequence of customer-supplier relationships: the wheat merchant supplies the mill, the mill supplies the factory, the factory supplies wholesalers and shops, and the shops serve consumers. Suppliers stand in tiers according to how close each one stands to the buyer it serves directly, so the mill is the factory's tier 1 supplier and the wheat merchant is a tier 2 supplier. Problems travel through these links. A failure at tier 2 leaves the tier 1 supplier unable to complete its orders, the manufacturer's line stops, later orders are short or late, and the shortage finally appears as empty shelves far from the business that failed.

Answer 2 is the exact misconception this situation disproves: the shortage reached the shops even though they never contracted with the mill or the merchant. Answer 3 confuses roles: the shops buy biscuits for resale and are not consumers of wheat at all. Answer 4 fails because the lesson names simple, practical safeguards against exactly this risk: buying critical items from more than one supplier and holding buffer stock to cover short interruptions.

The takeaway: availability is the joint achievement of many links, and the weak link may stand two tiers away from the business that feels the pain.

=== ASSESSMENT Q4 | tests lesson 2.2 ===
QUESTION:
A furniture manufacturer in Istanbul sells 250 chairs to a wholesaler in Kumasi. The manufacturer's freight forwarder arranges the ocean booking and delivers the sealed container to the port, and a shipping line carries the container to Tema, where the wholesaler takes delivery.

Which option correctly identifies the shipper and the consignee of this shipment?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The forwarder is the shipper, because it tenders the container to the shipping line, and the shipping line is the consignee, because it receives the container at the port.
2. The manufacturer is the shipper, and the Kumasi wholesaler is the consignee.
3. The shipping line is the shipper, because it carries the goods, and the manufacturer is the consignee, because it packed and sold them.
4. The wholesaler is the shipper, because it paid for the goods, and the manufacturer is the consignee, because it received the payment.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The shipper is the party that tenders goods for transportation, and in an export the shipper is usually the seller, identified as the consignor in the shipping documents. The manufacturer sells the chairs and tenders them for carriage, so it is the shipper. The consignee is the person or organisation designated to receive the goods under the transport document, and in an import that is usually the buyer, so the Kumasi wholesaler is the consignee.

Answer 1 fails because delivering the container to the port is work the forwarder performs on the shipper's behalf; the role of tendering the goods belongs to the exporter. The shipping line receives the container into its care for carriage, which is the carrier's function, not the consignee's; the consignee is designated to receive the goods at the destination. Answer 3 reverses the transport roles: carrying the goods is precisely what makes the line the carrier. Answer 4 confuses payment with the transport roles: paying for the goods makes the wholesaler the buyer and, in transport terms, the consignee, while selling and packing belong to the shipper's side.

The takeaway: the shipper sends and the consignee receives, and the forwarder, carrier and agent in between act for one of them rather than replacing either.

=== ASSESSMENT Q5 | tests lesson 2.2 ===
QUESTION:
A forwarder books space for a container of furniture on a Wednesday sailing and arranges a truck to deliver it to the port. The truck arrives at the terminal the morning after the container cut-off has passed, so the container is left behind. The consignee's arrangements at the destination, planned around the original arrival, now fall due at the wrong time.

Which statement best explains what has happened?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The shipping line must hold the vessel until the container arrives, because a confirmed booking guarantees carriage on the booked sailing.
2. The trucking carrier should have carried the container across the sea itself, because it had physical possession of the goods.
3. The consignee caused the delay, because in any shipment it is the consignee who must deliver the container to the port before the cut-off.
4. The container missed its sailing cut-off and will roll to a later vessel, because schedules must align at every handover and the late truck broke the connection between the road leg and the ocean leg.
CORRECT: 4
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 4.

An international shipment is a relay of handovers, and each handover carries two requirements: the schedules must align and the information must pass. Liner services run published routes on published schedules, and cargo is accepted only up to the container cut-off for each sailing. A container delivered after the cut-off is left behind and rolls to a later vessel, and every arrangement at the destination that was planned around the original arrival slips with it. The truck's late arrival broke the connection between the road leg and the ocean leg.

Answer 1 fails because a booking confirmation reserves space on a sailing; it does not entitle one container to delay a vessel that must keep its published schedule. Answer 2 fails because carriers are identified by the mode they operate: the trucking company performs road carriage, and physical possession of the goods for one leg does not qualify it to perform another. Answer 3 fails because the consignee receives the goods at the destination; the duty to deliver the container to the port before the cut-off sat with the origin side that arranged the truck.

The takeaway: cut-offs are the points where separate providers' schedules must meet, and one missed connection delays the whole relay.

=== ASSESSMENT Q6 | tests lesson 2.2 ===
QUESTION:
An exporter in Tamale has only 8 pallets of shea butter, not enough to fill a container, and does not want to pay for a full container of ocean freight to reach its buyer abroad.

Which option best describes how this need is normally served?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The exporter books a full container from a shipping line and pays for the unused space, because a small shipment cannot be combined with anyone else's cargo.
2. A shipping agent at the port combines the pallets with other cargo, because agents act for shippers in consolidating their consignments.
3. A freight forwarder can consolidate the pallets with other customers' cargo into one container, so the exporter pays only for the space its goods occupy.
4. The exporter keeps the pallets in a warehouse until enough of its own cargo has accumulated to fill a container.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

Consolidation is one of the freight forwarder's core responsibilities: combining several customers' smaller shipments into one full container, so that each shipper pays only for the space it uses. The lesson's worked example showed four small exporters' 28 pallets travelling from Hamburg to Dubai in a single container, with the cost shared in proportion to the space each consignment occupied. Because a forwarder combines many customers' cargo, it can often obtain better rates than a single small shipper could, which is one of the main reasons an occasional exporter appoints a forwarder at all.

Answer 1 fails because paying for a whole container to carry eight pallets is exactly the waste the exporter wants to avoid, and the premise that small shipments cannot be combined is false: combining them is the forwarder's service. Answer 2 fails through role confusion: a shipping agent represents a shipping line or vessel at the port and does not consolidate cargo for shippers. Answer 4 fails because waiting until enough of its own cargo accumulates leaves the buyer unserved, when the goods could travel now in a shared container.

The takeaway: the forwarder's organisation, not ownership of ships, is what gives small shippers access to container economics.

=== ASSESSMENT Q7 | tests lesson 2.3 ===
QUESTION:
At a seaport, loose pallets from several shippers are received at a facility inside the port, where they are checked, sorted, and consolidated into full containers. Some distance away, in an inland city, there is a facility with its own container stacking yard and a customs post on site, where importers clear and collect full containers.

Which option correctly identifies the two facilities?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Both facilities are inland container depots, because both handle containers and cargo.
2. The facility inside the port is an inland container depot, and the inland facility is a container freight station.
3. The facility inside the port is a container freight station, where LCL cargo is consolidated and deconsolidated, and the inland facility is an inland container depot.
4. The facility inside the port is a warehouse, and the inland facility is a distribution centre.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

A container freight station is defined by its LCL work: less-than-container-load cargo from several shippers is received, checked, sorted, and consolidated into containers at origin, and the work runs in reverse as deconsolidation at destination. CFSs stand at or near container terminals, which matches the facility inside the port. An inland container depot is defined by its location and its container-yard role: it is a piece of container terminal moved inland, with stacking areas, container-handling machines and, where authorised, a customs post, so that importers clear and collect containers close to their premises. That matches the inland facility.

Answer 1 fails because handling containers is not enough to name a facility; the lesson's reliable test asks where it is, what it handles, and what it is authorised to do. Answer 2 swaps the two names: a facility inside the port cannot be an inland depot, because the ICD is inland by definition, and the inland yard's work with full containers is not LCL consolidation. Answer 4 fails because the port facility's work is consolidation rather than general storage, and a distribution centre exists to receive, process, and distribute goods to customers, not to clear containers.

The takeaway: name facilities by function and location. The CFS consolidates loose cargo at the port; the ICD handles containers inland.

=== ASSESSMENT Q8 | tests lesson 2.3 ===
QUESTION:
An importer whose containers have been discharged at a container terminal decides to leave them standing in the terminal yard for several weeks, reasoning that the goods are stored there anyway, exactly as they would be in a warehouse, and that the space is already part of the port.

Which statement best identifies the flaw in this reasoning?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. There is no flaw, because a terminal yard and a warehouse are two names for the same kind of storage.
2. Storage in a terminal is a pause in transit while cargo waits for the next step or for release, whereas warehouse storage is a chosen part of the supply plan, so treating the terminal as a warehouse disrupts cargo flow and, once the free allowance ends, time-based charges begin to run.
3. The plan is lawful only if the containers first pass through a bonded warehouse, because imported containers may never wait in a terminal yard.
4. The flaw is that terminal yards store export cargo only, so imported containers must be moved to a warehouse immediately after discharge.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Storage in a terminal is a pause in transit. The container in the yard is waiting for the vessel, the truck, or the customs release, under the terminal's and customs' control, and it waits only as long as the operation allows. Storage in a warehouse is a chosen part of the supply plan: the importer decides to hold stock in order to serve demand. Treating the terminal as a warehouse therefore disrupts the system. Congestion at a port is often the visible result of cargo waiting longer than the system was designed to wait, and once the free allowance ends, time-based charges begin to run.

Answer 1 is the exact misconception the lesson dismantles: the two kinds of storage differ in purpose, and the difference has consequences. Answer 3 fails because imported containers routinely wait in the terminal yard until release, as the lesson's own import example shows; a bonded warehouse is one facility type that holds goods whose formalities are incomplete, not a compulsory first stop. Answer 4 fails because terminal yards hold import and export containers alike, discharged from vessels at one end and loaded onto them at the other.

The takeaway: the terminal's business is movement and the warehouse's business is keeping; plan storage where storage is the purpose.

=== ASSESSMENT Q9 | tests lesson 2.3 ===
QUESTION:
A distribution business handles two kinds of stock. Its fast-moving drinks must flow from the receiving docks straight onto outbound vehicles for the shops, almost every hour. Its imported tinned goods, by contrast, must be kept safely for several months before gradual distribution.

Which option best matches each need to the right arrangement?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Cross-docking at a distribution centre for the drinks, and storage in a warehouse for the tinned goods.
2. Cross-docking at a container terminal for the drinks, and storage in a terminal yard for the tinned goods.
3. Storage in a warehouse for the drinks, and cross-docking at a distribution centre for the tinned goods.
4. Keeping both lines at an inland container depot, because depots were designed for the long-term storage of trade goods.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

A distribution centre is designed to receive, process, and distribute goods quickly, and its fastest arrangement is cross-docking, where goods move from the receiving dock straight to the waiting outbound vehicles with little or no storage in between. Hourly replenishment of shops is exactly that tempo. The warehouse, by contrast, exists to bridge the time gap: it holds goods safely and in organised conditions for as long as the supply plan requires, which is what months of tinned goods need.

Answer 2 fails because a container terminal and its yard are built for transfer, where storage is only a brief, incidental pause in transit; neither an hourly flow nor months of stock belongs there. Answer 3 reverses the two arrangements: putting hourly cross-docked goods into storage removes their speed, and pushing months of stock through cross-docking leaves it nowhere to wait. Answer 4 fails because an inland depot handles containers and, where authorised, customs services, not shop replenishment, and its storage of containers is temporary while they wait for release or collection.

The takeaway: match the facility to the tempo of the goods, movement or keeping, and choose the arrangement whose purpose fits the need.

=== ASSESSMENT Q10 | tests lesson 2.4 ===
QUESTION:
One imported consignment involves five parties: the customs authority; the port authority; a clearing agent who prepares the declaration for the importer; the terminal operator whose cranes discharge the container; and an independent inspection company engaged by the buyer to check the quality of the goods before shipment.

Which statement best classifies these parties?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. All five are regulatory authorities, because each one checks, handles, or controls goods moving internationally.
2. The clearing agent and the terminal operator are authorities, because they work inside the port area, while the customs authority, the port authority and the inspection company are service providers.
3. The customs authority and the port authority exercise public power, while the clearing agent, the terminal operator and the inspection company are commercial service providers.
4. The inspection company is an authority, because the examination of goods is always a government function.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

A regulatory authority exercises public legal power: it is created and funded by the state, it applies the law, and it cannot be hired, replaced or shopped around. The customs authority controls the goods for the state, and the port authority governs the port as infrastructure and regulates the activities inside it. A logistics service provider is a commercial business: it is hired by contract, it competes for customers, and a dissatisfied customer can take the next shipment elsewhere. The clearing agent acts for the importer, not for customs; the terminal operator sells handling commercially under rules set by the port authority; and the inspection company was engaged by the buyer and reports to the buyer.

Answer 1 fails because checking, handling or touching controlled goods does not give a company public power. Answer 2 fails twice over: working inside the port area does not make the agent or the operator public bodies, which is the lesson's misconception of thinking a terminal belongs to the government because it operates inside a port, and it wrongly demotes the port authority, which is usually a public body, to a service provider. Answer 4 fails because inspection is not always a government function: buyers sometimes engage independent private inspection companies, and the function is the same authorised examination against requirements; what differs is who performs it.

The takeaway test: does the organisation enforce the rules, or does it sell a service?

=== ASSESSMENT Q11 | tests lesson 2.4 ===
QUESTION:
A container has been discharged at a container terminal. The terminal operator will not deliver it to the importer's truck, and the shipping agent says it cannot help beyond the line's documents. The importer's clearing agent has lodged the declaration and is awaiting the outcome.

Which statement best explains who may decide when the container can leave the terminal?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Only the customs authority can decide release, because imported goods remain under customs control until released; the terminal handles the container and the agent handles the line's documents, but neither holds the legal mandate over the goods.
2. The terminal operator may release the goods once its storage charges are paid, because the container stands on the operator's own yard.
3. The port authority may release the goods, because it governs everything that happens inside the port area.
4. The clearing agent may release the goods once the declaration is lodged, because lodging the declaration is the release decision.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

The customs authority decides whether, when and under what conditions goods may cross the border, and imported goods remain under customs control until they are released. Release is a legal decision made after the declaration has been assessed, including the risk-based selection that channels a consignment towards release without inspection, a document check, or physical examination. The terminal operator handles the container and the shipping agent handles the line's documents, but neither holds a mandate over the goods, and the clearing agent, who lodged the declaration, acts for the importer rather than for customs.

Answer 2 fails because custody of the yard gives the operator charge of the container, not legal power over the goods; the lesson names the belief that any service provider can release goods as a common and costly confusion. Answer 3 fails on the port authority and customs distinction: customs controls the goods for the state, while the port authority manages the place through which the goods pass. Answer 4 fails because lodging a declaration starts the assessment rather than concluding it; the broker's duty is to declare accurately on the client's behalf.

The takeaway: for every control question, ask which organisation holds the legal mandate. For imported goods, that organisation is customs.

=== ASSESSMENT Q12 | tests lesson 2.4 ===
QUESTION:
A truck carrying a fully declared consignment of general goods is stopped at a checkpoint and immobilised, although its cargo documents are in order and customs has no objection to the goods. The reason given is that the vehicle's load exceeds legal weight limits and the driver's licence category does not match the vehicle.

Which statement best explains this situation?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The stop must concern the goods, because regulatory control in logistics means control of cargo only.
2. The stop is unlawful, because a correctly declared consignment protects the vehicle carrying it from any inspection.
3. Transport regulation applies to passenger services only, so a freight vehicle cannot be stopped for licensing or load reasons.
4. Compliance covers the cargo and the carrier: customs controls the goods, while transport regulators oversee vehicles, drivers and operators, so a properly declared consignment can still be immobilised in an unfit or overloaded vehicle.
CORRECT: 4
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 4.

The lesson's practical point is that the legality of the goods and the legality of the movement are two different questions. Customs controls the goods: declarations, permits, and permissibility. Transport regulators control the means of movement: they register and license vehicles and operators, test and license drivers, and check roadworthiness and load safety, because overloaded trucks damage roads and cause serious accidents. A consignment can therefore be perfectly declared and still be immobilised because the truck carrying it is overloaded or unlicensed, which is precisely what happened at the checkpoint.

Answer 1 fails because regulatory control in logistics covers more than cargo; the movement itself is regulated in its own right. Answer 2 describes a protection that does not exist: correct customs documents say nothing about the condition of the vehicle or the qualification of its driver. Answer 3 is false because vehicle and operator licensing, driver standards, and load safety apply to freight transport exactly as the lesson describes.

The takeaway: regulatory compliance is a condition of the whole movement, so a professional checks both sides before loading, the cargo's documents and the carrier's fitness.
