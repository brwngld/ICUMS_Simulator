# DRAFT — Lesson 7.1: Ocean Freight Operations and Vessel Schedules

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Ocean freight and its place in international trade
BODY:
Course 3 moves the programme from foundations to operations, and Module 7 opens with the movement that carries most of the goods traded between continents: the sea. Lesson 3.2, Modes of Transport: Sea, Air, Road, Rail, and Multimodal, introduced sea transport as one mode among several. This lesson goes inside the mode and shows how ocean freight runs as a system: the services, the vessels, the schedules, and the cycle one container follows from the shipper's desk to the quay and across the ocean.

Ocean freight is the transportation of commercial goods by sea or other navigable waterways. The definition is wider than deep-sea voyages alone: coastal and river movements count as ocean freight too, wherever commercial goods travel by water.

Its importance to international trade rests on simple economics. A ship moves enormous quantities of cargo in one sailing, at a cost per unit of cargo that no other mode can match over long distances. For the heavy, bulky, and high-volume goods that flow between continents, grain, crude oil, clinker, vehicles, machinery, and the countless containers of finished goods, the sea is the only realistic carrier. For a trading nation such as Ghana, whose two seaports at Tema and Takoradi are managed by the Ghana Ports and Harbours Authority, ocean freight is not one option among several; it is the physical basis of import and export trade.

That importance gives the industry its shape. Liner services sail on published schedules, terminals plan their work around vessel calls, forwarders plan shipments around sailing dates, and customers plan their businesses around arrival dates. Every one of those plans is the subject of this lesson.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The participants in ocean freight operations
BODY:
No single organisation moves a container by sea. Ocean freight is performed by several participants whose roles interlock, and naming them is the first step towards understanding the coordination this lesson closes with. Lesson 2.2, Freight Forwarders, Carriers, Shipping Lines, and Agents, introduced the ecosystem; here the participants are seen through their ocean roles.

1. The shipping line. A shipping line is a company that operates or commercially manages maritime cargo transportation services. The line owns or charters vessels, publishes the schedules, sells the space, confirms bookings, issues bills of lading, and answers for the vessel's performance.

2. The shipper and the consignee. The shipper is the party whose goods travel, and the consignee is the party entitled to receive them at destination. They decide what moves, when, and on which service, and they supply the information and documents the rest of the network depends on.

3. The freight forwarder. Lesson 5.1, Role and Responsibilities of Freight Forwarders, defined the forwarder as the customer's agent in arranging transport. In ocean freight the forwarder usually books space with the line, arranges collection and delivery, prepares the shipping instructions, and manages the deadlines, the operational position this lesson's shipping cycle assumes.

4. Port and terminal operators. Lesson 2.3, Ports, Terminals, Warehouses, and Inland Depots, introduced ports and terminals. Terminal operators receive containers, stack them in the yard, and load and discharge vessels with cranes under plans agreed with the line; Module 9, Port and Terminal Operations, takes portside operations in depth.

Around these core parties work port authorities, customs administrations, and the ships' agents who represent the vessel in each port. The pattern to hold on to is dependence: each participant's plan is built from information the others supply.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Liner shipping and tramp shipping: two ways to hire the sea
BODY:
Ocean freight services divide into two families, and the division decides how nearly every shipment is planned.

1. Liner shipping. Liner shipping is a maritime transportation service operating on established routes with published or planned schedules. A liner service sails the same route again and again, calls at the same ports in the same order, and departs on dates published weeks in advance; a weekly sailing is the common rhythm on the major container lanes. The line publishes its routes and its tariffs, and any shipper whose cargo fits the service can book space in it, one container at a time. Container shipping is the classic liner business, and the scheduled services this lesson follows are liner services.

2. Tramp shipping. Tramp shipping is maritime transportation in which vessels are employed according to cargo availability and commercial arrangements rather than fixed scheduled routes. A tramp vessel has no timetable and no published rotation; it is hired whole, normally under a contract of employment called a charter party, and it sails where the hirer's cargo requires, when that cargo is ready. The hirer does not buy a few containers of space; it takes the entire ship, and with it the commercial terms of the voyage.

3. Who uses which. The cargo decides. Manufacturers, retailers, and trading companies with containerised general cargo buy space on liner services, because their quantities are modest and their planning depends on regular sailings. Owners of bulk commodities, grain houses shipping harvests, mining houses moving ores, cement producers importing clinker, and energy companies moving crude oil and products, hire tramp vessels, because their cargoes fill whole ships and their routes follow the commodity from where it is found to where it is needed.

The two families fit in one sentence: liners sell space by the schedule; tramps sell the whole ship by the voyage.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Vessel types and the cargo each carries
BODY:
Within the merchant fleet, five vessel types do most of the cargo work, and each exists because of a particular kind of cargo. This is the link Lesson 3.3, Cargo Types, Packaging, and Handling, makes visible from the cargo's side: the cargo decides the ship.

1. Container ships. These carry containerised cargo in standardised boxes stacked on deck and in the holds. They are the workhorses of liner shipping and the vessels this lesson's shipping cycle follows. Manufactured consumer goods, machinery, processed foods, and raw materials packed into containers all travel this way.

2. Dry bulk carriers. These carry unpackaged dry commodities poured directly into the hold: grain, ores, clinker, fertiliser, and similar cargoes of the kind Lesson 3.3 classes as bulk cargo. A bulk carrier's voyage is normally a tramp arrangement, one commodity, one hirer, one voyage.

3. Tankers. Tankers carry liquid bulk in the vessel's own tanks: crude oil, refined products, chemicals, and liquefied natural gas. Like the dry bulk trades, the tanker trades are overwhelmingly tramp and charter-based.

4. Roll-on/roll-off vessels, known as RoRo. RoRo vessels carry cargo that drives or rolls aboard under its own power or on wheels, cars, trucks, buses, and heavy plant, secured on vehicle decks; ramps replace cranes.

5. General or breakbulk vessels. The conventional multipurpose ships carry unitised or project cargo as separate pieces, pallets, crates, steel, and outsize project equipment too awkward for a container or a hold. These are the vessels of the breakbulk trade Lesson 3.3 introduced.

The mapping matters operationally. A forwarder arranging a movement chooses not only a route but a kind of service: containerised cargo goes to liner container services; a full cargo of rice goes to the tramp market. The rest of this lesson stays with the container trade, where most logistics careers begin.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The language of liner schedules: routes, rotations, and dates
BODY:
A liner service is described by its route, its rotation, and its schedule, and the schedule speaks in four abbreviations.

A shipping route is a maritime transportation path connecting ports or trading regions, the trade lane at its largest scale, for example the route linking East Asia with West Africa.

A port rotation is the planned sequence of ports visited by a vessel during a voyage or service. Rotations are read as a list of ports in calling order, and the service repeats its rotation voyage after voyage. An illustrative rotation for a Far East service calling in West Africa reads: Shanghai, Singapore, Tema, Lome, Singapore, Shanghai. The string tells a shipper which ports the vessel calls, and in which order. The port of loading, POL, is the port where cargo is loaded onto a vessel; the port of discharge, POD, is the port where cargo is unloaded from a vessel. An exporter in Tema shipping to a buyer in Shanghai finds both ports in the rotation, in that order, and knows the service can carry the cargo.

A vessel schedule is a planned timetable showing a vessel's expected port calls, departures, and arrivals. Where the rotation says where a service goes, the schedule says when. These are what shippers plan against: a forwarder finds the sailing whose departure fits the cargo's readiness date, then plans the operation backwards from that sailing's deadlines.

The estimated time of departure, ETD, is the anticipated date and time when a vessel is expected to depart; the estimated time of arrival, ETA, is the anticipated date and time when a vessel is expected to arrive. Estimated carries the meaning: plans, not promises. The actual time of departure, ATD, is the recorded date and time when a vessel actually departs, and the actual time of arrival, ATA, is the recorded date and time when a vessel actually arrives, the vocabulary of tracking that Lesson 5.4, Shipment Tracking and Customer Communication, taught.

Interpretation is the skill. An ATD four hours late is noise; an ATD two days late moves every downstream date and puts each connection, a transhipment, an inland collection, a buyer's warehouse slot, at risk until a revised ETA is published, and nothing is settled until an ATA is recorded. Disciplined operations compare actuals against estimates at every milestone and never quote an ETA as an appointment.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The shipping cycle: booking, confirmation, and equipment
BODY:
The clearest way to teach liner operations is to follow one container. The cycle begins at the shipper's desk with the commercial process Lesson 5.2, Freight Quotations and Shipment Booking, taught in full: enquiry, quotation, acceptance, and then the booking.

The forwarder books space with the shipping line for a named sailing, and the line returns a booking confirmation stating, above all, the vessel and voyage, the estimated departure, the routing, and the cut-off deadlines for that sailing. Historically, a paper called a shipping order instructed the vessel to receive the booked cargo; today these workflows are almost entirely electronic, and the booking confirmation is the operative document. From the moment of confirmation, the shipment has a plan with dates attached, and deadlines.

The equipment then enters the cycle on one of two paths.

1. Full container load. For a container of its own, the line releases an empty container to the shipper or forwarder. The box travels to the shipper's premises, is stuffed with the cargo, and is sealed. The sealed box is now a single handling unit with a weight that must be established, the next block's subject.

2. Consolidated cargo. For cargo too small to justify a whole container, the goods are delivered loose to a container freight station, where the forwarder consolidates them with other customers' cargo into one shared container, as Lesson 5.3, Shipping Instructions and Cargo Consolidation, explained.

Either way, the clock is now running. The container, stuffed or to be built, must reach the terminal before a deadline the booking confirmation stated: the cargo cut-off.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The shipping cycle: verified gross mass and the cargo cut-off
BODY:
Two obligations converge before a container may enter the terminal gate: a weight and a deadline.

The weight is a safety requirement under the International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea, SOLAS. Verified gross mass, VGM, is the verified total weight of a packed container, and since 1 July 2016 the shipper must obtain and communicate it for every packed container before loading. The rules recognise two methods: Method 1 weighs the packed container on a scale, such as a weighbridge; Method 2 adds the container's tare weight to the weights of its contents and packaging, obtained with calibrated equipment and documented figures. The rule is absolute: no container without a verified gross mass may be loaded aboard a vessel. The duty sits with the shipper, not with the line.

The deadline is the cargo cut-off: the date and time by which the packed container, with its VGM declared, must be delivered to the terminal, to the container yard, or to the container freight station for consolidated cargo. At the cut-off, the terminal's gate closes to that sailing's cargo.

Why does the gate close at all? Because loading a vessel is an engineered operation planned days in advance, and three bodies of data must be final before the plan can be built.

1. Stowage planning. The line and terminal decide where every container sits aboard, the stowage plan, and a plan built from unverified weights would endanger the vessel's stability.

2. Customs and manifest data. The export declaration and the vessel's manifest data for the cargo must be complete before departure.

3. VGM verification. The line and terminal must have each box's declared weight confirmed before the box can be worked.

The cut-off exists so those plans can be made from facts. A container that arrives after it is, operationally, a container the plan never accounted for.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The shipping cycle: documentation cut-off, loading, and departure
BODY:
With the container inside the gate, a second clock is still running: the paperwork's.

1. The documentation cut-off. This is the deadline for finalising the shipping instruction, the consignee, cargo description, weights, marks, and container details from which the carrier prepares the bill of lading, as Lesson 5.3 taught. The instruction is the carrier's raw material for the transport document; after the cut-off, alterations are amendments to submitted details, and for the vessel's papers the details must stand as submitted. Commonly the documentation cut-off falls after the cargo cut-off, but the booking confirmation governs each sailing's actual dates.

2. Loading. Once the cargo cut-off has closed the gate, the terminal works the boxes. It arranges the sailing's containers in the yard in an order that serves the stowage plan, moves them to the quay, and the ship's cranes lift each box into its planned position aboard. The verified weights the shipper declared are load-bearing facts in the most literal sense: they determine where a box may sit and how it is secured.

3. Departure. The vessel sails, and the actual time of departure is recorded against the estimated one. The export declaration and the manifest data stand lodged; aboard, the carrier holds cargo for which it has issued, or is about to issue, its bill of lading, the document the final stage of the cycle turns to.

Then comes the part no shipper can manage: the voyage, lasting days or weeks according to the route, ending at the port of discharge.

A missed cut-off at the front of this cycle rolls the container to the next sailing, about a week on a weekly service, because the plans behind a sailing cannot absorb a late box; Lesson 5.2's rollover example showed the cost, and this lesson has now shown the reason.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The shipping cycle: arrival, discharge, and the bill of lading
BODY:
At the far end of the voyage, the cycle reverses itself.

The line announces the vessel's approach through its local agents, and the actual time of arrival is recorded when the vessel reaches the port of discharge. The vessel berths and discharge begins: the cranes lift each container off, the terminal stacks them in the yard, and the cargo waits for its documents and its release.

The documents follow the pattern taught across Module 6. The bill of lading is issued by the carrier once the goods have been received and loaded aboard; Lesson 6.2, Bill of Lading and Air Waybill, taught its three functions as receipt for the goods, evidence of the contract of carriage, and document of title. The last of those gives the paper its mechanics: an original bill of lading controls the right to demand delivery, so a container is released only against an original bill, or against the carrier's authority under one of the paperless alternatives. Where the sale is paid through a bank, the originals travel through banking channels to the buyer. Where the parties trade on trust, the shipper may surrender the originals at origin for a telex release, and the carrier authorises electronic release at destination; or the parties may use a sea waybill, which names the consignee and requires no paper at all. Where a forwarder consolidated the cargo, the shipper holds the forwarder's house bill while the line's master bill covers the consolidated container.

On the importer's side, the goods are declared to customs at the port of discharge, in Ghana through ICUMS by a licensed clearing agent, as Lesson 6.1 described, and released against the transport document. The container is collected, returned empty to the line's depot, and the cycle that began at a desk closes at a gate.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Transshipment, feeder vessels, and hub ports
BODY:
Not every container sails from its first port to its last on one vessel, and the networks that connect small ports to big ones have their own vocabulary.

Transshipment is the transfer of cargo from one vessel or transport service to another during its journey to the final destination. Lesson 2.3, Ports, Terminals, Warehouses, and Inland Depots, introduced the term alongside the facilities that make it possible; the concern here is why the networks are built this way and what transhipment costs a shipment.

Liner networks are built like wheels with rims and spokes. The largest vessels, the mainline services, sail the heavy trade lanes and call at a few great hub ports where enormous volumes can be exchanged. Smaller ports, and ports whose volumes cannot justify a mainline call, are connected to the hubs by feeder vessels: vessels used to transport cargo between smaller ports and larger hub ports or terminals. A container from a smaller port travels to its hub by feeder, joins the mainline vessel there, and reverses the process at the far end of the trade. The economics are compelling, one mainline sailing gathering the cargo of dozens of ports through a handful of hubs, and much of the world's cargo moves this way.

The cost is paid in time and in risk. A transshipped container's journey has two or more legs, and between the legs stands a connection: the box must reach the hub in time for the departure of its onward vessel. Each transfer adds handling, and each connection adds a point where a delay on one leg becomes a missed departure on the next, and at a hub the next departure may be days away. Planners therefore treat a transshipped routing's transit time as its direct-sailing equivalent plus a margin, and watch hub connections closely whenever schedules slip, a caution the worked examples return to.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Coordinating the network, and why schedules still slip
BODY:
The cycle taught in this lesson shows three organisations holding one plan between them.

1. The shipping line owns the schedule. It publishes the rotations and dates, allocates vessel space, plans the stowage from the weights and instructions received, issues the booking confirmations and bills of lading, and announces every change when the plan moves.

2. The port and terminal operator owns the ground. It plans berths and yard space around the published schedule, receives containers against the cargo cut-off, and loads and discharges the vessel to the stowage plan.

3. The freight forwarder owns the customer's plan. It books against the schedule, arranges collection and gate-in before the cargo cut-off, submits the shipping instruction before the documentation cut-off, tracks the milestones Lesson 5.4 gave the vocabulary for, and keeps the customer informed.

Each party's plan is built from the others' information, which is why the deadlines exist and why accurate data, weights, instructions, and timings, is the currency of the relationship.

Yet the plan still slips, and the common causes are worth naming. Severe weather delays sailings and can close ports. Congestion at a busy port, too many vessels and too few berths or too little yard space, slows the work. Mechanical problems take vessels out of service for repair. A missed transhipment connection holds cargo at a hub until the next departure. And the network is chained: a vessel leaving one port late compresses the time available at every following port on its rotation, so one port's congestion becomes several ports' delay until the schedule recovers.

The professional response is not to promise perfection but to manage movement: monitor the actuals against the estimates, re-plan when the plan moves, and tell the customer before the customer asks. That is the operating posture of a working forwarder, and the closing habit of this lesson.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: One container from Tema to Shanghai: the cycle in dates
BODY:
A processor in Tema has sold 1,180 cartons of processed cocoa products to a distributor in Shanghai, packed into one 40-foot container. The forwarder is instructed to ship on a Far East liner service. All dates, times, and figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: Booking and confirmation. On Friday the 6th the forwarder books space and receives a booking confirmation for the vessel on voyage 118W: routing Tema to Shanghai, estimated departure from Tema on Friday the 13th at 20:00, estimated arrival at Shanghai about twenty-eight days later, cargo cut-off Wednesday the 11th at 16:00, documentation cut-off Thursday the 12th at 12:00. The confirmation states the service rotation: Shanghai, Singapore, Tema, Lome, Singapore, Shanghai. The port of loading is therefore Tema and the port of discharge Shanghai.

Step 2: Equipment and stuffing. On Monday the 9th an empty 40-foot container is released to the exporter, trucked to the factory, stuffed with the 1,180 cartons on pallets, and sealed.

Step 3: Verified gross mass. On Tuesday the 10th the laden container crosses a weighbridge, Method 1, weighing the packed container. The verified gross mass is declared to the line that afternoon: the shipper's duty, and a condition of loading.

Step 4: Gate-in. On Wednesday the 11th at 11:00, five hours before the cargo cut-off, the container enters the terminal's container yard, its VGM already declared.

Step 5: Documentation. The shipping instruction, consignee, description, weights, and marks, was submitted complete on Tuesday, two days before the documentation cut-off. The carrier prepares the bill of lading from it.

Step 6: Loading, departure, voyage. The vessel works the terminal on Thursday night, and the line issues the bill of lading once the goods are loaded on board. Because the buyer is a long-standing account customer, the exporter surrenders the original bills at origin under a telex release, and the carrier authorises electronic release at destination. The estimated departure of Friday the 13th at 20:00 becomes an actual departure on Saturday the 14th at 02:20, a little over six hours late and unremarkable. The voyage takes about twenty-eight days.

Step 7: Arrival and discharge. The published ETA slips by one day; the actual time of arrival is recorded on the Monday, and the container is discharged and stacked in the yard at Shanghai. The consignee, cleared for electronic release, takes delivery as the named consignee without presenting paper.

What does this example demonstrate?

The sailing was a chain of deadlines and estimates, and it succeeded because every deadline was beaten by days, so a six-hour departure slip cost nothing.

Key lesson: book early, work backwards from the cut-offs, and read the actuals against the estimates, never instead of them.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Forty minutes late: a missed cargo cut-off and the customer conversation
BODY:
A furniture manufacturer near Tema exports a 40-foot container of wooden furniture to a buyer in Jebel Ali on a weekly Middle East service. The booking confirmation states an estimated departure on Thursday, a cargo cut-off on Tuesday at 16:00, and a documentation cut-off on Wednesday at noon. All times and details are illustrative.

Step 1: The plan. The forwarder books the truck to gate-in on Monday, a full day before the cut-off, and the factory confirms that the stuffed, sealed container and its VGM declaration will be ready Monday morning.

Step 2: The slip. The factory's production runs late; the container is stuffed on Monday night instead, and the truck leaves for the terminal on Tuesday after lunch. On the highway, a breakdown ahead holds the truck for more than an hour. It reaches the terminal gate at 16:40, forty minutes after the cargo cut-off for that sailing.

Step 3: The rollover. The gate is closed to that sailing's cargo. The container cannot be worked into the vessel's stowage plan, which was finalised on the day's cut-off data. The line rolls the booking to the next sailing, seven days later, and issues revised cut-offs. The container gates in the following Wednesday, and the furniture sails a week late, arriving about a week after the date the sale anticipated.

Step 4: The customer conversation. The forwarder telephones the exporter the same afternoon, before the exporter hears of it from the buyer, and states the facts plainly: the container missed the cargo cut-off by forty minutes; the booking is rolled to next Thursday's sailing; the revised estimated arrival is a week later than promised; the additional costs are itemised and attributed; and the buyer's delivery commitment needs revising today. The call follows the discipline of Lesson 5.4, Shipment Tracking and Customer Communication: facts, impact, revised dates, and a written record the same day, not blame, and not silence.

Step 5: The prevention. Afterwards the parties change the plan's shape: gate-in is booked a full day before every cut-off, the factory's stuffing date is confirmed the day before the truck, and any risk to a deadline is escalated to the line before the cut-off passes, while options such as a later truck or an amended booking still exist.

What does this example demonstrate?

A sailing is planned from its cut-off data, so a late container is not a box that missed a truck; it is a box the vessel's plan never contained. Forty minutes became seven days.

Key lesson: the cargo cut-off is immovable. Plan to beat it by a day, and escalate before it passes, never after.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Shanghai to Basel: an arrival that needs a second leg
BODY:
An engineering firm in Basel, Switzerland, a landlocked city far from any deep-sea port, buys machine components from a supplier in Shanghai. The routing the forwarder books is a study in transhipment. All times and figures are illustrative.

Step 1: The routing. The container sails on a mainline service from Shanghai to Rotterdam, a European hub, in about thirty days. From Rotterdam it continues on a scheduled Rhine barge service to Basel, with barge departures twice weekly, and the final kilometres to the factory are completed by truck. One sale, one container, three legs.

Step 2: The first leg. The container departs Shanghai on the mainline vessel. The firm's planner notes the published ETA at Rotterdam and, three days after it, the barge departure the forwarder has nominated for the onward leg.

Step 3: The missed connection. Congestion at the hub holds the mainline vessel, and its actual arrival at Rotterdam is recorded three days after the estimate, after the nominated barge has sailed. The next barge departure is four days later, and barges keep their own schedules and cut-offs just as deep-sea vessels do. The container waits in the hub's yard, and the connection it missed cannot be recovered; it can only be replaced by the next one.

Step 4: The second leg and arrival. The container is loaded aboard the next barge, sails up the Rhine, and reaches Basel, where a truck completes the delivery to the factory. The door-to-door journey has taken about a week longer than the ocean ETA alone suggested.

Step 5: What the second leg adds. The firm learns the arithmetic of hub routings: total transit equals the mainline leg, plus the wait for the connection, plus the inland leg. Each connection is a fresh chance for one leg's delay to become the next leg's absence, so a transshipped routing needs a planning margin that a direct routing does not.

What does this example demonstrate?

Transshipment is not a delay in itself; it is how landlocked and smaller destinations reach the world's mainlines. But every transfer point converts upstream delay into downstream waiting unless the plan carries a margin.

Key lesson: plan hub-to-door transit from the final destination, not from the first port of discharge, and treat every connection as a date that can be missed.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: The schedule is a plan, not a promise
BODY:
Every date a shipping line publishes, the ETD, the ETA, the port calls on the rotation, is a plan, and the plan is exposed to weather, congestion, mechanical trouble, and the accumulated slippage of every port before yours. Three habits follow.

First, read estimates as estimates. A booking confirmation's dates state the service's intention, not a guaranteed arrival time. Plans that genuinely matter, a buyer's launch, a factory's production run, a vessel connection, need a buffer between the published ETA and the date the goods are truly required.

Second, expect delay to cascade. A vessel that leaves one port half a day late rarely recovers by the next: the rotation's remaining calls compress, terminal windows shift, and the delay can travel with the ship for days until the schedule's built-in slack absorbs it. This is why an ATD recorded at a port thousands of kilometres away is still the importer's information; it moves the ETA.

Third, treat reliability as variable. Some services and trades run to time more dependably than others, and the same service can differ by season and by port. That judgement is qualitative, and it belongs in every routing decision: a tight connection on a lane known for congestion is a risk bought knowingly or not at all.

None of this excuses poor communication. The forwarder's duty, taught in Lesson 5.4, is to pass schedule changes on the day they are known, with a revised estimate and its consequences, because a customer can manage a delay it is told about, and cannot manage one it discovers.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Verified gross mass is the shipper's obligation
BODY:
The VGM rule deserves its own warning, because the most dangerous habit in container shipping is guessing a weight.

The obligation is the shipper's. Under SOLAS, the shipper must obtain and communicate the verified gross mass of every packed container, by weighing the packed container or by adding the container's tare to contents weights obtained with calibrated equipment. The carrier does not weigh your box for you, and an estimated figure from a packing list is not a verified gross mass.

The reason is physical. A container ship's stability, its stacking plan, and the lashings that hold thousands of boxes through storms are all calculated from the declared weights. A container declared at 12 tonnes that actually weighs 19 tonnes sits where no 19-tonne box was meant to sit. Mis-declared weights can collapse stacks, injure crew, and force a vessel to re-stow mid-voyage. An inaccurate VGM is therefore not a paperwork error; it endangers the vessel, the crew, and every other shipper's cargo.

The consequence is commercial as well, and it lands on the shipper's own box: a container with no verified gross mass, or with a declaration the line cannot accept, will not be loaded. It waits ashore, missing its sailing and usually its customer's date, until the weight is properly declared.

The professional habit is short: weigh or calculate honestly, declare early, and treat Method 2 figures as measured facts from calibrated equipment, never as arithmetic built on estimates. The weight you declare is a promise the ship is loaded on.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Never assume a cut-off: the booking confirmation governs
BODY:
Cut-offs are the most dangerous numbers in the cycle to carry in your head from a previous shipment, because they are never general; they are specific.

They differ per port: the same line's cut-offs at Tema and at its next port of call differ, because different terminals work to different rhythms. They differ per line: two lines calling the same port on the same day can close their cargo gates hours apart. They differ per cargo and equipment: consolidated cargo built at a container freight station, refrigerated cargo needing inspection before shipment, and out-of-gauge cargo all face their own arrangements and often earlier deadlines. And they move week to week: a service's cut-offs shift with the rotation's timing, so last Thursday's cut-off tells you nothing certain about this Thursday's.

The only source that matters is the booking confirmation for the sailing actually booked: the vessel, the voyage, the cargo cut-off, the documentation cut-off. Reading it on the day it arrives, and planning backwards from its dates, is what turns the shipping cycle from a scramble into a schedule.

The professional habits are short. Check the confirmation against the operation the day it is received. Put the cut-offs into the plan before the truck is booked, not after. Chase the shipping instruction days before the documentation cut-off, not hours before. And when a deadline is at risk, call the line before it passes, because options exist before a cut-off that no longer exist after it.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A grain trader has 25,000 tonnes of maize to move between two ports, with timing flexible within a month. A furniture exporter has one 40-foot container of goods to move on a trade lane served by a weekly container service.

Which statement best describes the service each should use?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Both should use liner services, because all ocean cargo travels on published schedules.
2. The grain trader should hire a tramp vessel for the whole cargo under a charter arrangement, while the furniture exporter should book space with a liner service sailing on a published schedule.
3. Both should hire tramp vessels, because tramp shipping is always cheaper than liner shipping.
4. The grain trader should book space on the weekly container service, while the furniture exporter should charter a whole vessel for its single container.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The cargo decides the service. Tramp shipping employs vessels according to cargo availability and commercial arrangements rather than fixed schedules: a trader with 25,000 tonnes of maize fills a whole ship, so the commercial answer is to hire the vessel entirely, normally under a charter party, and send it where the cargo requires. Liner shipping operates on established routes with published or planned schedules and sells space by the container: an exporter with one 40-foot container cannot fill a ship and needs the regular sailings of a scheduled service to plan against.

Answer 1 fails because tramp cargo does not travel on published schedules at all; the two families exist precisely because cargoes differ. Answer 3 fails because tramp shipping is not always cheaper; cost depends on the cargo, and a single container chartered alone would be absurdly expensive. Answer 4 reverses the logic entirely: a bulk cargo of 25,000 tonnes cannot travel as container space, and one container cannot justify a whole vessel.

The takeaway: liners sell space by the schedule and suit containerised general cargo; tramps sell the whole ship by the voyage and suit bulk commodities.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
A forwarder holds a booking confirmation stating an estimated departure on Thursday, a cargo cut-off on Tuesday at 16:00, and a documentation cut-off on Wednesday at 12:00. The container will be stuffed only on Wednesday morning, and the weighbridge appointment for its verified gross mass is on Wednesday afternoon.

Which statement best describes the shipment's position?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The shipment is safe, because the vessel does not depart until Thursday and the container only needs to reach the terminal before the vessel sails.
2. The plan fails the cargo cut-off: the packed container with its VGM declared must be at the terminal by Tuesday at 16:00, so a Wednesday gate-in cannot load, and the operation must be expedited or moved to the next sailing.
3. The position is acceptable, because the verified gross mass can be waived when the packing list already states the gross weight of the goods.
4. The position is acceptable, because the documentation cut-off is the only binding deadline, and the shipping instructions can still be submitted on Wednesday morning.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The cargo cut-off is the deadline by which the packed container, with its verified gross mass declared, must be delivered to the terminal, because the line and terminal finalise the stowage plan, the manifest data, and the weight records from the cut-off position. A container stuffed on Wednesday and weighed on Wednesday afternoon cannot gate in by Tuesday at 16:00, so it cannot load on Thursday's vessel; the choices are to expedite the operation against a revised arrangement with the line or to accept a rollover to the next sailing.

Answer 1 fails because the deadline that matters is the cut-off, not the departure; the gate closes a day or more before the vessel sails. Answer 3 fails because verified gross mass is a shipper's obligation under SOLAS and is never waived; a packing list weight is an estimate, not a verification. Answer 4 fails because there are two separate deadlines and both bind: meeting the documentation cut-off while missing the cargo cut-off still leaves the container ashore.

The takeaway: plan backwards from the cargo cut-off, not from the departure date, and treat the VGM declaration as part of meeting it.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
An importer's booking confirmation showed an ETD on the 3rd and an ETA on the 28th, with a transhipment at a hub port. The line has now recorded the ATD as the 5th. The customer asks whether the goods will still arrive on the 28th.

Which response reflects correct professional practice?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Yes, because the ETA stated on the booking confirmation is a commitment that does not change after the booking is made.
2. No, but the exact arrival date is already known, because once the ATD has been recorded the ATA is determined with it.
3. The departure slipped two days, so the ETA must be re-assessed and the customer given a revised estimate, especially where the transhipment connection can be missed; only the ATA will confirm the actual arrival.
4. The delay entitles the importer to have the cargo transferred to a faster vessel at no additional cost.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The ETD and the ETA are estimates, and the ATD is a recorded fact showing the plan has already slipped by two days. Every downstream date timed against the old estimate is now questionable, and on a transshipped routing the risk is sharper still: the container must reach the hub in time for its onward vessel, so a late arrival can mean a missed connection and a wait of days for the next departure. Correct practice is to re-assess the ETA in the light of the ATD, obtain the line's revised estimate, and inform the customer promptly, exactly the tracking and communication discipline Lesson 5.4 taught. The actual time of arrival is recorded only when the vessel actually arrives.

Answer 1 fails because an ETA is an estimate, not a promise. Answer 2 fails because the ATD does not determine the ATA; delays, congestion, and connections stand between the two. Answer 4 invents an entitlement that does not exist; the remedy is honest re-planning, not a guaranteed transfer.

The takeaway: treat ETD and ETA as plans, watch the actuals, re-assess connections after any slip, and communicate the revised estimate the day it is known.
