=== ASSESSMENT Q1 | tests lesson 7.1 ===
QUESTION:
A truck delivering a laden container to the terminal at Tema reaches the gate forty minutes after the cargo cut-off for Thursday's sailing. The vessel is not expected alongside until Friday evening, and the driver argues that there is clearly time to load the box.

Which statement best explains the terminal's refusal?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The refusal is discretionary discipline for late trucks; with the vessel not yet alongside, the line can still add the container to the stowage plan at loading time.
2. The cut-off closes the sailing's cargo position because the stowage plan, the export and manifest data, and the verified weights are finalised from it, so a container the plan never contained cannot be absorbed and the booking rolls to the next sailing.
3. A container may gate in after the cut-off provided its verified gross mass was declared on time, since the declared weight is the only planning data the vessel needs.
4. The cut-off exists so that the terminal can raise its invoices; once the shipper settles the terminal charges, the container can still make the sailing.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

A sailing is an engineered operation planned from its cut-off position, and the lesson names the three bodies of data that must be final before the plan can be built: the stowage plan, which decides where every container sits aboard and depends on verified weights; the export declaration and the vessel's manifest data, which must be complete before departure; and the VGM verification for every box. Once the cut-off passes, the plan is closed. A container that arrives afterwards is, in the lesson's words, a container the plan never accounted for, and the remedy is a rollover to the next sailing, about a week later on a weekly service.

Answer 1 treats the refusal as discretionary, but the gate is not queue discipline; it exists so that the plans can be built from facts, and a plan cannot be rebuilt around a box that gates in late. Answer 3 seizes the partial truth that VGM matters and ignores that the weights are one of three data sets that close with the cut-off, alongside stowage and manifest information. Answer 4 mistakes an operational planning deadline for a billing milestone; settlement does not rebuild a stowage plan.

The takeaway: the cargo cut-off is immovable because the whole sailing's loading plan is built from the cut-off position, so plan to beat it by a day.

=== ASSESSMENT Q2 | tests lesson 7.1 ===
QUESTION:
An exporter's container gated in at the terminal on Monday, ahead of Tuesday's cargo cut-off, with its verified gross mass declared. The shipping instruction was submitted complete the same day. On Thursday, after the documentation cut-off for the sailing has passed, the exporter asks the forwarder to change the consignee named on the bill of lading.

Which statement best describes the position?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. No issue: with the container gated in and its VGM declared, every deadline for the sailing has been met, and the consignee can be updated at any time before the vessel reaches the port of discharge.
2. The bill of lading can never be amended after the documentation cut-off has passed, so the shipment must be cancelled and rebooked to change the consignee.
3. The documentation cut-off is the deadline for finalising the shipping instruction from which the carrier prepares the bill of lading; once it has passed, the details stand as submitted for the vessel's papers, and a change is an amendment to be arranged and confirmed with the carrier, not assumed.
4. Consignee details are purely a destination customs matter, so the ocean cut-offs have no bearing on changing them.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The lesson teaches two separate clocks before a sailing. The cargo cut-off closes the terminal's gate against the packed container and its declared VGM; the documentation cut-off is the deadline for finalising the shipping instruction, the consignee, cargo description, weights, marks and container details from which the carrier prepares the bill of lading. Gate-in satisfies only the first clock. After the documentation cut-off, the details stand as submitted for the vessel's papers, so a consignee change is an amendment to be requested and confirmed with the carrier, subject to the carrier's procedures and costs, never a quiet edit assumed to have taken effect.

Answer 1 collapses the two deadlines into one and assumes a change can wait until arrival; the transport document is prepared, issued and used long before the vessel berths at destination. Answer 2 swings to the opposite extreme: the lesson says alterations after the cut-off are amendments, which are possible but must be arranged, not impossible. Answer 4 misplaces the responsibility; the consignee appears on the carrier's transport document, which is prepared at origin from the shipping instruction.

The takeaway: beating the cargo cut-off does not clear the paperwork; both cut-offs govern, and each sailing's actual dates are read from the booking confirmation.

=== ASSESSMENT Q3 | tests lesson 7.1 ===
QUESTION:
A trader in Lome has sold goods to a buyer in Rotterdam and books space on a liner service whose published rotation reads: Rotterdam, Antwerp, Tema, Lome, then back to Rotterdam.

Which statement correctly identifies the ports for this shipment?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The port of loading is Lome and the port of discharge is Rotterdam: on this service the vessel calls at Lome after Tema and then returns to Rotterdam, so cargo loaded at Lome is discharged at Rotterdam.
2. The port of loading is Rotterdam and the port of discharge is Lome, because Rotterdam is listed first in the published rotation.
3. The service cannot carry the cargo, because a published rotation lists each port only once.
4. The port of loading must be Tema, because it is the larger container port and every service on the coast loads there first.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

A port rotation is the planned sequence of ports a vessel visits, read in calling order, and the service repeats its rotation voyage after voyage. On the leg that concerns this trader, the vessel sails from Europe, calls at Tema, then Lome, and returns to Rotterdam: the port of loading is Lome, where the cargo is loaded aboard, and the port of discharge is Rotterdam, where it is unloaded. Reading the rotation is also how a shipper confirms that a service can carry the cargo at all: both the origin and the destination must appear in the string, in the right order for the direction of the shipment.

Answer 2 reads the published list as a diary of the whole loop and forgets that the rotation repeats; the first port listed is not the loading port for every direction of trade. Answer 3 invents a rule; a rotation lists each port once per loop precisely because the service returns to it voyage after voyage. Answer 4 substitutes commercial folklore for the schedule: a port's prominence in the region has no bearing on which ports a named service calls, or in what order.

The takeaway: read the rotation in calling order for the direction of the cargo, and the port of loading and port of discharge identify themselves.

=== ASSESSMENT Q4 | tests lesson 7.2 ===
QUESTION:
A forwarder is quoting an air consignment of five identical cartons of canned food, booked as one consignment on the same flight. Each carton measures 50 x 40 x 30 centimetres and weighs 14 kilograms. A colleague who has heard that air freight is charged on volumetric weight quotes the consignment at 50 chargeable kilograms. All figures are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Which statement correctly determines the chargeable weight?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. 50 chargeable kilograms is correct, because once carton dimensions are declared, air freight is always charged on volumetric weight.
2. The consignment is charged on 70 kilograms, because chargeable weight is the greater of the actual gross weight, 70 kilograms, and the volumetric weight, 5 multiplied by 50 x 40 x 30 divided by 6,000, which is 50 kilograms.
3. The consignment is charged on 120 kilograms, because when the two weights differ, the volumetric weight and the actual weight are charged together.
4. The consignment pays a share of the whole unit load's chargeable weight, because once the cartons are built into an aircraft pallet they are charged as part of that unit load.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Chargeable weight is the greater of the actual gross weight and the volumetric weight, applied to the consignment's own figures. The volumetric weight of one carton is 50 x 40 x 30, which is 60,000 cubic centimetres, divided by the IATA divisor of 6,000: 10 kilograms. Five identical cartons give a total volumetric weight of 50 kilograms against a total gross weight of 70 kilograms. The actual weight is greater, so the consignment is charged on 70 chargeable kilograms. This is the mirror image of the light, bulky case the lesson dramatised: here the cargo exhausts the aircraft's payload before its space, so the scales, not the tape measure, decide the freight.

Answer 1 commits the inverse of the lesson's cardinal error: volumetric weight does not always govern; the rule is a comparison, and this time the actual weight wins. Answer 3 adds the two measures together, but they are alternatives in a comparison, never a sum; adding them would charge the same kilograms twice. Answer 4 merges the consignment into its unit load, but chargeable weight is assessed on each consignment's own weights, not on the consolidated unit load it later travels in.

The takeaway: compute both weights for the consignment, charge the greater, and never assume in advance which one will win.

=== ASSESSMENT Q5 | tests lesson 7.2 ===
QUESTION:
A forwarder delivers a consignment of general cargo to the airline's cargo terminal for a booked flight. The air waybill data and the booking state 12 pieces, but the physical count at acceptance finds 10 cartons.

Which statement best describes the position?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The airline must accept the consignment because the space was confirmed at booking; acceptance merely records what was delivered.
2. A two-piece discrepancy is minor: it can be noted on the documents and corrected with the consignee after arrival.
3. Checking the piece count is the forwarder's job before delivery; the airline or its ground handling agent checks only dangerous goods and security at the terminal.
4. Acceptance compares the physical consignment with the documents and the booking, and the three must agree, so the discrepancy stops acceptance until it is resolved, and the cargo does not fly until then.
CORRECT: 4
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 4.

Cargo acceptance is the airline's gatekeeping process, performed by the airline or its ground handling agent at the terminal, and its decisive principle is that the goods, the documents and the booking must agree. The physical check counts the pieces against the document, so a consignment presenting ten cartons against twelve on the air waybill data and the booking is a discrepancy that stops the process until it is resolved: the documents are corrected or the missing pieces are found. Until then the consignment is not accepted, and if the fault is not fixed before the acceptance cut-off, the cargo misses the flight, whatever the departure board says.

Answer 1 treats a confirmed booking as a guarantee of acceptance, but the booking assures space on the flight; acceptance is a separate gate that the consignment itself must pass. Answer 2 waves the discrepancy through, yet goods and a document that contradicts them are exactly what acceptance exists to stop, because the paperwork travels with the cargo into customs at the destination. Answer 3 hands the count to the forwarder, but the count at acceptance is the airline-side comparison of the physical goods against the document.

The takeaway: at acceptance the consignment is measured against its papers, and any disagreement stops the process until goods and papers agree.

=== ASSESSMENT Q6 | tests lesson 7.2 ===
QUESTION:
A new exporter delivers a consignment of leather goods to the air cargo terminal for a booked international flight. At the terminal, the exporter's colleague says the cargo need not be screened because it is ordinary general cargo.

Which statement best describes the security requirement?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Every consignment's security status must be established before loading: the cargo either travels under an approved trusted shipper's assessed status, commonly described as a known consignor, or it is screened before acceptance into the air side, and the status cannot be claimed at the desk.
2. Screening applies only to dangerous goods, because ordinary general cargo presents no threat to an aircraft.
3. A shipper may declare its own trusted-shipper status at the terminal on the day of travel and travel without screening from that point.
4. Consignments on dedicated freighter aircraft are exempt from screening, because no passengers are carried on the flight.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

Air cargo is security cargo: no consignment may be loaded unless its security status is established. The lesson teaches two broad routes, described generically because each country implements them through its own national regime. Cargo from an approved trusted shipper, commonly described as a known consignor, enters the air side on the strength of that shipper's assessed supply-chain controls, and the approval is conditional on maintaining the required procedures. Every other consignment must be screened, by scanning equipment or physical examination, before or at acceptance into the air side, and the status travels with the cargo as data and paperwork. Cargo whose status cannot be established is not loaded; it waits, which in practice can mean a missed flight.

Answer 2 narrows security to dangerous goods, but screening examines cargo for prohibited articles whatever the commodity. Answer 3 converts an assessed approval into a self-declaration, which is precisely what the concept does not allow: trusted-shipper status is earned through sustained compliant procedures, not asserted at the desk. Answer 4 invents a freighter exemption; the security concern is the loaded aircraft and the air cargo chain, not the passenger cabin alone.

The takeaway: security status is part of the acceptance picture, so allow time for it before the cut-off and never assume it can be claimed on the day.

=== ASSESSMENT Q7 | tests lesson 7.3 ===
QUESTION:
A project forwarder in Tema must ship an excavator that is wider than a standard container's internal width, together with fourteen cartons of spare parts of ordinary general cargo.

Which equipment plan is correct?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. A high-cube container, because the extra internal height creates room for tall machinery.
2. An open-top container, because machinery is lowered in from above through the removable roof, which also solves the width problem.
3. A flat-rack container, on which the out-of-gauge excavator is lashed and secured, with the cartons of spare parts packed separately in an ordinary dry container.
4. One forty-foot dry container for everything, because cargo securing straps can restrain the excavator safely between the cartons.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The lesson's selection rule turns on the cargo's own dimensions. An excavator wider than a standard container's internal width is out-of-gauge cargo, and out-of-gauge cargo points to a flat rack: a platform with fixed or collapsible end structures but no sides and no roof, built for items that are wider, longer or simply not rectangular, with lashing, securing and weather protection planned as part of the loading. The spare parts are ordinary general cargo with no special requirement, so they take a dry container beside the flat rack.

Answer 2 is the attractive trap, and it fails on one word: width. An open-top has a removable roof for top loading, which answers cargo that is too tall for the door aperture or too awkward to load through doors, but its sides are fixed, so cargo wider than the box cannot sit inside it. Answer 1 fails because a high cube adds height on the same footprint; the internal width is unchanged, and no amount of extra height admits a load that is too wide. Answer 4 ignores both the width and the lesson's compatibility rule: heavy machinery braced among fragile cartons is an inadvisable mix even if it physically fit.

The takeaway: characterise the cargo first, then choose the box; top loading answers height, and only a flat rack answers width.

=== ASSESSMENT Q8 | tests lesson 7.3 ===
QUESTION:
A small exporter in Takoradi ships about three pallets, roughly 8 cubic metres and 900 kilograms, to a buyer in Copenhagen four or five times a year. A colleague insists that every ocean shipment should be booked as FCL because LCL cargo travels loose in the ship's hold and is always damaged.

Which statement gives the correct guidance?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The colleague is right: FCL is always cheaper than LCL, whatever the volume, because a whole container is one price negotiation.
2. At this volume LCL is the natural choice: the consignment occupies part of a container consolidated with other shippers' cargo at a container freight station, the consolidated box travels sealed like any container, and the choice should weigh cost per unit, handling risk, timing and cargo sensitivity rather than follow a rule that FCL is always better.
3. LCL cargo cannot be sealed or tracked, so the exporter must book a twenty-foot container even though it would travel nearly empty.
4. Volume is irrelevant to the decision; the exporter should simply alternate between FCL and LCL from one shipment to the next.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The lesson's first decision factor is volume against a full box: a twenty-foot container offers about 33 cubic metres, and three pallets of roughly 8 cubic metres use only a fraction of it, which makes the consignment a natural LCL. An LCL shipment occupies part of a container and is consolidated with other shippers' cargo at a container freight station, and the consolidated box is sealed and moves as one unit, exactly like any container. The choice should then weigh the remaining factors: cost per unit, handling risk at stuffing and deconsolidation, the earlier CFS cut-off, and cargo sensitivity.

Answer 1 asserts that FCL is always cheaper; the lesson teaches the opposite shape: at low volumes the LCL share usually costs less in total, and the crossing point arrives well before a box is full, so the exporter would pay for space it does not use. Answer 3 describes LCL cargo as unsealable, but the seal travels on the consolidated container's doors, not on individual consignments, and the container number identifies the box wherever it moves. Answer 4 replaces the decision with a habit; the five factors exist because the shipment, not a formula, decides.

The takeaway: neither FCL nor LCL is universally right; small, occasional volumes are LCL's natural ground, packed well for the neighbours a shared box brings.

=== ASSESSMENT Q9 | tests lesson 7.3 ===
QUESTION:
A booking confirmation covers one forty-foot high-cube container and two twenty-foot dry containers on the same sailing.

Which statement correctly states the capacity the booking consumes in the industry's counting units?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Three TEU, one for each physical box on the booking.
2. Five TEU, because the high cube's extra height earns it three TEU against one for each twenty-foot box.
3. It cannot be stated until sailing, because TEU counts the cargo actually carried rather than the boxes booked.
4. Four TEU, because the high cube counts as one FEU, generally equal to two TEU, and each twenty-foot box counts as one TEU.
CORRECT: 4
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 4.

The twenty-foot equivalent unit measures nominal length: one TEU is one nominal twenty-foot container, and one FEU is one nominal forty-foot container, generally equal to two TEUs. The booking covers one forty-foot box and two twenty-foot boxes: the forty-foot high cube counts as one FEU, that is two TEU, and each twenty-foot box counts as one TEU, giving four TEU in total. The high cube's extra height changes nothing, because the unit measures the nominal length, not the volume; the lesson makes this explicit when it notes that a forty-foot high cube still counts as one FEU despite standing a foot taller than the standard box.

Answer 1 counts physical boxes, which confuses the number of units handled with the capacity measure in which vessels, ports and the industry count their business. Answer 2 awards the high cube a third unit for its extra height, but the TEU has no volumetric exchange rate; the high cube's roughly 75 to 76 cubic metres change the cargo plan, not the count. Answer 3 ties the count to the fill, yet TEU and FEU measure container capacity, not the cargo actually carried; an empty forty-foot box is still one FEU.

The takeaway: count TEUs from the nominal length of the boxes booked, and let a high cube's height influence the load plan, never the arithmetic.

=== ASSESSMENT Q10 | tests lesson 7.4 ===
QUESTION:
A forwarder in Accra is rating an LCL consignment of six crates of machine spare parts for ocean shipment. The crates weigh 9,000 kilograms in total and measure 8 cubic metres. The LCL rate is quoted per weight or measurement tonne, whichever produces the higher charge. All figures are illustrative for teaching purposes.

On which quantity will the freight be charged?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. On 8 revenue tonnes, because LCL cargo shares a box and is therefore always charged on the space it occupies.
2. On 9 revenue tonnes, because the chargeable quantity under weight or measurement rating is the greater of the weight in metric tonnes and the volume in cubic metres, and dense cargo exhausts the weight allowance first.
3. On 17 revenue tonnes, because the weight and the measurement are added together before the rate is applied.
4. On the number of crates, because consolidated cargo is charged by the handling unit rather than by weight or measurement.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Weight or measurement rating exists because a consignment can exhaust either the vessel's weight allowance or its space, so the tariff charges whichever measure produces the higher quantity: weight in metric tonnes against measurement in cubic metres, the greater becoming the revenue tonnes charged. Six crates weighing 9 tonnes and measuring 8 cubic metres convert to 9 against 8, so the weight wins and the consignment is charged on 9 revenue tonnes. This is the dense-cargo mirror image of the lesson's worked case, in which light plastic household goods paid on 6.8 cubic metres rather than 2.4 tonnes; the method is identical, only the winner changes with the cargo.

Answer 1 assumes LCL is always charged on space, which would undercharge exactly the dense cargo that fills a box's weight allowance long before its volume; weight governs only when it is the greater figure, as it is here. Answer 3 adds the two measures, but they are alternatives in a comparison, never combined; adding them would charge part of the consignment twice. Answer 4 invents a per-package basis, while consolidation rates are built on the consignment's weight or measurement.

The takeaway: under W/M, convert the consignment to both tonnes and cubic metres, charge the higher, and let the figures, not the cargo's appearance, choose the basis.

=== ASSESSMENT Q11 | tests lesson 7.4 ===
QUESTION:
An importer in Kumasi is arranging shipment of machinery from Shanghai to Tema. Forwarder P quotes 2,300 US dollars, port to port, and lists destination terminal handling, assistance with customs clearance, and delivery to the warehouse as excluded charges that together add 480 US dollars, payable locally at destination. Forwarder Q quotes 2,700 US dollars, door to door, including all of those charges. A colleague says Forwarder P is 400 US dollars cheaper and should be chosen immediately. All figures are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Which statement is correct?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Forwarder P is cheaper, and the 400 US dollar difference stands, because destination charges are minor extras that never change a comparison.
2. The two quotations cannot be compared at all, because every forwarder builds its prices differently and no common basis exists between offers.
3. Rebuilt to equal scope, Forwarder P's offer costs 2,780 US dollars, so Forwarder Q is 80 US dollars cheaper on the same scope; headline totals built on different scopes cannot be compared directly.
4. Forwarder Q should be chosen automatically, because a door-to-door quotation is always better value than a port-to-port quotation regardless of the figures.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The two quotations are built on different scopes, so the headline comparison measures scope, not value. Rebuilt to equal scope, Forwarder P's offer is 2,300 plus the 480 US dollars of destination charges it excludes, which is 2,780; Forwarder Q's 2,700 already covers the door. On equal scope Q is 80 US dollars cheaper, and the colleague's 400-dollar saving reverses once the destination tail is priced. The lesson's reading method is exactly this rebuild: read each quotation down to its lines, base freight, surcharges and origin local charges, add the destination charges it excludes, and compare only totals that cover the same scope, before weighing conditions such as validity.

Answer 1 repeats the headline error and pairs it with the lesson's most expensive assumption, that destination charges are minor extras; the local tail can rival the main carriage itself. Answer 2 abandons comparison altogether and forfeits the line-by-line discipline that makes quotations readable. Answer 4 buys inclusiveness as such, yet door to door is not automatically value; Q wins here on the rebuilt figures, not on its scope label.

The takeaway: rebuild both quotations to the same scope, then compare price, then conditions; a low headline measures what is missing, not what is saved.

=== ASSESSMENT Q12 | tests lesson 7.4 ===
QUESTION:
An exporter in Tema has sold a consignment of cocoa products to a buyer in Dakar on terms under which the buyer, not the exporter, arranges the main carriage (an E or F rule of Incoterms 2020). The forwarder asks how the freight payment should be designated before booking.

Which designation and guidance is correct?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Freight collect: the charges are payable at destination by the designated receiving party, so before booking the exporter should confirm that the buyer can and will settle promptly, because carriers release cargo against payment.
2. Freight prepaid, because the goods leave the seller's country and the seller therefore always settles the freight before shipment.
3. Either designation, because carriers treat prepaid and collect identically and the marking carries no operational effect.
4. Freight collect, with the buyer free to take delivery on arrival and settle the freight afterwards at its convenience.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

A freight collect designation indicates that the charges are payable at destination, or by the designated receiving party, under the agreed arrangement. The lesson links the marking to the sales terms: under Incoterms 2020 the E and F rules place the arrangement of the main carriage with the buyer, and where the buyer arranges the carriage the movement is commonly booked on collect terms so the carrier recovers its charges at the far end. Because carriers release cargo against settlement, the exporter should confirm before booking that the buyer can and will pay promptly, since a slow or unreachable consignee strands the cargo at destination, out of the exporter's control.

Answer 2 flips the logic: freight prepaid marks charges settled at or before shipment, which follows the seller arranging the carriage under the C and D rules, not this sale. Answer 3 declares the marking a formality, but prepaid and collect decide who pays and at which end, and the marking on the document, not the parties' assumption, is the authority. Answer 4 detaches payment from delivery, yet release of the cargo is precisely what awaits settlement; collect is not open credit after arrival.

The takeaway: prepaid or collect follows the party that arranges the main carriage, is verified before booking, and is marked consistently on the documents.
