=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The air freight product and why shippers choose it
BODY:
Air cargo is goods transported by aircraft for commercial or logistical purposes, and air freight is the transportation of goods by aircraft. Behind those definitions sits a distinct product with its own participants, documents, and operating discipline. Lesson 7.1, Ocean Freight Operations and Vessel Schedules, examined the sea leg of world trade; this lesson turns to the air above the same lanes and, centrally, to cargo acceptance, the gatekeeping process every air consignment must pass before it flies.

Businesses choose air freight when the value of speed outweighs its cost.

1. Speed. An aircraft crosses in hours what a vessel crosses in weeks. For urgent shipments the difference is not convenience but the point of the transaction: a production line stopped for a missing part, a customer waiting for a sample, a market window for fresh produce.

2. Security. Air cargo passes through controlled airport environments, screening procedures, and closely supervised handling. Exposure time is short, and the cargo's whereabouts are known at every stage.

3. Lower in-transit exposure. Because goods spend hours rather than weeks in transit, less capital is tied up in inventory on the move, insurance exposure over the journey is shorter, and goods reach the market sooner. These savings partly offset air freight's higher price per consignment.

What suits air? Perishable produce, pharmaceuticals, electronics and other high-value goods, urgent spare parts, and samples and documents are the classic air commodities: cargo whose value or vulnerability makes time the decisive factor.

Air is also an airport-to-airport product. An aircraft cannot call at a factory gate or a shop, so every air movement is completed by road: collection to the origin airport and delivery from the destination airport. Road feeder services, truck movements operating as part of the air network under air cargo documents, extend the air product to points no aircraft serves directly, so the waybill may name airports at either end while the goods travel the first or last kilometres by road.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The participants in the air cargo chain
BODY:
An air consignment passes through more hands than a new exporter expects. Lesson 2.2 introduced the ecosystem of intermediaries; in air freight the main participants are these.

1. The shipper. The exporter sending the goods. The shipper prepares the consignment, states its contents, weight, and dimensions, signs the transport document, and bears responsibility for the accuracy of that declaration.

2. The freight forwarder. The organiser of the shipment, introduced in Lesson 5.1, Role and Responsibilities of Freight Forwarders. In air freight the forwarder books space, prepares the documentation, delivers the goods to the terminal within the acceptance deadlines, and frequently consolidates several customers' consignments into one airline booking.

3. The airline. The carrier operating the aircraft and selling cargo capacity, either directly to shippers with substantial volumes or through forwarders. Some aircraft carry cargo in the holds of passenger services alongside baggage; dedicated freighter aircraft carry cargo as their main payload.

4. The air cargo terminal. The facility at or near the airport used for receiving, handling, storing, and processing air cargo. Terminals are where cargo is checked, screened, stored, and prepared for the aircraft, and where arriving cargo is broken down for collection.

5. The ground handling agent. A company providing ground-based services to airlines, including specified cargo handling activities. At the terminal, the ground handling agent works on the airline's behalf: it checks and accepts cargo, builds unit loads, loads and unloads aircraft, and manages the arrival process.

6. Customs and other authorities. Goods crossing borders pass export and import formalities, and some commodities carry additional health, plant-health, or security controls depending on the countries involved.

The practical picture is simple: the forwarder is the face of the operation, the airline provides the flying, the ground handling agent executes the terminal work, and the authorities control what may cross the border. Acceptance is where all these parties' requirements converge on one consignment.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Booking an air shipment and confirming space
BODY:
Every air movement begins with a booking, the reservation of cargo space on a named flight. The procedure runs in a familiar sequence.

1. Enquiry and quotation. The shipper, usually through a forwarder, describes the consignment: what the goods are, how many packages, the weight and dimensions of each package, any special handling needs, when the goods will be ready, and the destination. The forwarder or airline responds with a service proposal and a price calculated on the chargeable weight, taught in the next concept.

2. Booking request. The forwarder asks the airline for space on a suitable flight. Capacity is finite: a passenger hold fills alongside baggage, a freighter's payload and volume are fixed, and peak seasons leave space tight.

3. Booking confirmation. The airline confirms the space, the flight, and the acceptance details. Only at confirmation is the shipment's place on the aircraft assured.

4. Preparation. The shipper packs, marks, and labels the goods and assembles the documents, and the forwarder prepares the air waybill data and supporting paperwork, such as health or phytosanitary certificates where required.

5. Delivery within the acceptance cut-off. The consignment must reach the airline's cargo terminal before the acceptance deadline for the booked flight.

The details given at booking matter more than beginners assume: the airline plans the load, the space, and the handling from exactly those figures. If the goods delivered differ from the figures booked, the discrepancy surfaces at acceptance, and the shipment may be refused or held until the booking is corrected.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Chargeable weight: why air freight weighs space as well as kilograms
BODY:
Ocean freight, as Lesson 7.1 showed, is charged mainly by container or by the tonne; air freight is charged by weight, but by a special kind of weight. Chargeable weight is the weight used to calculate air freight charges, determined according to applicable actual-weight and volumetric-weight rules. In everyday terms: the carrier takes the greater of the actual gross weight and the volumetric weight, and charges that.

Volumetric weight is a conversion of the space a consignment occupies into equivalent kilograms. Under the IATA standard formula, volumetric weight in kilograms equals the length multiplied by the width multiplied by the height, in centimetres, divided by 6,000. A carton of 60 by 50 by 40 centimetres therefore has a volumetric weight of 120,000 divided by 6,000, which is 20 kilograms, regardless of what it actually weighs. Some carriers and contracts apply different divisors, so the applicable rule should always be confirmed for the trade lane concerned.

Why does the industry charge this way? Because an aircraft sells two scarce resources at once: payload and space. The hold has a maximum weight it may lift and a maximum volume it may fill, and whichever runs out first is the resource the cargo truly consumes.

1. Dense cargo exhausts the weight first. A carton of machine parts may reach the payload limit while occupying only a slice of the hold. Charging it by actual weight is fair to the airline.

2. Light, bulky cargo exhausts the space first. Polystyrene packaging, lampshades, and plastic housewares fill the hold long before the aircraft is heavy. If the airline charged only actual weight, it would be selling floor-to-ceiling air for the price of a few kilograms.

The chargeable weight rule makes every consignment pay for whichever resource it uses more of. The arithmetic is worked through in a later worked example; the habit is simple: measure every package as well as weighing it, because for light goods the tape measure, not the scales, decides the freight.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The air cargo terminal and the acceptance cut-off
BODY:
Between the shipper's premises and the aircraft stands the air cargo terminal, the facility at or near an airport used for receiving, handling, storing, and processing air cargo. The terminal is the air-freight equivalent of the port container terminal taught alongside Lesson 7.1, compressed into a far shorter timescale. Its core functions run in sequence.

1. Receiving and acceptance checking. Cargo is delivered to the terminal, where its documents and physical condition are checked before the airline accepts it.

2. Security screening. Consignments are screened or arrive under approved security status, as explained later in this lesson.

3. Temporary storage. Cargo waits for its flight in secure storage, with cold rooms for perishables and segregated areas for special cargo.

4. Build-up. Loose packages are assembled into unit load devices, the aircraft pallets and containers described later in this lesson.

5. Breakdown. On arrival, the sequence runs in reverse: unit loads are broken down, cargo is checked, notified, and released to the consignee after customs formalities.

The terminal's operating rhythm is set by acceptance cut-off times: the deadlines by which a booked consignment must be delivered, checked, and accepted before its flight. Acceptance is not a single stamp but a process of document checks, physical checks, screening, and build-up, each needing time before loading. Cut-offs therefore close well before departure, and they vary by airline, airport, flight type, and commodity; a shipper works to the deadline given for each booking.

The consequence is the one beginners find hardest to believe: cargo can miss a flight that has not yet departed. An aircraft will leave with hold space empty rather than take a consignment that arrived after acceptance closed, because the paperwork and the load plan close first. Worked Example 1 shows the clock in operation.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Cargo acceptance: the gatekeeping function
BODY:
Cargo acceptance is the process of examining and receiving cargo to determine whether it satisfies applicable transportation requirements. It is the central operational function of this lesson, because acceptance is where the air industry protects itself: the safety of the aircraft, the security of the flight, the accuracy of the documents, and the flow of every other consignment depend on refusing what does not meet the requirements. The airline or its ground handling agent performs acceptance at the terminal, and it has two sides, the documents and the physical goods, checked together.

1. The document check. Acceptance staff compare the paperwork against the booking: the air waybill details must match the parties, the routing, the number of pieces, the weight, and the description of the goods. The supporting attachments must be present where the goods require them: a dangerous goods declaration for regulated hazardous articles, phytosanitary certificates for plants and plant products, health certificates for products of animal origin, and any other required permits. A document that disagrees with the booking, or a missing attachment, stops the process until it is resolved.

2. The physical check. The consignment itself is examined. Pieces are counted against the document. The consignment is weighed and measured against the stated weight and dimensions, the figures the booking and chargeable weight were built on. Packaging is inspected for condition and suitability: damage, leaks, or packing inadequate for the unit-load handling and stacking of air carriage are grounds for refusal. Finally, marking and labelling are checked, including hazard labels for any dangerous goods.

Acceptance ends in one of two outcomes. The consignment is accepted, the acceptance is recorded, and the cargo proceeds to screening and build-up; or a check fails and the cargo is refused until the fault is corrected. The decisive principle is that the goods, the documents, and the booking must agree. Worked Example 3 shows a rejection, and why the rejection is the system working rather than failing.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Packaging, marking, and labelling for air carriage
BODY:
Lesson 3.3, Cargo Types, Packaging, and Handling, treated packaging as the protection of goods in transit. Air carriage adds three further pressures, and each shapes what the acceptance desk will and will not take.

1. Handling and stacking. Air cargo is built into unit loads, stacked inside aircraft containers and on pallets, and handled by equipment at every point of the journey. Packaging must be strong enough for stacking and unit-load handling even though air shipments are lighter than their sea counterparts.

2. Weight economy. Every kilogram of packaging can become chargeable kilograms, because the charge follows the greater of actual and volumetric weight. Air packaging is therefore designed to be light as well as strong, a balance shippers of dense goods in particular learn to appreciate.

3. Identification under pressure. Cargo at a terminal changes hands quickly among people who have never seen the consignment before. The packages must speak for themselves.

That last pressure explains the distinction between marking and labelling. Marking is the identification applied to the packages: the marks and package numbers, the shipper's and consignee's details, and the handling information that ties each physical piece to the air waybill. Markings must agree with the transport document, because at acceptance the two are compared. Labelling is the application of standard labels instructing the chain how to treat the package: handling labels indicate treatment such as orientation or fragility, and hazard labels declare and identify regulated dangerous goods, accompanying the shipper's dangerous goods declaration. A perishable consignment may carry special-handling labels so it is routed to cold storage.

The operational rule is blunt: packages that arrive unmarked, mislabelled, or in packaging unsuitable for air carriage fail the physical acceptance check, and the cargo does not fly until the fault is corrected. Labels are not decoration; they are the instructions the whole terminal acts on.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Security screening and the known-consignor concept
BODY:
Air cargo is security cargo. It travels in aircraft alongside people, and the security of the flight depends on no prohibited article entering the hold. Air cargo screening is the security procedure used to examine cargo for prohibited articles or potential threats before transportation, and no cargo may be loaded onto an aircraft unless its security status is established.

The concept works through two broad routes, and the lesson teaches them generically, because every country implements the principle through its own national regime.

1. Cargo from approved trusted shippers. Security regimes around the world allow shippers whose premises, personnel, and procedures have been assessed and approved to send cargo whose security is established at origin, on the strength of the shipper's own approved supply-chain controls. Such shippers are commonly described as known consignors; their consignments can enter the air side without further screening, though the approval is conditional on maintaining the required procedures.

2. Screened cargo. Cargo that does not arrive under an approved shipper's security status must be examined before acceptance into the air side. In practice the consignment is screened at or before the terminal by recognised means, which include scanning equipment and physical examination, and its screened status is recorded.

The security status travels with the consignment as data and paperwork, exactly as the transport document does: the terminal can see, for every piece, whether it is screened or covered by an approved shipper's status. Cargo whose status cannot be established is not loaded; it waits until screening is completed, which in practice can mean a missed flight.

For the shipper, two conclusions follow: security status is part of the acceptance picture, so the time before a cut-off must cover it; and trusted-shipper approval speeds a regular exporter's consignments through the terminal, but is earned through sustained compliant procedures, not claimed at the desk.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Special cargo, perishables, and dangerous goods acceptance
BODY:
Special cargo is cargo requiring additional handling, documentation, packaging, or transportation precautions because of its characteristics. The category exists because the acceptance procedures taught so far, documents, physical checks, screening, and build-up, are the standard path, and some commodities cannot safely travel that path without additions.

1. Perishable cargo. Goods that may deteriorate unless transported and stored under suitable environmental conditions, such as fresh produce, flowers, and certain foods. Perishables demand a cold chain, the unbroken sequence of temperature-controlled handling from packhouse to aircraft: cold rooms at the terminal, minimum waiting time between acceptance and loading, and often tighter cut-offs so that the goods spend as little time as possible on the ground. Worked Example 1 follows a perishable consignment through acceptance.

2. Temperature-sensitive pharmaceuticals, which add documentation and handling requirements proving and maintaining their storage conditions.

3. Live animals, valuables, and outsize items, each with prescribed packaging, documentation, and aircraft requirements.

4. Dangerous goods. Substances or articles that present hazards during transportation and are subject to applicable safety regulations. Lesson 3.3 introduced dangerous goods among the cargo categories and their handling needs; air carriage applies the strictest version of those requirements. The dangerous goods classes run from Class 1, explosives, through Class 3, flammable liquids, Class 8, corrosives, and Class 9, miscellaneous articles including lithium batteries, and air carriage of such goods is governed by the air industry's dangerous goods regulations, which are based on the international technical instructions for the safe transport of dangerous goods by air and are updated annually.

Dangerous goods acceptance is a discipline of its own. The shipper must classify the goods, declare them on the dangerous goods declaration, use the required performance packaging, and apply the correct hazard labels. At acceptance, dangerous goods may be checked only by trained and qualified staff, who verify the declaration, packaging, marking, and labelling against the applicable checklists; some dangerous goods are restricted to freighter aircraft or excluded from air transport altogether. Worked Example 3 shows what happens when a shipper tries to skip all of this.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Unit load devices and build-up
BODY:
Aircraft holds are curved, and their floors are fitted with restraint systems. Loose cartons loaded piece by piece would waste space, shift in flight, and delay departure, so air cargo travels consolidated in unit load devices. A unit load device, ULD, is an aircraft pallet or container used to consolidate and secure cargo, baggage, or mail for air transportation, and it is treated as equipment of the aircraft itself: built and certified for flight, and a damaged ULD is withdrawn from service rather than loaded.

Two broad families exist.

1. Aircraft containers, rigid boxes shaped to fit the fuselage contours of the hold in which they travel. The industry identifies common types by short codes; a standard lower-deck container, for example, is known by the code AKE.

2. Aircraft pallets, flat platforms on which cargo is built up, secured with nets or straps, and covered where required. A standard main-deck pallet is known by the code PMC.

Build-up is the terminal activity of assembling loose cargo into these devices. The ground handling agent sorts consignments by flight, builds each unit load to a plan respecting weight distribution, secures it, and records its contents so the load documentation agrees with the cargo loaded. Several shippers' consignments on one airline booking may share a unit load; the waybill structure described in the next concept records each consignment within it.

The shipper's stake is practical: packages must fit and survive being built into a unit load, which is why acceptance examines packaging for suitability for air carriage. The carton leaves the shipper and flies inside a certified unit load assembled by professionals at the terminal.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The air waybill, the cargo manifest, and the journey from acceptance to delivery
BODY:
Lesson 6.2, Bill of Lading and Air Waybill, taught the air waybill's legal character: the AWB is a non-negotiable document that evidences the contract of carriage and acknowledges receipt of the cargo, and it is never a document of title. This lesson adds its operational life. The document travels the whole journey as data, and its structure follows the contracts. The master air waybill, MAWB, is issued by the airline or its authorised agent covering cargo accepted under the airline's contract of carriage; the house air waybill, HAWB, is issued by the freight forwarder to a shipper for an individual consignment. Where a forwarder consolidates several customers' goods under one airline booking, each shipper holds its HAWB while the airline's MAWB covers the consolidated consignment, and each waybill number identifies its consignment at every checkpoint from acceptance to delivery. Since 1 January 2019, under IATA Resolution 672, the electronic air waybill has been the default contract of carriage on enabled trade lanes, paper the exception: the content and character are unchanged, but the document exists as data moving ahead of the goods.

Alongside the waybills runs the cargo manifest, a document or electronic record listing cargo carried on a particular transport service. Each flight's manifest tells the receiving airport what is coming, and manifest data commonly reach the destination customs authority before the aircraft lands, supporting advance risk analysis and faster release.

The complete journey of a consignment assembles every concept in this lesson.

1. Booking confirmed for a named flight.
2. Delivery to the terminal before the acceptance cut-off.
3. Acceptance: document check and physical check, including any dangerous goods acceptance.
4. Security screening or acceptance under an approved shipper's status.
5. Build-up into unit load devices.
6. Loading and departure.
7. Arrival, breakdown, and notification of the consignee.
8. Import customs formalities and release.
9. Delivery to the consignee.

Compared with the ocean journey of Lesson 7.1, the pattern is identical and the timescale is compressed: weeks become hours, the vessel becomes the aircraft, the container becomes the ULD, and the documentation cut-off becomes the acceptance cut-off, closing shortly before the flight rather than days before the sailing. The next lesson, Containerisation: FCL, LCL, and Container Types, returns to the sea and examines the container itself.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A vegetable consignment accepted at Kotoka International Airport
BODY:
A vegetable exporter based in Kumasi supplies fresh chillies and courgettes to a produce importer in Amsterdam. On Tuesday the importer requests a consignment of 30 cartons for Thursday's market. All figures and times in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: Booking. The exporter's forwarder asks an airline for space on Thursday evening's departure from Kotoka International Airport, Accra, to Amsterdam, stating the consignment's details: 30 cartons, the weight and dimensions of a packed carton, the nature of the goods, and their perishable handling requirement. The airline confirms the space and gives the acceptance cut-off: Thursday afternoon, several hours before the late-evening departure, because the goods must be accepted, screened, and built up in good time.

Step 2: Preparation and documents. The cartons are packed on Thursday morning from the morning's harvest in vented, stackable cartons, marked with the exporter's and importer's details and package numbers, and labelled for perishable handling. The forwarder assembles the paperwork: air waybill data agreeing with the commercial invoice and packing list, and the phytosanitary certificate issued by the plant-protection authority, since plants and plant products require it.

Step 3: Delivery and document check. The refrigerated truck reaches the cargo terminal on Thursday afternoon, ahead of the cut-off. At acceptance, staff check the documents against the booking: parties, routing, 30 pieces, weight, description, and the phytosanitary certificate, all in order.

Step 4: Physical check. The cartons are counted, 30, and checked against the stated weight and dimensions. The packaging is examined and found sound and suitable for build-up into a unit load; the markings agree with the air waybill.

Step 5: Screening and build-up. The consignment's security status is established through screening, and the cartons are held in the cold room. The ground handling agent builds the consignment into an aircraft pallet with other cargo for the same flight, securing and recording the unit.

Step 6: Acceptance recorded, flight, and delivery. The consignment flies that evening. In Amsterdam the unit load is broken down overnight, the importer is notified, customs formalities follow, and the cartons reach the market within the window on Friday.

What does this example demonstrate? Acceptance is a sequence, not a stamp: documents, physical condition, screening, and build-up each had to pass before the cartons could fly, and every step sat inside the cut-off so that the cold chain never broke.

Key lesson: perishable air freight succeeds backwards, from the market window to the harvest, with the acceptance cut-off as the pivot the whole plan must respect.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Chargeable weight on a quotation desk in Shenzhen
BODY:
A freight forwarder's quotation desk in Shenzhen handles two consignments for the same buyer in Accra, flying on the same service. All figures are illustrative for teaching purposes, and the IATA divisor of 6,000 applies.

Step 1: Consignment A, the dense carton. A manufacturer offers one carton of electric motor components, measuring 40 by 30 by 30 centimetres and weighing 18 kilograms. The volumetric weight is 40 multiplied by 30 multiplied by 30, which is 36,000 cubic centimetres, divided by 6,000: 6 kilograms. The actual weight of 18 kilograms is greater than the volumetric weight of 6 kilograms, so the chargeable weight is 18 kilograms. The carton is charged on what it weighs, because it exhausts the aircraft's payload, not its space.

Step 2: Consignment B, the bulky carton. A second manufacturer offers one carton of plastic householdware, measuring 60 by 50 by 40 centimetres and weighing 8 kilograms. The volumetric weight is 60 multiplied by 50 multiplied by 40, which is 120,000 cubic centimetres, divided by 6,000: 20 kilograms. This time the volumetric weight of 20 kilograms is greater than the actual weight of 8 kilograms, so the chargeable weight is 20 kilograms. The carton is charged on the space it occupies, because it exhausts the aircraft's volume long before its payload.

Step 3: The comparison. Consignment B weighs less than half of consignment A, yet its chargeable weight is higher: 20 chargeable kilograms against 18. Quoted by the same per-kilogram price for the flight, the lighter carton costs more to fly. The buyer's first reaction is disbelief; the explanation is the aircraft itself, which sells payload and volume together and charges whichever resource a consignment consumes more of.

Step 4: The desk's advice. The forwarder advises both manufacturers to state dimensions with every enquiry, and the bulky manufacturer to review its packaging: a carton redesigned to waste less space lowers its chargeable weight, because every cubic centimetre of air inside the packaging is billable volume.

What does this example demonstrate? Chargeable weight is the greater of actual and volumetric weight, and for light, bulky goods the dimensions, not the scales, decide the freight.

Key lesson: when you buy air freight, you are buying space as well as weight; measure your cartons before you price your shipment, because the airline certainly will.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Six drums refused: the acceptance check that catches a hidden dangerous good
BODY:
A coatings manufacturer near Tema receives an urgent order from an industrial buyer overseas for six steel drums of a solvent-based coating material, 120 kilograms in total. A colleague books the consignment with a forwarder as ordinary general cargo, described simply as coating material. All details are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: Delivery and the document check. The drums reach the cargo terminal at Kotoka International Airport in time for the booked flight. The acceptance officer compares the air waybill data against the booking: parties, routing, six pieces, the weight, and the description, coating material. Nothing on the documents mentions dangerous goods, and no dangerous goods declaration is attached.

Step 2: The physical check raises the question. Counting the pieces, the officer finds six sealed steel drums. Drums of liquid chemical are exactly the presentation that acceptance training flags: the description says coating, and solvent-based coatings are typically flammable liquids, which fall under Class 3 of the dangerous goods classes. The packaging carries no hazard labels and no marks of the performance packaging required for dangerous goods.

Step 3: The refusal. The officer asks for the product's safety data sheet, the manufacturer's document that classifies the product's hazards and is the starting point for deciding whether goods are regulated for transport. It confirms a flammable liquid. Carrying the consignment as general cargo, undeclared, unlabelled, and in non-performance packaging, is not permissible, and the acceptance check refuses the consignment. The drums do not fly, whatever the departure schedule says.

Step 4: The correct path. The manufacturer starts again, properly. The product is classified from its safety data sheet as a Class 3 dangerous good. A forwarder with trained dangerous goods staff is engaged, the airline is told at booking that dangerous goods are offered, the drums are packed in the required performance packaging, marked with the proper shipping name and UN number, and labelled with the Class 3 hazard label, and the shipper completes the dangerous goods declaration. At acceptance, staff qualified for dangerous goods check the declaration, packaging, marking, and labelling against the checklist, and the consignment is accepted to travel as what it is.

What does this example demonstrate? Acceptance exists to catch exactly this situation. The refusal was not the system failing; it was the system working, and the checks, documentary, physical, and specialist, combined to stop an undeclared hazard from entering an aircraft's hold.

Key lesson: the shipper decides the description, so the shipper owns the danger; classify products honestly before offering them for air carriage, and route regulated goods through trained hands.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Quote dimensions, not just weight
BODY:
The commonest expensive surprise in air freight is the shipper who prices a consignment on weight alone. The forwarder's quotation follows the chargeable weight, the greater of the actual and the volumetric weight, so a consignment of light, bulky goods can be quoted, booked, and then re-rated at the terminal when it is measured, leaving the shipper with a freight charge noticeably above the estimate the weight suggested.

The habit that prevents this costs nothing: state every package's length, width, and height, as well as its weight, in every enquiry and booking. The dimensions let the forwarder compute the volumetric weight, apply the applicable divisor, and quote the true chargeable weight before anyone commits. Irregularly shaped items occupy the space they measure at their greatest points, and palletised goods inherit the pallet's dimensions, so the outer envelope of the package is what the tape measure records.

Two corollaries are worth carrying with the habit.

1. Light goods are not cheap goods. In air freight the question is never only how heavy but how big. A consignment of plastic products can cost more to fly than a heavier consignment of steel parts, as this lesson's worked example showed.

2. Packaging design is freight-cost design. Air inside a carton is billable space. Reducing a bulky carton's dimensions, or reconfiguring packing so more product travels in less volume, lowers the chargeable weight directly.

When a quotation and an expectation diverge, ask which of the two weights drove the charge. The answer is almost always the volumetric one, and the tape measure explains what the scales never could.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: The paperwork closes before the aircraft does
BODY:
Passengers learn airport rhythms that do not transfer to cargo. A traveller can check in shortly before departure, and beginners reasonably imagine cargo boards the same way: deliver the goods to the airport an hour or two before the flight and all is well. The reality is the opposite, and it follows from the structure of this lesson. Cargo travels on passenger and freighter flights alike, but the paperwork and the operational preparation close first.

Consider what must happen between a consignment's arrival at the terminal and departure: the document check, the physical check, security screening, storage and, for perishables, cold storage, build-up of unit load devices, and loading to the aircraft's plan. Each step takes time, each is performed by different hands, and departure time is fixed. The acceptance cut-off is therefore set well before departure so the sequence fits inside it, and a consignment delivered after the cut-off has missed the flight even though the aircraft has not yet landed.

Missing a cut-off is not a fine and a smile at the desk. The consignment comes off the flight, and the forwarder must rebook it on a later departure, subject to space being available; on a busy lane the wait can stretch beyond the next scheduled service. For perishable goods the missed flight is not merely a delay but a deterioration risk, which is why perishable cut-offs are often the tightest of all.

The planning rule follows: work backwards from the cut-off, not from the departure time. Ask the forwarder for the acceptance deadline at booking, and treat that deadline, not the departure, as the moment the consignment must be ready, delivered, and documented.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Never sign a cargo description you cannot stand over
BODY:
The shipper's declaration is the foundation the entire acceptance process stands on. Every check at the terminal compares the goods against what the shipper stated: the description on the transport document, the weights, the dimensions, and, critically, the assertion, explicit or implied, that the consignment is what it claims to be and is not a regulated dangerous good travelling undeclared. When a shipper signs a description, the shipper, not the forwarder and not the airline, is the party that put those words into the documents.

The gravest form of failure is the one Worked Example 3 dramatised: dangerous goods offered as general cargo. An undeclared flammable liquid in an aircraft hold is a threat to the aircraft and everyone on it, and misdeclared dangerous goods are treated with corresponding seriousness wherever air cargo is handled. The honest, safe, and in the end quicker path is always classification first: consult the product's safety data sheet, and if the product is regulated, declare it, pack it, and label it as the dangerous goods requirements demand, through people trained to handle it.

The same integrity applies to ordinary descriptions. Guessed weights, estimated piece counts, or padded descriptions are discrepancies waiting to surface at acceptance, where the physical consignment is compared with the document, and afterwards in customs processing at the destination, where the declaration must match the goods. Lesson 3.3, Cargo Types, Packaging, and Handling, explained why cargo type drives handling; lesson 6.4, Document Verification and Common Discrepancies, showed what document errors cost once they are found.

The professional habit is short enough to memorise: weigh, measure, classify, describe, and only then sign. A description you cannot stand over is not paperwork; it is a liability travelling with the aircraft.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
An exporter in Accra asks a forwarder to quote for flying two cartons to the same buyer on the same flight. Carton A contains machine parts and weighs 18 kg, measuring 40 x 30 x 30 cm. Carton B contains plastic householdware and weighs 8 kg, measuring 60 x 50 x 40 cm. The volumetric divisor is 6,000.

Which statement best explains which carton attracts the higher air freight charge, and why?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Carton A attracts the higher charge, because air freight is always calculated on the actual gross weight shown on the air waybill, and 18 kg is more than double 8 kg.
2. Carton B attracts the higher charge, because its volumetric weight is 20 kg (120,000 divided by 6,000), which exceeds its actual weight of 8 kg, so its chargeable weight of 20 kg exceeds Carton A's chargeable weight of 18 kg.
3. Both cartons attract the same charge, because the airline adds the two actual weights together and prices the two cartons as one 26 kg consignment.
4. Carton B attracts the higher charge, because plastic goods are classified as special cargo and carry a surcharge on international flights.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Air freight is charged on chargeable weight, the greater of the actual gross weight and the volumetric weight, because an aircraft sells payload and volume together and each consignment pays for whichever resource it consumes more of. Carton A's volumetric weight is 40 x 30 x 30 divided by 6,000, which is 6 kg, so its actual weight of 18 kg governs and its chargeable weight is 18 kg. Carton B's volumetric weight is 60 x 50 x 40 divided by 6,000, which is 20 kg, so its volume governs and its chargeable weight is 20 kg. The lighter carton is charged more, which is exactly the surprise this lesson warns beginners about.

Answer 1 fails because air freight has never been charged on actual weight alone; volumetric weight exists precisely to price space. Answer 3 fails because each consignment's chargeable weight is assessed on the comparison between its own actual and volumetric weights, not by merging shipments into one weight. Answer 4 fails because plastic householdware is ordinary general cargo; the charge follows the dimensions, not the material.

Takeaway: in air freight the tape measure can outvote the scales; measure and weigh every package before pricing a shipment.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
A trading company delivers 15 cartons of general cargo to the airline's cargo terminal at 17:00 for an aircraft departing at 23:00 the same evening. The company's manager believes six hours is ample, but the terminal explains that the consignment cannot travel on that flight.

Which statement best explains the position?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The consignment cannot travel because general cargo is only carried on dedicated freighter aircraft, and the 23:00 departure is a passenger service.
2. The consignment cannot travel because acceptance for the flight closed earlier: cargo must be delivered, document-checked, screened, and built into unit load devices before the aircraft is loaded, so the operational clock closes well before departure.
3. The consignment cannot travel because airlines accept only consolidated loads containing many shippers' goods, and 15 cartons from one shipper is too small a booking.
4. The consignment will still travel if the company pays a late-delivery charge at the desk, since the aircraft's hold space is confirmed at booking.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The acceptance cut-off closes well before departure because a whole sequence must fit inside the time available: the document check, the physical check, security screening or verification of approved security status, storage, build-up of unit load devices, and loading to the aircraft's plan. The aircraft departs on schedule whether or not a late consignment has completed that sequence, so the paperwork and the load plan close first, and cargo can miss a flight that has not yet departed. The remedy is rebooking on a later departure, subject to space.

Answer 1 fails because cargo regularly travels in the holds of passenger aircraft; the flight type is not the obstacle. Answer 3 fails because small consignments fly routinely, often consolidated with others, and size is not the obstacle. Answer 4 fails because the cut-off is an operational deadline, not a payment threshold: no fee converts a consignment that missed acceptance, screening, and build-up into cargo that is safely loaded.

Takeaway: plan backwards from the acceptance cut-off, not from the departure time, and ask for the cut-off at the moment of booking.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A consignment is presented for carriage by air and passes through the checks performed before an airline will accept it. A colleague who has not studied air freight makes four statements about the process.

Which statement is NOT correct?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Acceptance includes checking that the number of pieces, the weight, and the dimensions agree with the air waybill and the booking.
2. Acceptance includes examining the packaging, marking, and labels for suitability for air carriage, including hazard labels for any dangerous goods.
3. Acceptance is finished as soon as the forwarder collects the goods from the shipper's premises, and the airline or its ground handling agent performs no checks of its own at the terminal.
4. Dangerous goods may be accepted only by trained and qualified staff who check the declaration, packaging, marking, and labelling against the applicable requirements.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

Acceptance is performed by the airline or its ground handling agent at the cargo terminal, and it is the gatekeeping function on which the safety, security, and document integrity of the flight depend. Collection from the shipper is only the delivery of the consignment to the start of the process; the document check, the physical check of pieces, weights, dimensions, packaging, marking, and labelling, security screening, and any dangerous goods acceptance all happen at or through the terminal afterwards. A forwarder's collection involves none of those checks.

Answer 1 is correct as a description of acceptance, because the physical consignment is compared against the documents the booking and chargeable weight were built on. Answer 2 is correct, because packaging suitability and correct marking and labelling are standard elements of the physical check. Answer 4 is correct, because dangerous goods acceptance is a specialist task reserved for trained, qualified staff, as this lesson taught and as Worked Example 3 showed.

Takeaway: acceptance is the airline's gate, checked at the terminal against documents and physical reality; nothing a forwarder does before delivery replaces it.
