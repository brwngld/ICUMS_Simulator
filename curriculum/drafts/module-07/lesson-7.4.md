=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Freight Rates, Shipping Charges, and Surcharges
BODY:
Every international shipment carries two loads: the cargo, and the charges that come with moving it. This lesson teaches the language of those charges and the practical skill of reading a freight bill from its first line to its last.

Three terms anchor everything that follows.

1. Freight rate. The freight rate is the price charged for transporting cargo according to an applicable pricing basis. It is a price per unit of transport: so much per container, per tonne, or per kilogram. The pricing basis, meaning what the rate is charged on, is the single most important fact about any rate, and the next concept examines it.

2. Shipping charges. Shipping charges are the total amounts payable for transporting the cargo and for the related services around it: handling, documentation, security, terminal work, delivery. The freight rate is one line among these charges, usually the largest, but never the only one.

3. Freight surcharge. A freight surcharge is an additional charge applied to a freight service to account for specified costs or operating conditions, such as the price of fuel or the pressure of a busy season. Surcharges are stated separately from the base rate precisely because they can change when the underlying cost or condition changes.

Beginners read a freight quotation down to the base rate and stop. Professionals read it as three layers: the base freight, the principal transportation charge before separately stated surcharges or additional services; the surcharges attached to the main carriage; and the local service charges raised at the origin and the destination. The whole of this lesson builds the habit of seeing all three layers, because the gap between the first layer and the last is where costly surprises live.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The Pricing Basis: What the Rate Is Charged On
BODY:
A freight rate is never a single sum invented for one shipment. It is a price per unit, and the unit, the pricing basis, is stated or implied in every quotation and every tariff. Three pricing bases dominate international freight.

1. Per container, for FCL ocean cargo. Lesson 7.3, Containerisation: FCL, LCL, and Container Types, explained that a full container load is the exclusive use of a box by one shipper. Ocean rates for such containerised cargo are commonly quoted per container: one price for the movement of the box, whether the shipper has filled it or not. A twenty-foot container and a forty-foot container are different pieces of equipment, so they are priced as different items; the larger box is priced higher than the smaller, typically well below double, because it is still one unit lifted once. The TEU and FEU counting units taught in Lesson 7.3 are the industry's currency for this kind of pricing.

2. Per weight or measurement tonne, for LCL ocean cargo. A less than container load pays a share of a box, and the share is rated by weight or measurement (W/M), a freight rating method in which charges are calculated using weight or volume, generally whichever produces the higher charge under the applicable tariff. Weight is taken in metric tonnes and measurement in cubic metres; the greater of the two becomes the quantity charged, a unit often called the revenue tonne. Light, bulky cargo therefore pays on space, and dense cargo pays on weight.

3. Per kilogram of chargeable weight, for air cargo. Air freight is charged on chargeable weight, the greater of the actual gross weight and the volumetric weight, the volumetric weight being a calculated weight based on cargo dimensions and an applicable volume-to-weight conversion factor, as Lesson 7.2, Air Cargo Operations and Cargo Acceptance, taught in full.

The same consignment of goods can carry three different price tags on three different bases, which is why the pricing basis must always be identified before any comparison is made.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Base Freight: The Principal Transportation Charge
BODY:
Base freight is the principal transportation charge before separately stated surcharges or additional services. It is the price of the main carriage itself: the vessel's or the aircraft's work of carrying the cargo across the ocean or along the air lane. On the sea leg, the base line-haul charge is commonly called the ocean freight charge, the charge for transporting cargo by sea under the applicable freight arrangement.

How are base rates established? Lesson 7.1, Ocean Freight Operations and Vessel Schedules, described liner shipping: scheduled services on fixed routes selling space by the container. Liner base rates emerge from published tariffs, from contracts between carriers and large shippers, and from continuous market negotiation in between. There is no universal price list; each carrier prices each trade lane according to its own costs and commercial position, which is why the same container can attract different base offers from different lines in the same week.

What does the base rate reflect? Several influences are visible even before surcharges are considered.

1. The trade lane. A route with heavy competition and abundant capacity is priced differently from a thin route served by few services. Distance matters, but the balance of trade on the lane often matters more.

2. The equipment. A twenty-foot box, a forty-foot box, a high cube and a reefer are priced as different items, and specialised equipment carries a different base from a dry box.

3. The service pattern. A direct sailing and a routing with a transhipment are different products, and the base rate reflects the difference.

4. The cargo itself. For cargo rated by weight or measurement, the consignment's own figures decide the quantity charged.

The base rate, in other words, is the foundation of the bill but not the whole building. The next two concepts add the storeys above it: surcharges, and the local charges at each end.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: What Moves Freight Rates
BODY:
Freight rates are prices, and prices move. Understanding the main forces behind the movement turns a freight quotation from a mystery into a readable market signal.

1. Fuel costs. Vessels and aircraft burn fuel by the tonne, and fuel is one of the largest costs of the main carriage. When fuel prices rise sharply, carriers do not usually rewrite every base rate; they recover the movement through fuel surcharges, as the next concept explains.

2. Exchange rates. Carriers earn in one currency and pay in others, so exchange-rate movements feed into pricing on international lanes, again often through a stated adjustment.

3. Capacity and demand on the lane. When trade on a route is booming and sailings are full, rates rise; when capacity exceeds cargo, they soften. Seasonality drives this rhythm: harvest exports, festive retail peaks and other recurring surges create predictable pressure periods, and a peak-season surcharge is one of the ways carriers manage them.

4. Route and service conditions. Congestion at a major port, security conditions on the route, and the choice between a direct service and one with a transhipment all shape what a lane costs to serve.

5. The cargo and its equipment. Weight, volume, stowage characteristics, the need for a reefer or other special equipment, and the extra work that dangerous goods require all influence the price calculated for a specific shipment.

Two lessons follow for the practitioner. First, a rate quoted today is a snapshot of these forces today; the forces keep moving, which is why quotations carry validity periods, a point this lesson returns to. Second, because many of the forces act through surcharges rather than the base rate, a shipper who watches only headline base movements reads only half the market. No amounts are quoted in this lesson, and none can be: the figures change continuously, and every carrier's tariff is its own.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Surcharges: Why the Bill Has Extras
BODY:
A freight surcharge is an additional charge applied to a freight service to account for specified costs or operating conditions. The logic is simple: the base rate is relatively stable between reviews, while the costs behind it, fuel, currency, seasonal pressure, swing from month to month. Rather than rebuilding the whole tariff every time conditions move, carriers attach separately stated surcharges to the main carriage and adjust those instead. The surcharges below are taught as concepts; this course quotes no amounts for them, because each carrier's surcharges are tariff items of its own and they change continuously.

1. Bunker Adjustment Factor (BAF). The bunker adjustment factor is a maritime freight adjustment associated with changes in fuel costs, where the carrier's pricing structure provides for it. Bunker is the traditional word for a ship's fuel. A BAF rises and falls with the cost of bunkers, and many carriers now express the calculation through index clauses published with the rate, so the surcharge moves by stated rule rather than by whim.

2. Currency Adjustment Factor (CAF). The currency adjustment factor is a freight adjustment associated with exchange-rate fluctuations, where applicable. It compensates the carrier for the gap between the currency it earns in and the currencies it pays in.

3. Peak-season and congestion surcharges. A peak-season surcharge applies during recurring high-demand periods on a lane; a congestion surcharge recovers the cost of delays and crowding at a busy port. Both are tied to operating conditions, not to the cargo itself.

4. The general rate increase (GRI). The GRI is the liner industry's instrument for lifting base rates themselves: an announced, across-the-board upward adjustment of base freight rates on a trade lane, typically timed for contract seasons or capacity management. It is distinct from ad-hoc surcharges, because a GRI changes the foundation, the base rate, rather than adding a line above it.

The practitioner's rule follows from this anatomy: a surcharge attached to the carriage can change between quotation and shipment, so no surcharge figure should ever be assumed to have frozen.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Local Charges: The Long Tail at Origin and Destination
BODY:
Beside the main carriage and its surcharges sits the third layer: local service charges, raised at the ports, terminals, airports and offices through which the shipment passes. These are the charges beginners overlook and freight bills never forget. The commonest are the following.

1. Terminal Handling Charge (THC). The terminal handling charge is a charge associated with specified cargo or container handling activities at a terminal. In container shipping a THC is raised at each end: the origin THC covers the lifting work between road chassis, stacking position and vessel at the port of loading, and the destination THC covers the mirror-image work after discharge. They are separate charges from separate facilities, and a quotation that includes only one of them tells half a story.

2. Documentation fee. The documentation fee is a charge for preparing, issuing or processing transportation documents, most familiarly the issuance of the bill of lading.

3. Seal charge. The container seal taught in Lesson 7.3, the numbered security device fixed through the closed doors, is itself billed as a small line item.

4. ISPS security fee. Maritime and port security measures are required by international convention, under the International Ship and Port Facility Security Code within the framework of the Safety of Life at Sea Convention, and carriers and terminals recover their cost through a security charge on the cargo.

5. CFS charges. For LCL cargo, the container freight station work described in Lesson 7.3, receiving consignments, stuffing them into the consolidation box, and later deconsolidating them at destination, is billed as CFS charges at both ends.

6. Collection and delivery charges. The road carriage that brings cargo to the port or airport of loading, or takes it from the port or airport of discharge to the premises, is a service of its own, charged separately unless a door-to-door scope bundles it in.

The practical lesson is arithmetic: door-to-door cost equals the freight plus a long tail of local charges, and the tail is not trivial. Destination charges are also commonly payable by the consignee, in the local currency at the far end, which is exactly why an origin-side port-to-port quotation can look strikingly small next to what the shipment actually costs to land at the door.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Prepaid and Collect: Who Pays, and Where
BODY:
Every freight bill must be paid by someone, at some end. The two designations that record this are among the most consequential markings on a transport document.

1. Freight prepaid. A freight prepaid designation indicates that the relevant freight charges are payable at or before shipment. In practice the shipper, or another party on the origin side, settles the freight before the goods sail, and the transport document is marked to show it.

2. Freight collect. A freight collect designation indicates that the freight charges are payable at destination, or by the designated receiving party, under the agreed arrangement. The consignee at the far end settles the charges as a condition of taking delivery.

Which arrangement applies is a commercial decision, and it usually follows the sales contract. Lesson 4.3, Incoterms and Allocation of Responsibilities, explained that under Incoterms 2020 the E and F rules place the arrangement of the main carriage with the buyer, while the C and D rules place it with the seller. Where the seller arranges the carriage, freight is commonly prepaid; where the buyer arranges it, the movement is often booked on collect terms so that charges are recovered at the destination end. The parties can agree otherwise, so the marking on the document, not the assumption, is the authority.

The operational consequences are real. On collect terms the consignee must be ready to pay the freight, and commonly the destination local charges as well, promptly on arrival, because carriers release cargo against settlement. An exporter who ships collect to a consignee who is slow to pay, or unreachable, has engineered a delay for cargo it no longer controls. Prepaid or collect is therefore verified before booking, marked consistently on the documents, and communicated to everyone in the chain.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Reading a Freight Quotation and a Freight Invoice
BODY:
Lesson 5.2, Freight Quotations and Shipment Booking, taught how quotations are requested, structured and compared. This lesson adds the reading skill that turns that knowledge into cost control: knowing what each part of the document is telling you. A freight quotation or shipping invoice, however it is laid out, contains the same anatomy.

1. The parties and the shipment reference. Who is quoting, for whom, and for which movement: places, equipment, cargo description.

2. The scope of service. Port to port, port to door, or door to door. The scope is the boundary of the price; everything outside it is an exclusion, however small the print.

3. The pricing basis and quantity. One twenty-foot container, a number of revenue tonnes under W/M, or a chargeable weight in kilograms. This line lets you verify how the price was built.

4. The base freight line. The principal transportation charge, on the stated basis.

5. The surcharge lines. Fuel, security and their fellows, each stated separately.

6. The origin local charges. Terminal handling, documentation, seal and origin handling within scope.

7. The destination charges. Often the most important lines, because they are frequently shown as excluded, or as payable locally by the consignee.

8. The conditions. Currency, payment basis, prepaid or collect, and the validity date, the date until which the quoted prices hold.

Two key terms complete the anatomy. An all-in freight rate is a quoted transportation rate that includes the charges expressly covered by the quotation, which may exclude other services or destination expenses; all-in, in other words, means read the list of what is included, never assume that nothing else can be charged. A freight invoice is a document issued to request payment for transportation and associated services; it is the quotation's post-shipment counterpart, and the professional habit is to reconcile each invoice line against the quotation and the booking before paying, querying any line that was never quoted.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Air Freight Charges in Parallel
BODY:
Everything this lesson has taught about layers applies unchanged in the air. The names differ; the anatomy does not.

1. The air freight charge. The air freight charge is the charge for transporting cargo by aircraft, calculated using the applicable chargeable weight and the tariff or agreed rate. Lesson 7.2 taught chargeable weight: the greater of the gross weight and the volumetric weight, with volumetric weight computed from dimensions, under the IATA standard by dividing length times width times height in centimetres by 6,000. The pricing basis is therefore kilograms of chargeable weight, and the base freight line is that weight multiplied by the applicable rate.

2. Air surcharges. Airlines attach fuel surcharges, responding to the cost of jet fuel, and security surcharges, recovering the cost of screening and security regimes, to the carriage. Both are commonly stated per kilogram, so they scale with the chargeable weight exactly as the base does.

3. Air local charges. Airports have their own tail: cargo terminal charges for handling and screening at the origin airport, mirror-image terminal charges at the destination, documentation and air waybill fees, and collection or delivery carriage at either end.

The air waybill itself usually serves the freight-bill function on the air leg: Lesson 7.2 described the AWB as receipt for the goods, evidence of the contract of carriage and freight bill, and its charges often appear on its face. Where a forwarder quotes an all-in price per kilogram, the same caution as at sea applies: an all-in rate covers only the charges expressly listed, so the list is read before the price is believed.

The transferable conclusion deserves stating plainly. Whatever the mode, the total cost of a shipment is the main carriage, plus the surcharges attached to it, plus the local charges at the ends. A learner who can read that structure on an ocean bill can read it on an air bill.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Doing the Arithmetic: Simple Freight Calculations
BODY:
Freight arithmetic rewards method over speed. All figures in this concept are illustrative for teaching purposes only. Three model calculations cover the common cases.

1. FCL ocean freight. Multiply the base rate per container by the number of containers, then add surcharges and local charges. Example: two twenty-foot containers at a base of 1,200 US dollars each give a base freight of 2,400; adding surcharges of 350 and origin local charges of 300 gives an origin-side total of 3,050 US dollars. Destination charges, if excluded, are added separately to see the door cost.

2. LCL freight under W/M. Convert the consignment to both units: weight in metric tonnes, volume in cubic metres. A consignment of 2,400 kilograms and 6.8 cubic metres is 2.4 tonnes against 6.8 cubic metres; W/M charges the higher, so the chargeable quantity is 6.8 revenue tonnes. At an illustrative rate of 85 US dollars per revenue tonne, the freight is 6.8 multiplied by 85, which is 578 US dollars, before CFS and other local charges are added.

3. Air freight. Establish the chargeable weight, multiply by the rate per kilogram, then add per-kilogram surcharges and flat local charges. A consignment of 40 chargeable kilograms at 4 US dollars per kilogram carries a base freight of 160; surcharges of 48 and local charges of 112 lift the bill to 320 US dollars, a structure the final worked example examines in full.

The method is always the same four steps.

1. Identify the pricing basis: per container, per revenue tonne, or per chargeable kilogram.

2. Compute the quantity: the containers, the greater of weight and measurement, or the greater of gross and volumetric weight.

3. Multiply the quantity by the base rate.

4. Add each surcharge and local charge in its turn, and state the currency.

Perform the steps in order and the total explains itself, line by line, exactly as a freight invoice should.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Reading the Freight Invoice: One Twenty-Foot Container from Tema
BODY:
A processor of cocoa products in Tema exports a twenty-foot container of cocoa butter in cartons to a buyer in Hamburg, booked with a forwarder on a liner service as a full container load, port to port, freight prepaid. When the vessel sails, the forwarder's freight invoice arrives. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: Confirm the frame. The invoice names the parties, the one twenty-foot container, the route from Tema to Hamburg, and the scope: port to port. The pricing basis is per container, so no weight or volume arithmetic applies. The payment basis reads freight prepaid.

Step 2: Read the main-carriage lines. The base ocean freight appears first: 1,150 US dollars for the twenty-foot container. Below it stands the bunker adjustment factor, the fuel-related surcharge: 180 US dollars. Main carriage subtotal: 1,330 US dollars.

Step 3: Read the origin local lines. The origin terminal handling charge appears next at 150 US dollars, then the documentation fee for issuance of the bill of lading at 60, the container seal at 15, and the ISPS security fee at 25. The origin locals total 250 US dollars.

Step 4: Reconcile and total. The exporter checks each line against the quotation accepted weeks earlier: the base matches, the BAF figure is within the terms quoted, the documentation fee covers one bill of lading, and no unexplained lines appear. The invoice total is 1,580 US dollars, marked freight prepaid, settled at origin.

Step 5: Add the lines that are not on the invoice. Because the scope was port to port, the destination work is absent. At Hamburg the buyer's agent pays the destination terminal handling charge of 220 US dollars and delivery to the buyer's warehouse of 190, a further 410 US dollars settled locally. The true door-to-door cost of the movement is therefore 1,580 plus 410: 1,990 US dollars.

What does this example demonstrate?

A freight invoice is read as three layers: base freight, surcharges, and local charges. It also covers only the scope booked, so the invoice that looked like the whole cost was, on a port-to-port movement, only the origin-side part.

Key lesson: reconcile every line against the quotation before paying, and always add the destination tail before calling a shipment cheap or dear.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Two Quotations, One Container: Comparing Scope in Hamburg
BODY:
A confectionery manufacturer in Hamburg buys a twenty-foot container of cocoa products from a processor in Tema. The agreed commercial terms make the buyer responsible for arranging the main carriage, so the manufacturer's logistics desk invites two forwarders to quote for moving one twenty-foot container from Tema to the company's warehouse in Hamburg. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: Quote A, port to port. Forwarder A quotes 1,575 US dollars: base ocean freight 1,180, bunker adjustment factor 175, origin terminal handling charge 150, documentation fee 50, and container seal 20. The quotation is valid for seven days, and its conditions list the exclusions: destination terminal handling, assistance with import clearance, and delivery to the warehouse, all payable locally in Hamburg by the consignee.

Step 2: Quote B, door to door. Forwarder B quotes 2,040 US dollars for the same box on comparable sailing options: base ocean freight 1,150, bunker adjustment factor 175, origin local charges 210 covering terminal handling, documentation and seal, destination terminal handling charge 230, assistance with import clearance 90, and delivery to the warehouse 185. The quotation is valid for fourteen days.

Step 3: Rebuild to equal scope. The desk adds to Quote A the very charges it excludes, taking the figures from Quote B's stated lines: destination terminal handling 230, clearance assistance 90, delivery 185, a destination tail of 505. Quote A's like-for-like door-to-door cost is therefore 1,575 plus 505: 2,080 US dollars. Against Quote B's 2,040, the dearer-looking quotation is in fact 40 US dollars cheaper on equal scope, and it carries twice the validity period.

Step 4: Decide. The desk recommends Quote B: the lower equal-scope total, one responsible party for the whole movement, and a validity period long enough for the production plan behind the order.

What does this example demonstrate?

Headline totals built on different scopes cannot be compared. The comparison only means something when both quotations are rebuilt to the same scope, and the charges one quotation pushes to the destination end are real money however they are labelled.

Key lesson: compare like with like, first the total scope, then the price, then the conditions.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: The Air Bill That Doubled: A Bulky Carton from Accra
BODY:
An exporter of hand-woven baskets and other home decoration items in Accra flies one large carton to a retail buyer in London, departing from Kotoka International Airport. The carton measures 80 by 60 by 50 centimetres and weighs 26 kilograms. The exporter, thinking in kilograms, expects a modest bill. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: Establish the chargeable weight. Applying the rule Lesson 7.2 taught, the volumetric weight is 80 multiplied by 60 multiplied by 50, which is 240,000, divided by the IATA divisor of 6,000: 40 kilograms. The gross weight is 26 kilograms. Chargeable weight is the greater of the two: 40 kilograms. The carton will pay for the space it occupies, not for what it weighs.

Step 2: Compute the base freight. The agreed rate is 4 US dollars per chargeable kilogram, so the base air freight charge is 40 multiplied by 4: 160 US dollars. Even this correct figure already exceeds the exporter's mental estimate, which had multiplied the real weight of 26 kilograms by the rate.

Step 3: Add the surcharges. The fuel surcharge of 0.80 US dollars per kilogram adds 32; the security surcharge of 0.40 per kilogram adds 16. The surcharges together add 48 US dollars, all scaling with the chargeable weight.

Step 4: Add the local charges. Origin terminal and screening charges at Accra add 40; the air waybill and documentation fee adds 24; at London, destination terminal charges add 28 and delivery to the buyer's premises adds 20. The local tail totals 112 US dollars.

Step 5: Total the bill. Base 160, surcharges 48, locals 112: the freight bill comes to 320 US dollars. The charges around the carriage, 160 US dollars of them, exactly match the carriage itself, and the total is roughly three times the exporter's first estimate.

What does this example demonstrate?

In air freight the base rate is only the floor of the bill. The chargeable weight rule raised the quantity charged from 26 to 40 kilograms, and the surcharges and terminal charges then doubled the apparent base rate.

Key lesson: budget the bill, not the rate, and give the forwarder exact dimensions and weight so the true total is quoted before anyone commits.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: A Low Headline Rate Is Not a Cheap Shipment
BODY:
The most expensive reading error in freight is stopping at the headline. A port-to-port quotation from which every local charge is excluded will almost always look cheaper than a door-to-door quotation that includes them, and the difference between the two totals is not saving; it is scope. Remember the definition of the all-in freight rate: it includes the charges expressly covered by the quotation, which may exclude other services or destination expenses. All-in promises a list, not an empty space for extras.

The discipline that prevents the error is rebuilding. Before two quotations are compared, each is taken down to its lines and rebuilt to the same scope: origin locals, main carriage, surcharges, destination locals, delivery. Only totals at equal scope are comparable, as the Hamburg example demonstrated, and only after the conditions, validity, currency and transit time, have also been read.

One asymmetry deserves particular care in Ghanaian trade and everywhere else: destination charges are commonly payable by the consignee at the far end. A low headline rate on the export side can quietly hand a large tail to the buyer, and an importer who accepts a collect arrangement without first pricing the destination end has bought a surprise at the port. Whether you are the shipper or the consignee, the question to ask of any quotation is not whether the number is low, but what is not on it.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Surcharges Move: A Quotation Lives Only Until Its Validity Ends
BODY:
This lesson has repeatedly said that no surcharge figure can be assumed to hold. The reason is structural. The bunker adjustment factor moves with fuel, the currency adjustment factor with exchange rates, congestion and peak-season surcharges with operating conditions, and a general rate increase can lift the base rate of a whole trade lane at a stroke. The charges are designed to move; that is their purpose.

This is why the freight validity period taught in Lesson 5.2, Freight Quotations and Shipment Booking, matters so much in this lesson's world. A quotation holds only until its stated validity date, and the surcharges finally invoiced are those applicable when the shipment actually sails, not those printed on a document that has expired. Three habits convert this from a warning into a procedure.

1. Accept within validity. If the booking decision slips past the validity date, ask for the prices to be reconfirmed before instructing anyone to proceed.

2. Reconfirm after change. If the shipping date moves, or a carrier announces a rate adjustment on the lane, revalidate the quotation rather than hoping it still stands.

3. Reconcile at invoice. Check the invoiced surcharge lines against the quotation and the booking, and query promptly any line that was never quoted.

A quotation figure is a fact about a date. Carried beyond that date into a sales price or a budget without reconfirmation, it becomes the quietest kind of cost overrun.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: What a Freight Bill Does Not Include
BODY:
A freight bill, however long, is not a list of everything a shipment will ever cost, and the verification habit this lesson builds includes knowing what to look for elsewhere.

Customs duties and taxes never appear on a freight invoice, because they are statutory charges assessed by customs authorities, not prices charged by carriers or forwarders; the import charges due at the destination are the importer's separate business under the agreed commercial terms. Cargo insurance is likewise a separate service, arranged and charged separately unless a specific quotation expressly includes it. And one sentence this lesson owes to completeness: demurrage, detention and storage are separate charge families that begin when free time ends, and Lesson 9.4, Demurrage, Detention, and Storage Charges, treats them fully.

The practical conclusion is the one this lesson closes on. Before confirming any transportation arrangement, confirm three things in writing: the scope, so the exclusions are visible; the validity, so the prices are alive; and the exclusions, so the tail is expected. After the shipment, reconcile the freight invoice against the quotation and the booking before paying it, line by line, querying whatever was never quoted. A learner who can define a freight rate, name the three layers of a freight bill, mark a document prepaid or collect knowingly, and rebuild two quotations to equal scope already reads freight documents more carefully than the common mistakes of this trade assume.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
An importer in Takoradi is arranging shipment of machine parts from Shanghai. Forwarder A quotes 2,600 US dollars, port to port, and lists destination terminal handling, assistance with customs clearance, and delivery to the warehouse as excluded charges payable locally at destination. Forwarder B quotes 2,930 US dollars, door to door, including all of those charges. A colleague says Forwarder A is 330 US dollars cheaper and should be chosen immediately.

Which option best explains the correct way to compare the two quotations?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Forwarder A is cheaper, because the base freight and surcharges are the true cost of shipping, and local charges at destination are minor extras that never change the comparison.
2. The quotations cannot be compared at all, because every forwarder is free to quote whatever total it wishes and no common basis exists between offers.
3. Both quotations must first be rebuilt to the same scope by adding Forwarder A's excluded destination charges to its total, because the cheaper headline may cost more once the shipment actually reaches the door.
4. Forwarder B should be chosen automatically, because a door-to-door quotation is always better value than a port-to-port quotation regardless of the figures.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The two quotations are built on different scopes: Forwarder A prices the main carriage and the origin charges only, while Forwarder B prices the whole movement including the destination tail. Until A's excluded destination charges are added to its total, the 330 US dollar difference measures scope, not value. Rebuilding both quotations to equal scope, exactly as the Hamburg worked example demonstrated, is the only comparison that means anything; Forwarder A may still prove cheaper after the rebuild, but nobody can know before it is done.

Answer 1 dismisses destination charges as minor extras, which is precisely the misconception this lesson dismantles: the local tail can rival the main carriage itself. Answer 2 abandons comparison altogether and forfeits the discipline of reading quotations line by line that Lessons 5.2 and 7.4 both teach. Answer 4 commits the mirror-image error, assuming that inclusiveness guarantees value; a door-to-door quotation can still be the dearer buy once both are rebuilt to equal scope.

The takeaway: compare totals at equal scope first, then weigh price, validity and service conditions.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
A forwarder in Accra is rating an LCL consignment of plastic household goods for ocean shipment. The consignment weighs 2,400 kilograms, which is 2.4 tonnes, and measures 6.8 cubic metres. The LCL rate is quoted per weight or measurement tonne, whichever produces the higher charge.

Which statement best describes how the consignment will be charged?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. On the weight of 2.4 tonnes, because LCL cargo is always charged on its actual weight regardless of the space it occupies.
2. On the measurement of 6.8, because under weight or measurement rating the charge follows whichever of the weight in tonnes and the volume in cubic metres produces the higher charge.
3. On the total of 9.2, because the weight and the measurement are added together before the rate is applied.
4. On the number of packages, because consolidated cargo is charged per pallet rather than by weight or by volume.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Weight or measurement rating exists because a shipper's consignment can exhaust either the vessel's weight allowance or its space, so the tariff charges the greater of the two. The consignment is converted to both units, 2.4 tonnes against 6.8 cubic metres, and the higher figure, 6.8 revenue tonnes, becomes the quantity to which the LCL rate is applied. Light, bulky cargo such as plastic household goods is the classic case that pays on measurement, not weight.

Answer 1 assumes weight always governs, which would undercharge exactly the cargo that fills a box without weighing much; weight governs only when it is the greater figure. Answer 3 misreads the rule by adding the two measures together; they are alternatives, never combined, and adding them would charge the same cubic metre or kilogram twice. Answer 4 invents a per-package basis; consolidation rates are built on the consignment's weight or measurement, not on a pallet count.

The takeaway: under W/M, compute both the tonnage and the cubic metres, charge the higher, and never assume which one will win before the figures are converted.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A trading company in Accra ships a consignment of shea butter to a buyer in Dakar on freight collect terms. Before the goods sail, the logistics officer wants to be certain what the marking on the transport document means for payment.

Which statement best describes a freight collect arrangement?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The freight charges are payable at destination, normally by the designated receiving party, who is expected to settle them before the cargo is released.
2. The freight charges are payable at or before shipment in Accra, and the transport document would instead carry the marking freight prepaid.
3. The freight charges may be settled by the consignee at any convenient time after taking delivery of the goods.
4. The freight charges are included in the price of the goods, so neither party pays the carrier separately.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

A freight collect designation indicates that the freight charges are payable at destination, or by the designated receiving party, under the agreed arrangement. Carriers release cargo against settlement, so the consignee in Dakar must be ready to pay the freight, and commonly the destination local charges as well, promptly on arrival. This is why a shipper confirms the collect arrangement with the consignee before booking: a slow or unreachable receiving party turns into a delayed delivery of cargo the shipper no longer controls.

Answer 2 describes freight prepaid, the opposite designation, under which charges are settled at or before shipment on the origin side. Answer 3 mistakes collect for open credit after delivery; the release of the cargo is precisely what awaits settlement, so payment does not trail delivery at the consignee's leisure. Answer 4 confuses the payment arrangement with the pricing of the goods; whether freight is built into the sale price is a matter for the sales contract and the agreed Incoterms 2020 rule, and it is separate from who pays the carrier and when.

The takeaway: prepaid and collect decide who pays and at which end, and the marking is verified, not assumed.
