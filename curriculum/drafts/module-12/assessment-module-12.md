=== ASSESSMENT Q1 | tests lesson 12.1 ===
QUESTION:
A regional study compares two international corridors serving the same landlocked market from different gateway ports. The two corridors' roads are of similar quality and their facilities — yards, rest points, terminals — are comparable, yet door-to-door transit times on one corridor are reliably shorter, and its crossing times far more predictable. The auditors find little to choose between the physical infrastructure.

Which statement best explains the difference in performance?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The faster corridor must simply be shorter on the map: distance is the decisive factor in corridor performance, and the auditors' methods cannot have controlled for it properly.
2. The difference must lie in pavement quality after all: road condition is the only real determinant of how fast cargo moves, so the study's finding of physical parity has to be mistaken.
3. The difference lies in the procedural layer: the faster corridor's transit documents, seals, guarantees, and agreed checks between neighbouring administrations let trucks cross with less duplicated work, and procedures are corridor infrastructure as real as pavement.
4. The faster corridor's carriers must simply employ better drivers: operator skill, not network design, is what separates reliable corridors from unreliable ones.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

A transport corridor is a defined network of routes, border crossings, facilities, and the procedures and agreements that move goods legally across the borders — and the lesson is explicit that two corridors with identical roads can perform differently for procedural reasons alone. The procedural layer — the transit regime, the single transit document, the accepted seal and guarantee, the arrangements by which neighbouring offices recognise one another's work — decides how much duplicated work each truck suffers at every crossing. Where one corridor's offices process compliant trucks on a document and seal check while the other's repeat examinations and paperwork, the difference lands in every door-to-door transit time on the route, whatever the pavement is like.

Answer 1 retreats to map distance, the beginner's measure the lesson's routing example dismantles: distance is one input to driving time, and driving time is only part of the journey. Answer 2 treats pavement as the only real determinant; a finding of physical parity with diverging performance is precisely the signature of a procedural cause, not evidence that the audit missed the asphalt. Answer 4 blames the fleets, but drivers on both corridors face the same control points, and no amount of driving skill removes standing time that procedures create.

The takeaway: the corridor is one system of physical and procedural layers, and where the physical layers match, the procedures are where the difference lives.

=== ASSESSMENT Q2 | tests lesson 12.1 ===
QUESTION:
A corridor authority has funds for a single improvement on an international corridor. A study of door-to-door journeys finds that the largest share of travel time is standing time at one heavily worked border crossing, while the pavement along the route is in reasonable condition and the route's facilities are adequate. Four proposals are on the table.

Which should the authority expect to improve door-to-door transit time most?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The border package: align the crossing's operating hours with traffic, exchange pre-arrival data so the office can prepare its decisions, and agree joint processing with the neighbouring administration, because the corridor is only as fast as its slowest border and investment buys the whole route only at the bottleneck.
2. Repave the corridor's best-paved section, because surface quality is the input that sets transit time, and the works will shorten every journey on the route.
3. Build a new weighbridge at the midpoint of the route, because overloaded vehicles are the main reason trucks queue at borders, so enforcement there will shorten the crossing.
4. Add truck rest points along the route, because driver fatigue is the largest single cause of lost hours on international corridors.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

The lesson's maxim — a corridor is only as fast as its slowest border — makes the bottleneck the target of investment. The study has found where the hours sit: the largest share of door-to-door time is standing time at one crossing. The border package attacks exactly that point. Aligned operating hours remove the closed windows that hold movements; pre-arrival data lets the office risk-assess and prepare before the truck arrives; agreed joint processing removes duplicated examinations. Improvement bought at the bottleneck is felt across the whole route, because every consignment passes through it.

Answer 2 repaves a section already in reasonable condition: the lesson is direct that improving a section that is already quick buys almost nothing, because it shrinks the smaller term of the journey while the larger term stands still. Answer 3 adds a control point to a corridor whose problem is its control points; at best it is neutral for through traffic, and its rationale — that overloading is the main cause of border queues — is not what the study found. Answer 4 serves driver welfare and legal pacing, which matter, but rest points do not govern where the journey's hours concentrate.

The takeaway: audit where the hours actually sit, then spend at the slowest control point — which may be procedure rather than pavement.

=== ASSESSMENT Q3 | tests lesson 12.1 ===
QUESTION:
At a land crossing on a busy regional corridor, every arriving truck is physically examined, because there is no way of telling in advance which movements deserve attention. Queues stretch through the working day, and compliant hauliers cannot predict their crossing times from one week to the next. A working group is asked which trade-facilitation measure is designed for exactly this friction.

Which option names it?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Raise the crossing's fees and build more examination bays, so that more trucks can be examined at the same time and the queue shortens.
2. Open the crossing around the clock, so that arriving trucks can be examined at whatever hour they reach the post.
3. Establish a joint border post in which both countries' agencies process each truck at one stop, so that every vehicle stops once instead of twice.
4. Introduce risk management: assess arriving consignments and channel them by risk, releasing compliant, low-risk trucks on their documents with little or no physical inspection and concentrating examination where the risk sits.
CORRECT: 4
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 4.

The friction described is undifferentiated physical control: every truck is examined because arriving traffic is not assessed. The trade-facilitation remedy designed for exactly this is risk management — customs administrations channel consignments by risk, releasing compliant, low-risk traffic on their documents with little or no physical inspection and concentrating attention where the risk sits. Differentiated checking is what makes fast movement possible at all; universal inspection freezes a corridor. The predictable queues and unpredictable crossing times in the stem are that freeze, felt daily by every compliant haulier.

The distractors are real remedies pointed at different frictions. Answer 1 buys more capacity for the same undifferentiated control, so the freeze persists at a larger scale — more bays examining everyone is still everyone examined. Answer 2 addresses hours of operation, the remedy for crossings whose closed windows hold movements that arrive in the gap; hours are not this crossing's disease, since the examination burden follows the truck at whatever hour it arrives. Answer 3, the joint border post, is the remedy for duplicate processing — one stop instead of two — but the stem describes one examination repeated on every truck, not one truck processed twice.

The takeaway: match the remedy to the friction — undifferentiated control is cured by differentiated control, not by more capacity, longer hours, or a shared building.

=== ASSESSMENT Q4 | tests lesson 12.2 ===
QUESTION:
A new colleague at a haulage company asks why a coastal state's customs releases containers to cross its territory toward a landlocked neighbour without collecting any duty at the port where the goods enter.

Which statement best explains the arrangement?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Transit cargo is exempt from customs law altogether: goods in transit pass unwatched, and a state that stopped them for checks would breach the regional transit arrangement.
2. The movement is secured, not unwatched: the goods travel under customs control on a single transit document, sealed, with a guarantee standing behind the duties at risk until the transit is discharged — borrowed trust that lets the transit state serve a neighbour's cargo while its treasury stays protected.
3. The goods legally belong to the transit country until they reach the border of the destination country, so there is no import to tax until that moment arrives.
4. Transit is simply the cheapest way to route a truck: the duty is avoided as a side effect of the lighter paperwork, and nothing stands behind the goods if they leave the arrangement.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Transit is best understood as borrowed trust. The transit state lends its port, its roads, and its corridor to goods that belong somewhere else, and the loan is secured: the goods move under customs control from the office of departure to the office of discharge, on a single transit document that travels with them, sealed so that every later office can verify that nothing entered the local market, with a guarantee standing behind the duties at risk for the whole journey. That machinery is why the coastal treasury can release a foreign-bound container without collecting duty — the revenue is not unprotected, it is secured, and the security is called upon if the goods never exit the regime.

Answer 1 states the exact opposite of the definition: transit goods are under customs control throughout, never exempt from it. Answer 3 reaches for ownership, which is not the mechanism; duties are suspended because the goods are not entering that market, and it is the guarantee — not a theory of possession — that answers for them if they do. Answer 4 is the misreading the lesson's closing note opens with: transit is a procedure with conditions and a price for breaking them, not a routing discount.

The takeaway: transit is borrowed trust — the guarantee is the money, the seal the evidence, the single document the continuity.

=== ASSESSMENT Q5 | tests lesson 12.2 ===
QUESTION:
A truck carrying goods under a regional transit arrangement toward a landlocked destination stops overnight in the transit country it is passing through. A local merchant offers to buy part of the load on the spot, at a price the crew finds attractive. The driver is tempted, and the escort reasons that selling goods the truck is already carrying is an ordinary commercial decision.

Which statement best describes the position?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The crew may sell if the buyer pays the equivalent of the import duty afterwards, since the revenue is what the transit state actually cares about.
2. Selling part of the load is an ordinary commercial decision for the crew: the goods are physically in the country, and a sale between willing parties is no concern of customs.
3. The sale is permissible provided the remainder of the cargo stays sealed and the journey continues on the declared route, since only a full diversion breaks the transit.
4. Goods in transit never enter free circulation in the transit country: they may not be sold, unloaded, or diverted there except with customs authority, and a roadside sale would put dutiable goods into the local market, trigger a claim on the guarantee, and turn the movement into an investigation.
CORRECT: 4
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 4.

The rule the temptation collides with is absolute: goods in transit never enter free circulation in the transit country. They may not be sold, unloaded, or opened there except with customs authority, because the entire regional arrangement — one country's gateway serving another country's market — rests on the cargo passing through under control. A roadside sale is the exact event the guarantee exists to cover: dutiable goods leaking into the market of a state they were only passing through. The consequence pattern is fixed — the movement is held, the seal and document record is reconstructed, the customs authority whose revenue was at risk claims against the guarantee, and the guarantor pays before pursuing the declarant. The procedure ends, at best, in investigation and discharge refused — not in delivery.

Answer 2 is the free-circulation misconception stated plainly: physical presence never converts transit cargo into stock the crew may trade. Answer 3 tries to quarantine the breach — unloading and selling part of the load is itself the prohibited act, whatever remains sealed behind it. Answer 1 invents a private settlement; only customs can authorise any entry of the goods into that market, and a side payment between buyer and crew is not that authority — the duty consequence follows the event, not the arrangement.

The takeaway: transit cargo passes through a country under control, not through its market — the seal and the guarantee travel with the goods precisely so that the temptation finds no door.

=== ASSESSMENT Q6 | tests lesson 12.2 ===
QUESTION:
A truck moving under a regional transit arrangement finds the authorised route blocked by a flooded river crossing, with the transit period still running. The driver proposes to take a faster alternative road that rejoins the corridor beyond the obstruction; the escort suggests noting the change by hand on the transit document to keep the file tidy.

Which response reflects the transporter's responsibilities under the arrangement?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Contact the transit customs office without delay, report the obstruction, and obtain authority for the diversion before leaving the authorised route, because route and period are conditions of the release and an unauthorised diversion is the classic transit offence.
2. Take the alternative road and note the change by hand on the transit document, since the document belongs to the operator and a tidy annotation keeps the file consistent.
3. Take the alternative road without formality as long as the transit period is still met and the seal remains intact, because the conditions attach to the goods rather than to the road.
4. Abandon the transit at the obstruction and enter the goods for import in the transit country, since a procedure whose route has failed cannot lawfully continue at all.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

Route and period are conditions of the release, not suggestions. The movement was released on an authorised route for a defined transit period, and complying with both is a legal obligation with a price attached, not a planning preference the driver may weigh against the schedule. Where the route is blocked, the transporter's duty is to contact the transit customs office and obtain authority before leaving the authorised route — and to do so immediately, because in transit an unreported anomaly hardens into a suspected irregularity with time. Diversion without authorisation is the classic transit offence, the exact event the guarantee chain answers for, and it is committed the moment the truck turns, however honest the purpose.

Answer 2 adds the handwritten annotation: nothing in the transit file may be altered except by or under the authority of customs, and an unauthorised alteration converts a delay into an irregularity with a gap in the record. Answer 3 rests on the seal alone; the conditions travel as a set, and an intact seal does not supervise the route. Answer 4 abandons the procedure altogether: entering the goods for import in a country that was never their destination triggers duties and formalities there and substitutes one compliance failure for another.

The takeaway: deviation is never the driver's scheduling decision — report early, obtain authority, and let the record tell the story customs released.

=== ASSESSMENT Q7 | tests lesson 12.3 ===
QUESTION:
A trading company in Accra supplies electrical equipment to a buyer in a landlocked neighbouring capital: the consignment moves by sea to a coastal gateway and onward by road under transit. After a troubled first move, a review finds that every individual party performed competently — the transporter left on time, the terminal handled cleanly, each agent processed its own country's papers correctly. Yet the move still stalled: the transit guarantee was never confirmed, the border agent was never told the truck had departed, and the delivery booking at destination was never made. Each party had assumed another was handling it.

Which statement best diagnoses the failure and its remedy?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The parties were individually at fault after all: competence reviews and refresher training for each of them would have prevented the three gaps.
2. The move lacked a named orchestrator owning the whole journey: when nobody holds the plan, every party optimises its own piece and the interfaces belong to no one; the remedy is to name the lead forwarder as orchestrator and give it authority to set the plan, hold a departure when a document line is red, and demand status from every party.
3. Written coordination artifacts were unnecessary on a familiar corridor: experienced parties know their jobs, and the paperwork of matrices and plans adds delay without adding control.
4. The remedy is volume: every party should update every other party constantly by telephone and message, because coordination means more communication.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The lesson's diagnosis fits exactly: the commonest coordination failure is not incompetence but the absence of a named orchestrator. When nobody owns the whole journey, every party manages its own piece intelligently — the transporter its departure, each agent its own queue — and the interfaces between the pieces belong to no one: the guarantee no one confirmed, the departure no one announced, the booking no one made. The remedy is structural, not motivational: name the orchestrator, usually the lead freight forwarder, and give the role the authority it requires — to set the plan, to hold a departure when a document line is red, and to demand status from every party on the responsibility matrix.

Answer 1 blames competence where the review found none missing; retraining competent parties leaves the blank cells blank, because the gaps were between the parties, not inside them. Answer 3 discards the artifacts that make coordination explicit — the responsibility matrix, the milestone plan, the document matrix, the communication rhythm — on the strength of familiarity, which is precisely the assumption cross-border moves exist to test. Answer 4 is the volume misconception: coordination is better-aimed communication tied to milestones and named channels, and an operation in which everyone telephones everyone about everything is not coordinated, it is loud.

The takeaway: if the question of who is coordinating this move has no single name as its answer, the move does not yet have a plan.

=== ASSESSMENT Q8 | tests lesson 12.3 ===
QUESTION:
On a multi-country consignment, the coordinator keeps the document list as a working file to be completed as the journey goes, reasoning that any gap can be fixed en route with emails. Two days after departure the truck halts at the first border: a certificate the goods require there was never obtained, and the fix that would have taken minutes at origin now takes days of long-distance chasing while the load stands still.

Which statement best captures the discipline the coordinator missed?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Documents are verified at each interface as custody passes, so checking en route is the designed control, and assembling the file earlier would add nothing to it.
2. Digital systems make the document matrix unnecessary: a single-window declaration and electronic transit data assemble and validate the file on their own as the move proceeds.
3. The document matrix is completed before the goods move and audited row by row before departure, because documents are the permission slips that let each interface function, and a gap discovered at a border cannot be conjured there.
4. The receiving border agent owns every document gap on arrival, so the origin coordinator had no outstanding duty once the truck had been dispatched.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The document matrix records, for every document the move needs, the node at which it is required, who prepares it, who submits it, and its status — and the lesson attaches two disciplines to it: complete the matrix before the goods move, and audit it row by row before departure. The reason is structural. Documents are the permission slips that let each interface function: a declaration cannot be lodged until permits exist, and a truck cannot cross in transit until the transit document is in order behind its guarantee. A document discovered missing at a border cannot be conjured there. The audit costs minutes at origin; the same gap on the road costs days of long-distance chasing, with the goods standing still and every downstream clock running.

Answer 1 points at a real practice — document sets are checked as custody passes at each handover — but handover checks verify a complete set; they do not assemble one, and they operate at the point where a missing document is most expensive. Answer 2 overstates the systems: single windows and electronic transit accelerate validated data, they do not obtain certificates that were never applied for, and data quality governs everything downstream. Answer 4 misplaces ownership: the matrix names the preparing party at origin, and ownership does not migrate to whoever receives the truck.

The takeaway: documents gate every downstream interface, so audit the matrix before departure — while every problem is still cheap.

=== ASSESSMENT Q9 | tests lesson 12.3 ===
QUESTION:
A forwarder running a transit consignment toward a landlocked market lodges the movement's data with the next border office and sends document copies and schedule details to the destination agent before the truck leaves the coastal port. A colleague objects that the effort is wasted: the papers travel with the truck anyway, and the office and the agent will see everything on arrival.

Which statement best assesses the forwarder's practice?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The forwarder is right: information should travel ahead of the goods, because advance data lets the next office risk-assess and prepare before the truck arrives, and it lets the destination agent arrange clearance, transport, and labour for the plan that will actually arrive.
2. The colleague is right: presenting the transit document at every office is what the procedure requires, so advance copies and early schedules add nothing that the carried papers do not already provide.
3. Both are partly right: advance data helps the office, but schedule details should be withheld from the destination agent until the goods physically arrive, so that changed plans create no obligations.
4. The forwarder's practice substitutes for the border formalities: with the data lodged ahead, the seal and document checks at the crossing can be spared for that movement.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

The decisive principle of information exchange is that information should travel ahead of the goods. Data lodged with the next office before the truck arrives lets that office risk-assess, decide, and sometimes clear in advance, so the border spends minutes rather than hours on a compliant movement. Document copies and schedule details held by the destination agent before departure let it arrange clearance, transport, and labour against the plan that will actually arrive. Goods that outrun their information arrive to unprepared offices and unmade arrangements — delay purchased twice, once in waiting and once in confusion.

Answer 2 is right that the transit document must travel with the truck and be presented at every office — but legal presentation and advance preparation are complements, not substitutes: the carried document satisfies the procedure, while the advance data prepares the decision. Answer 3 manufactures information asymmetry: destination arrangements depend on the schedule, which is exactly why the agent must hold it early, and when a plan changes the revision travels to every affected party rather than being withheld until arrival. Answer 4 confuses acceleration with waiver — pre-arrival data speeds the risk decision, but the seal is still verified by eyes and hands at the crossing, and the goods are still what the discharge must account for.

The takeaway: data sent once, to everyone who needs it, before it is needed — the border should confirm, not discover.

=== ASSESSMENT Q10 | tests lesson 12.4 ===
QUESTION:
A distribution manager assembles a container's journey under separate contracts: a booking with a shipping line for the sea leg, and a separate contract with a rail operator for the inland leg. A colleague reviews the file and concludes: because the contracts are separate, this cannot be an intermodal movement, and the container will have to be unpacked and its goods re-handled at each mode change.

Which statement best assesses the colleague's reasoning?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The colleague is right: separate contracts oblige each carrier to unpack and repack the goods on its own leg, so re-handling at each mode change is the price of assembling a journey.
2. The colleague has the term wrong but the practice right: any movement booked on separate contracts is through transport, and through transport always involves handling the goods between stages.
3. The colleague has tied the loading unit to the contracts, but the two questions are separate: intermodal describes goods moving in the same loading unit across modes without handling the goods themselves, whatever the contract shape — separate contracts per leg are fully compatible with one container carried unopened throughout, and multimodal is what a single contract with one responsible operator would add.
4. The colleague is wrong only about the label: a journey combining sea and rail is automatically multimodal, because the sea carrier's booking always imposes a single contract covering the inland leg.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The colleague has welded together two questions the lesson teaches to ask separately. Intermodal transport is defined physically: goods moving by multiple modes in the same loading unit, typically a container, without handling the goods themselves during the mode changes — and that definition says nothing about contracts. Multimodal transport is defined commercially: at least two modes under a single multimodal transport contract, with one operator contracting as principal and answering for the whole journey. A container packed once at origin, lifted from vessel to rail wagon, and opened only at destination is an intermodal movement whether the legs were bought as one service or assembled under separate contracts; the contract shape decides only whether the movement is also multimodal, and who answers for it end to end.

Answer 1 invents a re-handling rule: nothing in a separate sea contract obliges anyone to cut the container open at the rail transfer, and the unchanged loading unit is precisely the economy intermodal exists to capture. Answer 2 misdefines through transport, which is the umbrella term for door-to-door movements of any contractual shape, sold under one document or assembled. Answer 4 invents an imposed contract: a sea booking does not by itself create a multimodal contract covering the inland leg.

The takeaway: ask whether the loading unit stayed unchanged, and separately, who has contracted to answer for the journey — neither answer implies the other.

=== ASSESSMENT Q11 | tests lesson 12.4 ===
QUESTION:
An importer buys machinery overseas on terms that leave the carriage to the buyer, and assembles the journey itself: a shipping line for the sea leg to the coastal port, a rail operator for the inland leg, and a haulier for delivery to the warehouse. At the warehouse, several machines show crush damage. The cases left the overseas port in apparent good order, no handover record localises the damage to a leg, and the transfer between the port terminal and the rail operator took place in a yard that neither contract clearly covered.

Which statement best describes the buyer's claims position?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The three carriers are jointly liable for the whole journey, so the buyer may recover the full loss from whichever of them it chooses to pursue.
2. The claim follows the leg: under separate contracts each carrier answers only for its own performance, so the buyer must localise the damage to a leg and claim under that leg's contract — and where no record localises it, each carrier can point to the leg after its own, while the seam between the contracts may belong to no carrier at all.
3. The claim fails outright: goods that travelled under more than one contract cannot be claimed for at all, whatever the local evidence shows.
4. The last carrier in the chain answers by default, because responsibility passes forward at each handover and ends with the party that delivered the goods to the warehouse.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Under separate contracts, each carrier is responsible for its own leg under its own contract — the line under its bill of lading, the rail operator under its consignment note, the haulier under its road contract — so the claim follows the leg. The buyer must first localise the damage; where the evidence cannot, each carrier can point to the leg after its own, and the claimant carries a burden of proof it cannot discharge. Worse, the seams between the contracts — here, the terminal yard between discharge and the rail pickup — may belong to no carrier's clear cover at all, falling to whichever party let the gap exist: usually the one who assembled the legs. This is the claims complexity of assembled transport.

Answer 1 invents a joint liability that separate contracts do not create; each contract binds only its own parties to its own leg. Answer 3 overstates the position: the claim is not impossible, it is unbundled — damage localised to a leg is claimable against that leg's carrier, which is exactly why the handover records the lesson demands are, under assembled transport, the claim itself. Answer 4 invents a forward-rolling default; responsibility does not pass to whoever happened to deliver last.

Under a single multimodal contract the same loss would have one address: the MTO answers from receipt to delivery regardless of the leg, then pursues recovery inside its own network.

The takeaway: under separate contracts an unknown leg means an orphaned claim — the through-document exists to give the claim an address.

=== ASSESSMENT Q12 | tests lesson 12.4 ===
QUESTION:
To win a contract on the fastest promised delivery, a planner designs a multimodal move with zero planned dwell at the road leg's border crossing, calculating that customs and transit formalities should take no meaningful time, and argues before the board that buffer time is wasted time that only erodes the quotation.

Which statement best assesses the design?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The design is sound: a promised delivery date disciplines the whole chain, and planning for delay would only invite it.
2. Buffer belongs at seaports, where congestion is known and documented; a land border crossing needs no planned dwell, because its formalities are procedural rather than physical.
3. Any ordinary formalities can be absorbed by making up time on the open road, so planned dwell at the border can safely be set to zero.
4. The planner has confused the two dwellings: planned dwell at an interface is a buffer — time bought so that a late arrival or a slow examination is absorbed where it is cheap — while unplanned dwell is delay discovered at the worst point of the chain, with the onward legs already booked and the storage and demurrage clocks ready to run; buffers are risk management with a calendar, and each should carry a milestone so its consumption is noticed while alternatives still exist.
CORRECT: 4
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 4.

Planned dwell and unplanned dwell are different animals wearing the same number of hours. Dwell planned at an interface is a buffer: time bought on purpose so that a late arrival, a slow examination, or a missed connection is absorbed where it is cheap — before the onward legs are booked against a promise. Dwell that surprises is delay discovered at the worst point of the chain, with the next leg already scheduled and the demurrage and storage clocks ready to run. A plan without buffers assumes an ordinary day, which cross-border moves rarely provide. Buffers are risk management with a calendar, and each should carry its own milestone, so that when one is eaten, someone notices while alternatives still exist.

Answer 1 lets the promise stand in for the plan; a delivery date disciplines nothing about the variance it must survive. Answer 2 confines friction to seaports, but the module locates it wherever control points and connections stand, and a border crossing is exactly such a seam: transit formalities inside a multimodal move need time and documents reserved, not treated as the driver's private concern. Answer 3 proposes to drive standing time back on the open road; hours consumed at a control point are not recoverable at speed, and the pressure to recover them is what produces route offences.

The takeaway: design each interface's clock deliberately — the buffer is purchased insurance, priced in hours, against failures priced in missed deliveries.
