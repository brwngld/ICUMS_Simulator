=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Why warehouse documentation and recordkeeping matter
BODY:
Module 6 taught a discipline about trade documents: check every detail against its source, keep one truth from document to document, and investigate discrepancies instead of burying them. This lesson carries that same discipline off the ship's rail and across the warehouse gate. It is called warehouse documentation, the documents and electronic records used to support and verify warehouse activities.

The governing principle is short to state and demanding to live: every physical movement has a matching record, and every record has an author.

Goods arrive, and something records their arrival: what came, how much, in what condition, and who received it. Goods move within the building, and something records the move. Goods leave, and something records what left, for whom, and who took custody. When the physical and the recorded travel together, the warehouse can answer at any hour what it holds, where it holds it, and what happened to it. When they drift apart, the business is running on a story.

The consequences of drift are concrete. Sales teams confirm customer orders against stock that does not exist. Purchasing reorders goods that are sitting on the shelf. Losses stay invisible until a count announces them months later, when no investigation can realistically find their cause. And when a supplier's invoice arrives, the company may hold no receipt record to prove what it actually received.

The second half of the principle, that every record has an author, is what makes accountability possible. Each paper document carries a name or signature, and each electronic posting carries a user identity and a time. Nobody can adjust a balance invisibly, and honest staff are protected, because the record shows who did what.

Poor recordkeeping is therefore not a clerical weakness at the edge of logistics. It is a failure at the centre, because every picking decision, every replenishment, every payment and every loss investigation stands on the records.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Receiving: the purchase order, the delivery note, and the Goods Received Note
BODY:
Lesson 8.2, Receiving, Put-Away, Picking, and Dispatch, walked through receiving as a process. This lesson looks at the documents the process produces, because receiving is where the stock record begins.

Three documents meet at the receiving dock.

1. The purchase order
The purchase order is the buyer's own order document, stating what was ordered, in what quantity and at what agreed price. It is the reference against which every delivery claim is tested.

2. The delivery note
The delivery note is the document that accompanies the goods from the supplier or carrier, stating what the supplier says is being delivered. It is a claim, not a fact, until the buyer's own count confirms it.

3. The Goods Received Note
The goods received note, usually shortened to GRN, is a document or electronic record confirming the receipt of specified goods into a warehouse or organisation. The receiving team raises it only after counting the goods and checking their condition, and it states what was actually received, which may differ from what the delivery note claimed.

The essential rule follows: the receipt document creates the stock record. Until a GRN is raised, the goods do not exist in the company's records, however physically present they are; and whatever quantity the GRN states is the quantity the balance becomes. A careless or pressured receiving entry poisons every figure downstream.

Discipline at the dock is therefore simple and non-negotiable. Count before signing. Inspect condition before signing. If the count is short, or cartons are crushed, or the wrong item has arrived, the discrepancy is noted on the delivery note at that moment and countersigned by the driver, before the truck leaves the yard.

A signed delivery note is also a legal record. It is evidence of what was handed over, when, and in what visible condition, and it protects both parties: the buyer against claims for goods that never arrived, and the supplier against claims for goods it never promised.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Put-away and location records
BODY:
Received goods must then be put away, and the moment they are placed, a second kind of record begins: the location record.

A location record states what quantity of which item sits in which place. Every storage position carries a code, such as A-14-03 for aisle fourteen, level three, and every put-away posts the item and quantity to that code. In a computerised warehouse the warehouse management system, the software that records what enters, where it sits, and what leaves the building, holds these location balances. In a smaller store the same record may be a bin card or location list kept by hand. The logic is identical either way.

Location accuracy is not an administrative nicety. It is what makes picking possible. A picker sent to a location by the record must find there what the record promises, in the quantity it promises. When put-away posts goods to the wrong code, the goods are not lost in the physical sense, but they are lost in every practical sense: the system says a location holds stock that is not there, and another location holds stock the system denies.

This is the first appearance of the distinction that runs through the whole lesson: system versus shelf. The system is what the records say. The shelf is what is physically there. Warehousing is the constant work of keeping the two identical, document by document, and the put-away record is one of the points where they can silently separate.

A useful working habit follows. If an item cannot be found where the system says it is, the search should begin with the most recent put-away and transfer records for that item, because that is where the truth and the record last had a chance to part company.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Moving and issuing stock: transfer notes, pick lists, and dispatch notes
BODY:
Every change in the warehouse after put-away is a stock movement, any recorded change in the quantity or location of inventory, including receipts, issues, returns, and transfers. Four kinds of record carry the day-to-day movements.

1. The transfer note
A transfer note records an internal move between locations: forty-eight cartons from bulk location B-07 to picking location P-03, for example. The move is posted twice, once out of the source location and once into the destination, and the two postings must name the same quantity. An internal move never changes what the warehouse holds in total; it only changes where the stock sits. That property matters later, in reconciliation.

2. The pick list
The pick list instructs the picker: which items, from which locations, in what quantities, for which order. It is an instruction when it leaves the office and a record when it comes back signed, confirming what was actually taken. Together the pick list and the dispatch paperwork convert a customer order into a recorded issue.

3. The dispatch note
The dispatch note states what left the warehouse, in what quantity, for which customer, on which vehicle and at what time. It accompanies the goods and is signed by the driver or carrier, acknowledging custody. The signed copy returns to the warehouse as proof of dispatch.

4. The signature
The signature on dispatch paperwork is the movement's author, and it marks the moment responsibility transfers. Once the carrier has signed, shortages and damage discovered downstream are argued between carrier and customer against that document, so the count and condition at handover must be checked before signing, not assumed.

The rule beneath all four records is the one this lesson opened with: nothing moves without a record, and nothing is recorded without a name. The undisciplined shortcuts, a pallet walked from one aisle to another with no transfer note, or a driver waved off with a verbal confirmation, take thirty seconds each and create discrepancies that cost hours to trace months later.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Returns: documenting the reverse flow
BODY:
Lesson 1.4, Types of Logistics, introduced reverse logistics, the flows that run back toward their origin: customer returns, rejected deliveries, recalled goods, and packaging returning for reuse. A warehouse that documents goods arriving and leaving but never documents goods coming back will find its records pulled apart in both directions.

The returns note is the document of the reverse flow. It states what came back, from whom, when, why, and in what condition, and it is raised before the goods re-enter stock, not after.

Returned goods are not simply put back on the shelf. They are received in reverse: counted against the returns note, inspected, and graded. Sellable goods return to a normal location through a normal put-away record. Damaged goods go to a quarantine area, to be written off through the adjustment process described shortly. Goods returned to a supplier travel out again against their own document, so that the outbound leg of the return is recorded like any other dispatch.

Two failure patterns deserve naming. First, goods that return without paperwork: the records say the goods left and were never received back, so the customer's account and the stock balance are both wrong. Second, goods that return and are shelved informally: physically present, yet recorded as issued. Both create discrepancies, and the second hides real stock from the people planning orders against it.

The discipline is the same as at the front door, run backwards: every return has a document, every document has an author, and the stock record only changes when the returns note says so.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The perpetual record: stock cards and the inventory ledger
BODY:
All those documents feed a running account of the stock. Two records matter here.

The stock card is a manual or electronic record showing receipts, issues, and balances for a particular inventory item. One card, physical or electronic, belongs to one item, sometimes to one item in one location. Every receipt is added, every issue is subtracted, and a new balance is struck after each entry. The arithmetic of every stock card is the same: receipts minus issues, applied to the opening balance, gives the closing balance.

The inventory ledger is a record of inventory transactions and balances maintained for stock management and accounting purposes. Where the stock card follows one item, the ledger holds the whole: every item and every transaction in one place, valued as well as counted, so that the accountants and the warehouse are reading the same figures.

Together they form the perpetual record, the running balance that exists without anyone going to the shelf to look. The perpetual method is what makes modern inventory control possible: Lesson 8.3's reorder points and ABC classifications all assume the system can be trusted to say what is on hand at any moment.

Three habits keep the perpetual record trustworthy. First, transactions are posted at the time of the movement, against the document that caused them: the GRN number, the transfer note number, the pick list. Second, every posting keeps its document reference, so that any balance can be unwound and checked line by line. Third, no posting is made from memory. A balance keyed the next morning, from recollection, is a guess wearing the costume of a record.

The perpetual record is a claim about the shelf. It is not the shelf. The rest of this lesson is about the moment the claim is tested.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Damage, loss, and excess: documenting the adjustments
BODY:
Records can also be changed deliberately, and how a warehouse changes them is a test of its discipline.

A damage report is a document recording goods identified as damaged, including relevant quantities, conditions, and circumstances. Damaged goods are moved to quarantine, described or photographed, and reported: what was damaged, how, when, and by whom it was found. A stock adjustment is an authorised correction made to inventory records to reflect verified changes or discrepancies. Damaged stock is written off by adjustment; so is stock confirmed missing after investigation; so is excess found on the shelf.

Three rules govern every adjustment.

1. Every adjustment has a reason
The record does not simply change by three cartons. It changes by three cartons because a count found a variance, or a damage report was raised, or a transfer error was traced. The reason is written on the adjustment.

2. Every adjustment has an authoriser
The person who found the discrepancy is not automatically the person who corrects the record. An authorised stock adjustment requires approval, normally by a supervisor or manager, and the approval is itself recorded. This separation is what stops a recording mistake from becoming a convenient habit.

3. Excess is documented like shortage
Cartons found on the shelf that the record does not expect are a discrepancy in the positive direction, and they are investigated with the same seriousness. Stock may have been received twice, returned without a returns note, or moved from another location on an unrecorded transfer. Finding stock is a finding, not a gift.

The reduction in recorded inventory caused by theft, damage, loss, or recording errors is called inventory shrinkage. Shrinkage is not one phenomenon but the residue of many. This lesson's work, documenting movements, reporting damage, and adjusting only with cause, is how a business keeps that residue honest and small.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Stocktaking and the periodic physical count
BODY:
Sooner or later the claim must be tested against the shelf. Stocktaking is the physical counting and verification of goods held in inventory. Its purposes are direct: to confirm that the records describe reality, to give the accountants verified quantities for the financial statements, to expose loss and damage, and to reset the records to a counted truth.

Periodic stocktaking is the physical counting of inventory at predetermined intervals, commonly once a year. In its fullest form it is the wall-to-wall count, and its procedure has a discipline of its own.

1. Operations freeze
Receiving and dispatch stop, or are confined to a segregated, clearly marked area, so that nothing moves while it is being counted. A stock movement during a count turns the count into a photograph of a moving object.

2. Everything is counted
Every location and every item is counted, including quarantine areas, returns and goods still at receiving, against count sheets that state the location, the item, the unit of measure, and either the recorded quantity or a blank space for the count.

3. Variances are investigated before records are adjusted
The comparison of counts to records produces a variance report. Only after the variances have been investigated, recounted where necessary and their causes identified, are the records corrected through authorised adjustments. The count tests the records; it never replaces the investigation.

4. Counting is verified
Good practice recounts locations showing large variances, ideally by a different person, before accepting the figure.

A periodic count is thorough but blunt. Once a year the business discovers twelve months of accumulated error at once, operations stop, and the causes of the variances are usually long cold by the time anyone investigates them. That weakness is what cycle counting answers, in the next block.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Cycle counting
BODY:
Cycle counting is an inventory verification method in which selected items are counted regularly rather than counting all inventory at once. Instead of one annual freeze, the count is spread through the year in a rotating schedule, a few items or locations each day or week, while operations continue.

Cycle counting is not random. Lesson 8.3 introduced ABC analysis, the classification of stock items by value contribution: A items, the few that carry most of the value; B items in the middle; and C items, the many that carry little. Counting frequency follows the classification.

1. A items are counted most often, commonly monthly
These are the high-value, fast-moving items where an error costs the most and recurs the soonest.

2. B items are counted less often, perhaps quarterly.

3. C items are counted rarely, once or twice a year.

The result is that the most important stock is verified many times a year, while the total counting effort stays a fraction of a wall-to-wall count and the business never stops.

Cycle counting has a second, deeper purpose beyond verification: diagnosis. Each count compares a small piece of the perpetual record with the shelf while the trail is fresh. A variance found in March can still be traced to February's receipts; a variance found at the annual count in December often cannot. Counted this way, every variance is an early signal pointing at a process: a receiving slip, a picking error, a mis-keyed transfer. The cause is found, corrected, and the same error stops being made. Inventory accuracy, the share of counted items whose physical quantity matches the record, becomes a managed number rising through the year rather than an annual surprise.

Counting is therefore not a punishment for suspected wrongdoers. It is the routine instrument by which a warehouse discovers where its documents and its discipline are failing, while the findings are still small enough to fix.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Why records and reality differ: causes of inventory discrepancies
BODY:
An inventory discrepancy is a difference between recorded inventory information and actual stock quantities or conditions. Discrepancies have causes, and reading the pattern of a discrepancy is how the cause is found. The common causes are worth learning as a checklist.

1. Receiving errors
A short delivery accepted uncounted, a wrong item signed for, a GRN keyed at 100 for a delivery of 110. The record is wrong from its first moment.

2. Picking errors
The wrong item picked, one carton short picked and dispatched, or an item taken from the wrong location. The physical stock has moved differently from the record of it.

3. Unrecorded moves
The pallet walked to another aisle with no transfer note. The stock is on the premises, but the system is looking in the wrong place. In warehouses with many locations this is the most common cause of variance at individual locations.

4. Damage not written off
Goods crushed in racking, leaks discovered on a pallet, and no damage report raised. The shelf holds less sellable stock than the record claims.

5. Theft and pilferage
Stock leaves without a document. This is why every record carries an author: movements with authors can be audited, movements without cannot.

6. Unit-of-measure confusion
A receipt recorded in pieces, an issue posted in cartons, a count sheet that does not say which unit it wants. The arithmetic of the record stays self-consistent while the units underneath it are mixed, producing losses and surpluses far larger than any physical event. The important notes return to this cause, because it is the quietest.

Two disciplines follow. First, treat a discrepancy as information: a variance that clusters on one item, one location, one shift or one supplier is telling the investigator where to look. Second, never let the size of a variance decide its importance. Small recurring variances have cost businesses more than single dramatic ones, precisely because nobody investigates what looks trivial.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Stock reconciliation and the audit trail
BODY:
Stock reconciliation is the process of comparing physical inventory quantities with recorded balances and investigating differences. It is the formal name for the work this lesson has been building towards, and its procedure is a discipline in steps.

1. Compare
Count the physical stock and set it against the recorded balance, location by location, in one agreed unit of measure.

2. Recount
Confirm the variance with a second count, ideally by another person, before treating it as real. Many discrepancies die at this step, because they were counting errors.

3. Trace the movements
For the affected item and location, follow the documents since the last verified balance: receipts against GRNs, issues against pick lists and dispatch notes, transfers against transfer notes, returns against returns notes, and write-offs against damage reports.

4. Check the recent activity
Recent receipts and recent transfers are the likeliest places for a fresh error, and a variance at one location should prompt an immediate look at the locations it exchanges stock with.

5. Adjust with approval
Only when the cause is found, or the investigation has exhausted the trail, is a stock adjustment posted, with the reason stated and the authoriser's approval recorded.

6. Fix the cause
The adjustment repairs the record; only correcting the cause prevents the next discrepancy. An adjustment without a fix is a promise that the same variance will return.

Every step leaves its mark on the audit trail, a chronological record of inventory transactions and changes that supports verification and accountability: who counted, who recounted, who investigated, who approved the adjustment, when, and against which documents. The audit trail is what turns the records from an opinion into evidence.

The trail reaches beyond the warehouse, to the payment gate. Before a company pays a supplier's invoice, careful practice matches three documents: the purchase order, the goods received note and the invoice, and payment is released only when they agree. This is the three-way match. The warehouse's paperwork is the company's evidence in that match: the GRN proves what was actually received, and a signed delivery note carrying a dock-side shortage remark can settle an invoice argument in minutes. A warehouse that documents carelessly does not merely lose track of its stock; it loses the ability to prove what it should pay for.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A reconciliation walk-through at a Kumasi distributor
BODY:
A wholesale distributor of tinned tomatoes in Kumasi stores one brand in bulk racking at location B-07, due for its monthly count. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: The record's claim
The stock card for B-07 shows an opening balance of 300 cartons at the last verified count. Since then: a receipt of 100 cartons on GRN 214, issues of 152 against three customer pick lists, and a transfer of 45 cartons out to picking location P-03, transfer note 87. The expected balance is 300 + 100 - 152 - 45 = 203 cartons. The system says 203.

Step 2: The shelf's answer
The count team finds 200 cartons; a second person recounts and confirms. The record claims 203; the shelf holds 200, a variance of three cartons.

Step 3: Recorded, not adjusted
The supervisor records the variance and opens an investigation. No adjustment is posted yet.

Step 4: The receipt is checked
GRN 214 matches the supplier's delivery note and the purchase order: 100 received and counted at the dock. Correct.

Step 5: The issues are checked
The three pick lists total 152 cartons, each matching a signed dispatch note. Correct.

Step 6: The transfer is checked
Transfer note 87 says 48 cartons were moved from B-07 to P-03, but the system posting shows 45 keyed on both sides. Counting P-03: its record expects 20 + 45 - 30 = 35 cartons; the shelf holds 38. The mirror image of the missing three.

Step 7: The truth
Physically: 200 + 38 = 238 cartons. On the records: 203 + 35 = 238. Nothing has been lost or stolen: a transfer of 48 cartons was posted as 45, leaving B-07 overstated by three and P-03 understated by three.

Step 8: The approved adjustment
The supervisor corrects both balances, citing the transfer posting error and referencing transfer note 87 and both count sheets. The manager authorises the adjustment, and the audit trail records counts, traces, correction and approval.

Step 9: The cause is fixed
Replenishment quantities are now scan-confirmed at the destination location before posting, and transfers keyed from memory are prohibited.

What does this example demonstrate?
A variance at one location is a question, not a verdict. Keying B-07 down to 200 without investigation would have made the record match the shelf while the true error at P-03 survived to become a phantom surplus elsewhere. The investigation took under an hour because every movement had a numbered document.

Lessons from the example
The perpetual record's arithmetic makes the expected balance computable, the document trail makes variances traceable, and the authorised adjustment, with its reason and approval, closes the loop honestly.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Cycle counting finds a slotting fault in Rotterdam
BODY:
An independent spare-parts distributor serves marine workshops from a store in Rotterdam, holding several thousand items classified A, B and C by value contribution. The A items, the small number of high-value lines that carry most of the business, are counted monthly in a rotating schedule; B items quarterly; C items once or twice a year. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: The programme
Two trained counters work through the A items each month while the store keeps operating. Variances are reported the same day and investigated while the trail is fresh.

Step 2: The first finding
In the first month, counts of one family of bearings show small variances: two bins short by a box or two, one bin over. Each variance is recounted and traced, but the traces find nothing conclusive. The quantities are adjusted, with approval, and a note is made.

Step 3: The pattern
The second and third monthly counts show the same cluster: the same pair of adjacent bins, holding two bearing part numbers whose codes differ by a single digit, ending 471 and 474. Whenever one bin counts short, its neighbour counts over by a similar quantity. The family total is nearly always correct; the individual bins are wrong.

Step 4: The diagnosis
The variances are not theft or disappearance. They are put-away and picking slips between two look-alike bins: same box size, small labels printed alike, bins side by side, and the pattern concentrated in the first weeks of recently started staff.

Step 5: The fix
The two part numbers are re-slotted to non-adjacent bins. The labels are replaced with large, colour-banded labels showing the full part number and a check character, and put-away must be scan-confirmed at the bin before it posts.

Step 6: The proof
The next two monthly counts show no variance on either item. The monthly count, not the annual one, found and killed the fault within a quarter.

What does this example demonstrate?
Counting as diagnosis, not punishment. Nobody was suspected, accused or disciplined; a process fault was found and fixed. Had the small variances simply been adjusted away each month, the shrinkage line would have quietly absorbed them, and the look-alike corner of the store would still be manufacturing new errors every week.

Key lesson
Frequent counting of the most valuable items turns the annual surprise into a monthly feedback loop, and repeated small variances are a signal to read, not a rounding error to post away.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: The three-way match blocks a payment in Accra
BODY:
A grocery wholesaler in Accra receives cooking oil from a local bottler. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: The order
The buyer issues a purchase order, PO 4087, for 120 cartons of cooking oil.

Step 2: The delivery and the dock
A truck arrives with a delivery note stating 120 cartons. The receiving clerk counts 100. He notes the shortage on both copies of the delivery note, 100 cartons received and 20 short, and the driver countersigns before the truck leaves. The GRN is raised for 100 cartons, referencing the purchase order and the annotated delivery note. The stock record now says 100, and says why.

Step 3: The invoice
A week later the supplier's invoice arrives: 120 cartons.

Step 4: The match
Accounts places the three documents side by side: PO 4087 for 120, the GRN for 100, and the invoice for 120. The three-way match fails on quantity. Payment is held, not refused, and the query goes to the supplier with copies of the purchase order, the signed delivery note carrying the shortage remark, and the GRN.

Step 5: The resolution
The supplier checks its dispatch records: 120 cartons left its depot on the truck, and the driver's own copy carries the same shortage remark. Its investigation finds the 20 cartons left behind at its depot in that morning's loading confusion. The supplier delivers the missing 20 cartons the following week, and the warehouse counts them in and raises a further GRN.

Step 6: The payment
Accounts now holds a complete match: purchase order 120, GRNs of 100 and 20, invoice 120. Payment is released in full, within the agreed terms, and both parties' records agree.

What does this example demonstrate?
The warehouse paperwork is the company's evidence at the payment gate. Without the dock count, the shortage remark and the driver's countersignature, the wholesaler would be arguing a recollection against an invoice; with them, the discrepancy resolved in an afternoon. The three-way match protected the buyer from paying for 20 cartons it never received, and equally protected the supplier from an unsupported refusal to pay.

Key lesson
Receiving discipline is payment discipline. The GRN and the signed delivery note are not paperwork trailing behind the transaction; they are the documents the finance team stands on.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Adjusting the record without finding the cause
BODY:
The most damaging habit in warehouse recordkeeping is also the most understandable: the count disagrees with the system, month-end is close, so somebody keys the balance to match the shelf and moves on.

Understandable, and precisely how losses hide.

A stock adjustment is an authorised correction made to reflect verified changes. It is the conclusion of an investigation, never a shortcut around one. When records are forced to agree without a cause being found, three things happen.

1. The real cause survives. The mis-keyed transfer, the receiving error or the picking fault that produced this variance will produce the next one, and the next.

2. Genuine losses become invisible. Theft and damage that should surface as unexplained variances dissolve into a general pool of adjustments, where no pattern can be seen and nobody is accountable.

3. The audit trail testifies against the practice. A chronological record of transactions and changes shows plainly a balance corrected repeatedly, without recorded reasons, by the same user on the last day of each month. Auditors read exactly that pattern, and it changes the question from why this stock is short to who is hiding what.

The warning has a mirror image. After an honest investigation has exhausted the trail, refusing to adjust is also wrong: records that permanently disagree with reality undermine trust in every figure the warehouse produces. Adjust, then, with the reason stated, with the authoriser's approval recorded, and with the cause corrected or named as unresolved and watched.

Remember too that recurring small variances on the same item deserve as much attention as one large variance. The small ones are the ones nobody investigates.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: The count is only as good as the discipline between counts
BODY:
A stock count captures one moment. Its value depends almost entirely on what happened before the counters arrived.

If documents lag reality, with receipts keyed at the end of the week from memory, transfers walked but never posted, and dispatch paperwork completed days after the event, the perpetual record is already fiction before counting begins. The count will then find variances that are not mysteries at all, merely unposted movements, and the business will adjust its records to explain a chaos of its own making.

Practices that make counts meaningful:

1. Post at the moment of movement, against the document that caused it.

2. Freeze or segregate during the count. Goods arriving during a wall-to-wall count are held in a marked area, never mixed into stock that has been counted.

3. Count labelled, tidy locations. Goods without labels, or sitting in places the system does not know about, cannot be counted reliably by anyone.

4. Allow no informal borrowing. Taking cartons from one location to cover a short pick elsewhere, without a transfer note, manufactures two discrepancies to save one trip.

The same discipline underwrites cycle counting. Its diagnostic value depends entirely on being able to trace a variance through documents that exist. A business that counts once a year but tolerates undocumented movement between counts has not verified its records; it has photographed its chaos at high resolution.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Unit of measure: the quietest way to lose stock
BODY:
Records count numbers; shelves hold things. The unit of measure is the translation between the two, and it is where quiet losses live.

One pallet of a bottled product may hold 60 cartons, and one carton 24 bottles, giving 1,440 bottles to a pallet. These conversions are illustrative, but their consequence is not: if a receipt is keyed in bottles and a later issue is posted in cartons, the record keeps perfect arithmetic while the units underneath it are mixed. A single unit slip at pallet scale makes thousands of bottles appear or vanish on paper, with no physical event behind them at all.

The rules that prevent it are simple.

1. One agreed unit of measure per stock record, chosen deliberately, normally the unit in which the item is issued.

2. Every document states the unit beside every quantity: the GRN, the transfer note, the pick list and the count sheet.

3. Conversions are printed on the documents, one pallet equals 60 cartons, so that receiving counts in the unit the record expects.

4. Count sheets carry the unit, and counters count in it.

Unit-of-measure confusion is called the quietest loss because nothing about it looks dramatic. Totals stay plausible, no dramatic disappearance triggers an alarm, and small slips at carton scale pass for ordinary shrinkage for years. When a variance refuses to trace to any movement, check the units before blaming the staff: a mixed unit is a record-keeping fault, and it is found in the documents, not on the shelf.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A warehouse supervisor in Takoradi counts a storage location and finds 96 cartons on the shelf where the stock record shows a balance of 101. Month-end is two days away.

Which action best describes correct professional practice?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Key the record down to 96 so the balance matches the shelf, because the physical count is always the more reliable figure.
2. Post a stock adjustment of five cartons to damage, since damage is the most common cause of differences between records and shelves.
3. Recount the location, trace the receipts, issues and transfers since the last verified balance, and adjust the record with approval only once the difference has been investigated.
4. Leave the record unchanged, because differences of five cartons or fewer are too small to affect the business.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

Stock reconciliation is a procedure: compare, recount, trace the movements through their documents, then adjust with a stated reason and an authoriser's approval, and fix the cause. The recount comes first because many variances turn out to be counting errors. The trace comes next because a variance that can be followed through GRNs, pick lists, transfer notes and damage reports usually explains itself, as the Kumasi worked example showed.

Answer 1 is the habit this lesson exists to dismantle: forcing the record to match the shelf buries the cause, manufactures a phantom variance at whatever location the real error sits, and teaches the audit trail to testify against the warehouse. Answer 2 skips investigation and invents a cause; posting an adjustment to damage without a damage report or evidence is adjustment by guesswork. Answer 4 ignores the lesson's warning that small variances matter: small recurring differences, quietly tolerated, have cost businesses more than single dramatic ones, because nobody investigates what looks trivial.

The takeaway: a variance is a question, not a verdict. Investigate before adjusting, and only adjust with authorisation.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
A buyer's accounts office receives a supplier's invoice for 120 cartons. The purchase order was for 120 cartons, but the warehouse raised the goods received note for 100 cartons, and the delivery note returned from the dock carries a shortage remark countersigned by the driver.

Which statement best describes what should happen?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The invoice should be paid in full, because the purchase order and the supplier's invoice agree on the quantity of 120 cartons.
2. Payment should be held while the difference is investigated, and the goods received note with the signed delivery note are the company's evidence that only 100 cartons were actually received.
3. The entire delivery should be returned to the supplier, because quantities that do not match the invoice make the whole consignment unacceptable.
4. The invoice should be quietly paid for 100 cartons without contacting the supplier, because the warehouse count is always the correct figure.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The three-way match compares the purchase order, the goods received note and the invoice, and payment is released only when the three agree. Here they do not: the order says 120, the receipt says 100, the invoice says 120. Payment is therefore held while the discrepancy is investigated, and the warehouse paperwork decides the case: the GRN is the verified record of what was received, and the delivery note countersigned at the dock proves the shortage was noted before the truck left, as the Accra worked example showed.

Answer 1 pays against a promise. The purchase order and the invoice agreeing proves only that the supplier billed what was ordered; the GRN, not the order, is the evidence of what arrived, and paying 120 would mean paying for 20 cartons never received. Answer 3 overreacts: 100 correct cartons are 100 cartons of value, and the remedy is resolution of the shortage, not rejection of the delivery. Answer 4 reaches the right quantity by the wrong road, skipping the investigation and the supplier's right to check its own records, which is how evidence becomes a long argument instead of a quick settlement.

The takeaway: warehouse paperwork is the company's evidence at the payment gate.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
The owner of a spare-parts store asks his team why they should count their highest-value items every month when the entire store is already counted once a year at the annual stocktake.

Which option best answers him?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Monthly counting of key items exists mainly to prove to the owner that the staff are honest, which is the true purpose of any inventory verification.
2. Cycle counting is suitable only for small stores with few items, while annual stocktaking is the method designed for stores with many thousands of lines.
3. Cycle counting replaces good documentation, because frequent counting catches every error eventually and makes recordkeeping at the moment of movement unnecessary.
4. Cycle counting counts selected items, typically the most valuable, on a continuous schedule, so errors are found and their causes fixed during the year instead of surfacing all at once at the annual count.
CORRECT: 4
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 4.

Cycle counting counts selected items regularly rather than everything at once, with frequency following the ABC classification from Lesson 8.3: A items most often, B items less, C items rarely. Its advantage over the annual wall-to-wall count is timeliness. A variance found in March can still be traced through documents that are weeks old, so the cause, a receiving slip, a picking error, a mis-keyed transfer, is found and fixed while it is small, and inventory accuracy rises through the year instead of collapsing into one annual surprise, as the Rotterdam worked example showed.

Answer 1 misses the point of counting entirely: verification is a diagnostic tool for processes, not an honesty test, and treating it as policing corrodes the culture it is meant to protect. Answer 2 inverts reality; cycle counting scales to any size and is at its most valuable precisely where thousands of lines make a full count impractical. Answer 3 confuses verification with recordkeeping: counting finds errors, but only documents posted at the moment of movement keep the record true between counts, and a count can only trace variances through documents that exist.

The takeaway: count what matters most, most often, and treat every variance as a signal to investigate.
