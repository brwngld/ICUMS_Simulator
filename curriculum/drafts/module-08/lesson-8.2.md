=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: One Unbroken Chain: From Arrival to Dispatch

BODY:
The previous lesson, Warehouse Functions and Layout, mapped the building: the receiving area, the storage areas, the picking area and the dispatch area. This lesson moves goods through that map. In standard warehouse practice, goods travel through a fixed sequence of stages: receiving, then put-away into storage, then picking for orders, then packing, and finally dispatch to the customer. Each stage exists because of a specific question that must be answered before the goods may move on. Receiving asks: what actually arrived, and is it what we ordered? Put-away asks: where do these goods live, and does the record say so? Picking asks: which goods, exactly, does this customer's order require? Packing and dispatch ask: is the order complete and protected, and is it released to the right vehicle with the right documents?

The chain works only when each stage hands over cleanly to the next. An honest receiving record makes the stock records true. True stock records make the pick list trustworthy. A disciplined pick makes packing a confirmation rather than a hunt. Verified packing makes dispatch a controlled release instead of a gamble. Break any single handoff and the weakness travels: goods accepted without a count poison the stock records, poisoned records produce failed picks, failed picks produce short deliveries, and short deliveries produce complaints, returns and lost customers.

This whole outbound sequence sits inside a wider idea. Order fulfilment is the set of activities involved in processing, picking, packing and delivering goods to satisfy customer orders. Fulfilment can never be better than the receiving and put-away work that feeds it, because a warehouse cannot pick what its records cannot find. That dependence is this lesson's theme: the four processes are one unbroken chain, and running it well means respecting every handoff.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Goods Receiving Begins Before the Truck Arrives

BODY:
Goods receiving is the process of accepting, inspecting, verifying and recording goods arriving at a warehouse. The phrase begins before any vehicle opens its doors, because a receiving dock that is surprised by arrivals cannot check them properly.

1. Scheduling and dock allocation. Deliveries are arranged by appointment, and each vehicle is allocated a dock door and a time window. Scheduling prevents queues in the yard, spreads the workload across the day, and guarantees that staff and equipment are free when the truck arrives. Suppliers support this by sending advance notice of what is coming and when, so the receiving team knows the expected vehicle, quantity and arrival time before the journey begins.

2. Preparation. Before the vehicle is unloaded, the receiving team has the purchase order available for comparison, the unloading equipment suited to the goods, and clear space in the receiving area. Receiving a palletised food delivery without a pallet truck, or receiving machinery without the right lifting capacity, is how goods and people get hurt.

3. Unloading and staging. Goods are unloaded safely, with the vehicle secured so it cannot move during the work, and staged inside the receiving area. Nothing is signed, accepted or stored yet; the goods wait in receiving until they have been checked.

Routine work done under time pressure needs a standard. A standard operating procedure, or SOP, is a documented set of instructions describing how a routine activity should be performed consistently and safely. A receiving SOP states who may sign for goods, what must be counted, what must be inspected, how discrepancies are recorded and which documents are completed. It turns receiving from something every employee does their own way into one repeatable, checkable process, and it is the baseline for training new staff.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Verifying the Delivery: Right Goods, Right Quantity, Right Condition

BODY:
At the heart of receiving sits a comparison between three things: the purchase order, which says what was bought; the delivery note that travelled with the goods, which says what the supplier says was sent; and the goods themselves, which say what actually arrived. The receiving officer's job is to make all three agree before anything is accepted. This is the same discipline of checking one document against another taught in Lesson 6.4, Document Verification and Common Discrepancies, extended to its most important case, because here the third document is physical cargo.

The check answers three questions.

1. Right goods. Each delivered item is identified and compared against the purchase order lines. Identification relies on the stock keeping unit, or SKU, a unique identifier used to distinguish and manage a particular product or inventory item. Two cartons of similar appearance may hold different SKUs, and accepting an SKU the company never ordered clutters the warehouse with stock nobody wants.

2. Right quantity. Goods are physically counted: cartons, pallets, drums or units, whatever the pack configuration requires. The count is made from the goods, not from the paperwork. Counting what the delivery note says, instead of what the floor holds, is not a check at all.

3. Right condition. The goods are inspected for damage, crushed or wet cartons, broken seals, leakage, and, where relevant, correct temperature and intact packaging. Cold-chain goods that arrived warm, or bags of cement that arrived set solid, are condition failures even when the count is perfect.

When the three-way comparison fails, the result is a discrepancy: a shortage, an excess, a wrong item or damaged goods. The discrepancy is recorded at the dock, immediately, because the next concept shows what the receiving records are used for.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Recording Receipt: The Goods Received Note and Honest Paperwork

BODY:
What the dock proves, the record must preserve. The goods received note, or GRN, is the document or electronic record confirming the receipt of specified goods into a warehouse or organisation. A complete GRN states the date, the supplier, the purchase order reference, the SKUs received, the quantities actually counted, the condition observed, and any discrepancy between ordered, advised and received quantities, with the name of the person who received the goods.

The GRN does three jobs at once.

1. It updates the stock records. The goods enter official stock only from the moment the GRN is raised, so every later promise about availability rests on it.

2. It supports payment control. In standard buying practice, the supplier's invoice is paid only when three records agree: the purchase order, the goods received note and the invoice. This check is known as the three-way match. If the GRN honestly says 96 cartons were received against an order and invoice for 100, the company pays for 96, not for 100. A sloppy GRN therefore does not merely mislead the warehouse; it can cause the company to pay for goods it never received.

3. It is evidence. If a dispute arises weeks later about what was delivered, the GRN, made at the time by the person who was there, is the evidence that settles it.

Timing and honesty are everything here. The GRN is completed at the moment of receipt, not reconstructed from memory at the end of a shift. And it records what was actually received, even when that contradicts the delivery note. A signature on the receiving document transfers responsibility, so the officer signs for the counted quantity, and any difference is written on the record before the signature goes on, never after.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Three Doors at the Dock: Put Away, Cross-Dock, or Quarantine

BODY:
Once goods are verified and recorded, they are labelled and identified for their life inside the warehouse, typically with labels or barcodes carrying the SKU, and receiving decides their disposition. There are three doors out of the receiving area.

1. Put away to storage. The default door: goods move to their assigned storage locations and become part of available stock. This is the path most goods take, and the next concept treats it fully.

2. Cross-docking. Cross-docking is a logistics process in which incoming goods are transferred to outbound transportation with little or no intermediate storage, moving from the receiving dock directly towards the shipping dock, normally within about twenty-four hours. It suits goods that are already spoken for: pre-sold orders, promotional stock with a fixed launch date, or fast-moving lines arriving purely to replenish today's departures. Cross-docking saves the double handling of storage and speeds availability, but it demands precise coordination between inbound and outbound schedules, because there is no buffer of stored stock to absorb a mistake.

3. Quarantine or hold. Goods that cannot be accepted, damaged items, goods on a quality query, deliveries with incomplete documentation, go through the third door: a separate quarantine area, kept apart physically and in the system, until the question is resolved. Quarantined stock is not available stock, and mixing the two is how a warehouse sells goods it does not have, or ships goods it should have rejected.

The disposition decision belongs at receiving because every hour of delay distorts the picture. Goods left in receiving are owned but unavailable, recorded but unpickable, and in the way of the next delivery. Deciding the door immediately keeps the receiving area empty and the chain moving.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Put-Away: From Dock to Designated Storage Location

BODY:
Put-away is the process of moving received goods from the receiving area to their assigned storage locations. A storage location is a designated position within a warehouse where goods are kept, identified by a location code, for example an aisle, rack and level reference such as A-12-3, so that every pallet in the building has exactly one address.

In a warehouse using a warehouse management system, the software that serves as the system of record for what enters the warehouse, where it sits and what leaves, put-away is system-directed. The system proposes a location by applying rules: heavy goods stored low and light goods higher; fast-moving lines close to the forward picking area; goods needing cold, secure or segregated storage sent to the zones the previous lesson's layout study described. The put-away operator follows the instruction, places the goods, and confirms the move by scanning the location and the goods. The confirmation matters more than it looks: the move only becomes real in the records when the scan says the right SKU now sits at the right address. Confirming without placing, or placing without confirming, writes the first lie into the stock records.

Immediate put-away is a discipline, not a preference, because goods left on the receiving floor pay three penalties. They are invisible: until put-away is confirmed, the system cannot promise them to an order, so the company may hold stock and still miss sales. They are damage-prone: floor-stacked goods get clipped by traffic, double-handled, and crushed under whatever is placed on top. And they are unpickable: a picker sent to a storage location finds nothing, because the goods never arrived there.

Where goods are placed also serves stock rotation, deciding which quantity will be issued first; the rules of rotation are the subject of the next lesson, Inventory Control, FIFO, and FEFO.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Dock-to-Stock Time and Inventory Accuracy: The Two Measures

BODY:
How does a warehouse know whether its receiving and put-away pipeline is healthy? Two measures work together.

The first is speed. Dock-to-stock time is the performance measure covering the interval from a vehicle's arrival at the receiving dock to the moment the goods are recorded as pickable stock in their storage locations. It measures the whole inbound handoff: unload, check, record, move, confirm. The measure matters because of a simple commercial fact: goods cannot be sold, promised or picked until the system holds them as available stock. Every hour a delivery spends part-checked on the dock is an hour the company owns goods it cannot use, and slow dock-to-stock shows up downstream as availability failures the sales team cannot explain.

The second is truth. Inventory accuracy is the share of stock records that match the physical goods actually held. It is the output measure of every honest count, scan and GRN in this lesson. A warehouse can post an impressive dock-to-stock time by rushing, skipping counts and confirming put-aways that never happened, but the shortcut is charged to inventory accuracy, and the bill arrives later as failed picks and phantom stock.

The two measures must always be read together, because either one alone rewards the wrong behaviour. Speed without accuracy fills the system with comfortable lies. Accuracy without speed leaves goods stacked on the dock, owned but unusable. A disciplined receiving operation is measured by both: goods move from the dock to pickable locations quickly, and the record of where they sit is true. The remainder of the chain, picking, packing and dispatch, builds directly on that double achievement, which is why the inbound half of this lesson comes first.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Order Picking: The Pick List and the Pick Path

BODY:
Order picking is the process of selecting goods from warehouse storage locations to fulfil an order. It is, by most warehouses' experience, the most labour-intensive stage of the chain, and it is where the warehouse's accuracy is won or lost.

Picking is driven by the pick list, a document or electronic instruction identifying the goods and quantities to be selected for an order. In a system-directed warehouse, the pick list is built from the stock records and sequences the work as a pick path: an ordered route through the storage locations, item by item, so the picker walks the aisles once in a sensible order instead of criss-crossing the building. Walking is the largest single cost of picking time, so the pick path, the previous lesson's forward picking layout, and the methods discussed in the next concept all attack the same enemy: unnecessary travel.

Two checks guard accuracy at each pick. The picker confirms the location, by scan or positive identification, that the goods being taken come from the address the system named; and confirms the item, by SKU, that the goods taken are the goods listed. Two independent confirmations, right place and right product, stand between a disciplined warehouse and the classic error of picking from memory, in which an experienced picker trusts recognition over verification, until the day recognition is wrong.

Pick accuracy deserves to be called the crown jewel of warehouse performance, because a picking error is never private to the warehouse. The wrong item or the wrong quantity reaches the customer, generating returns, claims, credit notes, redelivery costs and, hardest to recover, lost confidence. Lesson 1.3, The Seven Rights of Logistics, listed the rights a delivery must satisfy; picking is the stage where the right product and the right quantity are physically decided for each order.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Four Picking Methods and When Each Suits

BODY:
Warehouses organise picking work in several recognised ways. Four methods cover most practice, and each exists to solve a particular problem of scale.

1. Discrete picking: one order is picked in full, in a single pass, by one picker. It is the simplest method to understand and control, and the natural choice for few large orders, urgent orders and heavy or bulky items that cannot share a trolley with another order's goods. Its weakness is travel: one pass per order means the picker re-walks the same aisles all day.

2. Batch picking: several orders are picked together in one pass, with the system keeping each order's items identified for separation at pack. Because the picker gathers the jointly-travelled lines of many orders at once, walking per order falls sharply. Batch picking suits the opposite profile from discrete: many small orders, each with only a few lines.

3. Zone picking: pickers are assigned their own zones and stay in them; each order travels from zone to zone and is completed by the pickers it passes. No picker walks the whole building, which suits large warehouses with long pick faces and many lines per order. The cost is coordination: orders must flow between zones without queuing.

4. Wave picking: orders are released for picking in scheduled groups, aligned to departure times, carrier cut-offs or workload peaks. Wave is less a route than a timing policy: it matches labour to the day's rhythm, so the warehouse is not picking at dawn what sails at dusk, nor drowning at the carrier cut-off.

Which method suits is decided by the order profile: how many orders per day, how many lines per order, how large and heavy the items, how urgent, and against which departure schedule the work must land. Real warehouses combine methods, releasing waves of batched orders across zones, and the combination follows the profile, not fashion.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Packing and Dispatch: The Last Free Look

BODY:
Packing is the process of preparing and protecting selected goods for storage, handling or transportation. The protective choice, carton or crate, strapping, wrap, cushioning, follows the cargo types and handling risks that Lesson 3.3, Cargo Types, Packaging, and Handling, introduced; packing differs for a domestic van journey, an ocean container, and fragile or dangerous goods.

But packing has a second, less obvious job: verification. Packing is the last point at which the order can be checked cheaply, before the goods are sealed, staged and loaded. At the pack bench, the contents are verified against the order: the right SKUs, the right quantities, the right condition. This is the last free look, the final opportunity to catch a wrong item or a short count while it costs a minute, rather than after it has become a delivery failure, a return, a claim and an apology. A packing slip listing the order's contents accompanies the goods, so the customer can perform the same verification at receipt.

Dispatch is the process of releasing and sending goods from a warehouse to another location or recipient. It begins in the dispatch area, where completed orders are staged by route or carrier, so each vehicle loads in reverse of its delivery sequence and nothing travels on the wrong truck. Loading follows a check: the right vehicle for the goods, the count of units loaded matching the documents, the load secured for the road, and, where seals are used, seal numbers recorded. The release is documented: a dispatch note, the document or electronic record identifying goods released from a warehouse for delivery or onward transportation, is raised, the delivery note travels with the goods for the customer to check against, and a record of proof of dispatch, what left, on which vehicle, loaded by whom and when, closes the warehouse's side of the chain.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Safe Hands and True Records: The Habits Beneath Every Stage

BODY:
Two habits run beneath all four processes, and both are the difference between a warehouse that lasts and one that accumulates accidents and mysteries.

The first habit is safety. Each stage carries its own risks: at receiving, moving vehicles, reversing trucks and dock edges; in storage and picking, forklifts sharing aisles with walking staff, unsafe stacking, and goods stored above head height; at packing and dispatch, manual handling injuries and vehicles loading at the dock. The countermeasures are specific: vehicles secured before loading or unloading, pedestrians and forklifts on separated routes, heavy goods stored low and racks not overloaded, correct lifting, and housekeeping that keeps aisles and docks clear. Safety is not a separate activity competing with throughput; an injury stops the entire chain, and near-misses are its warning.

The second habit is recordkeeping. Every physical movement in the chain has an information shadow: goods received, goods put away, goods moved, goods picked, goods dispatched. The rule is that the record is made when the movement happens, by the person doing it, through the GRN, the put-away confirmation, the pick confirmation and the dispatch note. Records written from memory at the end of a shift are not records; they are recollections, and recollections are where phantom stock is born.

The standard operating procedure, introduced at the start of this lesson, is what carries both habits, because it writes the safe and honest way of working down, trains it, and audits it. The common errors this lesson warns against are all departures from that written way: signing for an uncounted delivery, skipping the put-away confirmation, picking from memory, packing without checking, dispatching without documents. Each seems faster; each poisons a different stage downstream.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: One Order's Journey Through a Tema Distribution Centre

BODY:
A distribution centre in Tema supplies retail shops across Greater Accra with fast-moving consumer goods. On Monday morning it receives a delivery of 400 cartons of cooking oil, and that afternoon it serves an order from a retailer in Madina. Following one order through the whole chain shows every handoff and its control. All quantities, times and locations in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: Before arrival. The supplier's delivery was booked by appointment for 09:00 at Dock 3. The receiving desk has the purchase order, for 400 cartons of one cooking-oil SKU, ready for comparison.

Step 2: Unload and verify. The vehicle is secured and unloaded. The team counts 400 cartons, checks them against the delivery note and the purchase order, and inspects condition: cartons dry, seals intact, labels readable. Goods, note and order all agree.

Step 3: Record the receipt. The receiving officer raises the GRN for 400 cartons, notes the condition as acceptable, and only then signs the supplier's delivery note. The stock records now show the warehouse holding 400 cartons.

Step 4: Put away. The system directs the pallets to storage location A-12-3. The operator scans the location, scans the goods, and confirms. The dock-to-stock clock stops before midday: the cartons are now pickable stock.

Step 5: The order arrives. At 13:00 the system releases a pick list: 24 cartons of the cooking oil SKU for the Madina retailer, sequenced along the pick path with other orders.

Step 6: Pick. The picker walks the path to A-12-3, scans the location, scans the item, and takes 24 cartons. The checks pass: right place, right product, right quantity.

Step 7: Pack. At the pack bench, the contents are verified against the order: 24 cartons, correct SKU, sound condition. The last free look passes. A packing slip goes inside; the cartons are strapped to a pallet.

Step 8: Stage and dispatch. The pallet is staged in the Accra route lane. The dispatch note is raised, the delivery note is placed with the driver, and the loading check confirms the count and secures the load. The proof of dispatch record closes the warehouse's part of the journey.

What does this example demonstrate?

Eight stages, eight controls: appointment and dock allocation, the three-way check, the GRN before the signature, the put-away confirmation, the pick list with scans, the pack verification, and the loading check with its documents. The order succeeded not because any stage was heroic, but because every handoff was confirmed before the goods moved on.

Key lesson: a warehouse is only as strong as its weakest handoff, and every handoff has a named control.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Ninety-Six Cartons Out of a Hundred: The Same Shortage, Handled Two Ways

BODY:
Two branches of a pharmaceutical distributor in Accra each expect a delivery of 100 cartons of a pain-relief medicine. At both docks, the driver's manifest says 100 cartons, but only 96 are on the vehicle. All quantities and figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Branch A, the shortage handled at the dock. The receiving officer counts 96, compares against the delivery note and the purchase order, and informs the driver before any paperwork is signed. The delivery note is annotated: 96 of 100 cartons received, 4 short. The GRN records 96 with the shortage stated, and a photograph of the loaded vehicle supports it. The officer signs for 96. The same day, the branch sends the supplier the annotated note and the GRN. The supplier confirms a short-shipment from its own records and delivers the missing 4 cartons within the week. The branch's stock record said 96 throughout, and the record was true.

Branch B, the shortage discovered late. Under pressure from a queue of vehicles, the receiving officer signs the manifest clean for 100 cartons, intending to raise the difference with the supplier by email afterwards. The system records 100. Two days later a pharmacy customer orders 30 cartons; the pick list is confident, but the storage location holds only 96. A day is spent searching the warehouse, recounting and investigating. The branch short-ships the customer, restates its stock records, and submits a shortage claim, which the supplier now contests: the signed delivery note says 100 cartons were received clean. The claim weakens exactly because the document that should prove the shortage was signed as if none existed.

What does this example demonstrate?

The shortage was identical; the price was not. Branch A spent minutes at the dock and recovered its goods within the week, with records that stayed true. Branch B signed away its evidence at the dock and paid twice: once in a failed pick and a short-shipped customer, and again in a claim it could no longer prove. Late discovery is not free discovery; it is discovery without leverage, without time and without a truthful record.

Key lesson: record the discrepancy at the dock, before the signature, because the signature is the moment the evidence is fixed.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Choosing a Picking Method: A Wholesaler in Rotterdam and a Furniture Warehouse in Kumasi

BODY:
Picking methods are chosen from the order profile: how many orders, how many lines per order, how big and heavy the items, how urgent, and against which departures. Two very different warehouses show the reasoning. All quantities in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

A pharmaceutical wholesaler in Rotterdam supplies several hundred independent pharmacies. Its profile: hundreds of orders a day, each with only three to five lines, mostly small, light items. If each order were picked discretely, a picker would walk the same aisles hundreds of times a day, and walking, not picking, would consume the shift. The wholesaler therefore uses batch picking: several orders are combined into one pass along the pick path, the picker gathers the lines once, and the system keeps each order's items identified for separation at the pack bench. Walking per order falls sharply, and the pack bench verification keeps the orders from blurring into one another. The few urgent same-day orders still run discretely, because one picker on one order is the fastest and most controllable path for a rush.

A furniture warehouse in Kumasi serves a different world: about ten orders a day, but each order is large, dozens of bulky, heavy items for a single customer such as a hotel furnishing its rooms. Batch picking is impractical: a picker cannot gather another order's beds and wardrobes while collecting this one's, and mixing two customers' furniture on one trolley invites chaos at pack. The Kumasi warehouse picks discretely: one order, one picker team, one complete pass, item by item against the pick list. Travel per order is high, but there are few orders; accuracy and completeness, which matter most when a customer is waiting for a full consignment, are easiest to control one order at a time. When the warehouse adds scheduled deliveries to distant towns, it may borrow a timing idea from the method set: releasing orders in waves aligned to each departure, so picking finishes when the truck must load.

What does this example demonstrate?

The method follows the order profile, not fashion or habit. Many small orders reward batching; few large ones reward discrete control; large buildings point to zones; fixed departure schedules point to waves.

Key lesson: before choosing how to pick, count your orders, weigh your items, and read your departure board.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Signed Clean Is Signed Lost: Never Sign Against a Known Discrepancy

BODY:
The most expensive signature in warehousing is the one placed on a clean document while a known discrepancy stands unrecorded. The delivery note or manifest signed at the dock is not a courtesy; it is the record against which every later claim is judged. Sign it for 100 cartons when 96 arrived, and you have attested, in a document the supplier holds too, that 100 arrived. The email you send afterwards, the phone call, the honest memory of the receiving officer, all of it now argues with a signed statement, and signed statements win. This is the trap summarised in four words: signed-then-queried is signed-then-lost.

The discipline that prevents it costs minutes. Count before signing. Compare before signing. If the counts disagree, write the discrepancy on the delivery note, state it on the GRN, and sign for what was actually received, with the driver aware. A receiving officer who feels pressured by a driver in a hurry should remember whose risk each signature carries: the driver's schedule is the supplier's concern, but the shortage, once signed clean, becomes the receiver's loss to absorb and almost impossible to recover.

The same principle extends down the chain wherever a signature or confirmation transfers responsibility: a put-away confirmed for goods not placed, a loading check ticked for a count not made, a proof of dispatch raised for goods not verified. Every one of them is a clean document standing in front of a dirty fact. Record the truth first, sign second, and the evidence will be on your side when the query comes, and in this trade, the query always comes.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: The Pick Face Lies Unless Put-Away Is Disciplined

BODY:
Beginners imagine that a picking error means a careless picker. Experienced warehouse managers check upstream first, because of an uncomfortable truth: the pick face, the storage locations from which pickers draw goods, tells the truth only if put-away is disciplined. The pick list is generated from the stock records, not from the shelves. What the system says is in location A-12-3 must physically be in A-12-3, or the picker sent there finds nothing, finds the wrong thing, or finds the right thing in the wrong quantity.

Every skipped confirmation writes a lie into those records. A pallet placed on the floor without put-away leaves the system blind to goods that exist. A pallet placed in the wrong location, or in a location while the record names another, leaves the system pointing at an empty address. An unrecorded internal move, a damaged carton quietly set aside, a carton borrowed for an urgent order and never returned to the record, each one is a divergence between the shelf and the system, and the picker inherits all of them. The failed pick looks like a picking problem; it is a put-away problem wearing a picking costume.

This is why this lesson teaches the chain as one chain. Pick accuracy is not created at the pick face; it is inherited from the honesty of every confirmation made before the first pick list of the day was printed. When picks start failing, the professional question is not only who picked wrong, but where the record and the shelf first parted company, and the answer is usually found at receiving or put-away.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Accuracy Beats Speed at Every Stage

BODY:
Every stage of this lesson's chain can be rushed, and every stage gets worse when it is. A delivery counted in two minutes instead of twenty produces a clean-looking record nobody trusts. A put-away confirmed without the scan saves a keystroke and loses a pallet. A pick taken from memory saves a scan and ships the wrong item. A pack bench that skips verification ships the error to the customer, the one place errors cost the most. In each case the minutes saved are real but tiny, and the failure purchased with them is large: returns, claims, credit notes, redeliveries, a customer who orders elsewhere next time.

Lesson 1.3, The Seven Rights of Logistics, gives the standard for judging the trade-off. A fast wrong order fails the right product or the right quantity or the right condition, and a failed right is a service failure however quickly it arrived. A slower right order satisfies every right, and satisfies the customer. Speed is genuinely valuable, dock-to-stock time and pick rates are honest measures, but only in partnership with accuracy. That is why the professional reads every speed measure together with its accuracy partner: dock-to-stock time with inventory accuracy, pick rate with pick accuracy, dispatch volume with claims.

The practical rule for a learner is simple to state and career-defining to keep. Never trade a certain error for a possible saving of time. When the queue is long and the pressure is on, the counts, the scans and the checks are not the bureaucracy slowing the chain down; they are the chain.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A distributor in Takoradi expects a delivery of 60 cartons of wall tiles. While unloading, the receiving officer counts only 54 cartons, although the driver's delivery note states 60. The driver says he is in a hurry and asks for the paperwork to be signed so he can leave.

Which action should the receiving officer take before signing?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Sign for the full 60 cartons to keep the documents consistent with the supplier's delivery note, and report the shortage to the supplier by email afterwards.
2. Count and record 54 cartons as received, annotate the delivery note and the goods received note with the shortage of 6 cartons, and confirm this with the driver before signing for 54.
3. Refuse the entire delivery and send all 54 cartons back to the supplier with the driver.
4. Accept the 54 cartons and reduce the purchase order to 54 cartons so that all documents agree.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The receiving record must state what was actually received, and any discrepancy must be written on the documents before the signature transfers responsibility. Recording 54 cartons, annotating the delivery note and the GRN with the shortage, and confirming with the driver preserves the evidence the distributor will need to recover the missing goods, exactly as the Accra worked example demonstrated. Signing for 54 is honest, and it is also commercially strong: the supplier's own records can be reconciled against an annotated document within days.

Answer 1 commits the error this lesson's first important note dismantles: signed-then-queried is signed-then-lost, because a clean signature for 60 cartons contradicts every later claim the distributor makes. Answer 3 overreacts; a shortage of 6 cartons is resolved by recording and claiming, not by rejecting goods the distributor genuinely ordered and can use. Answer 4 confuses records with reality; the purchase order belongs to the buying process and is not rewritten at the dock to absorb a delivery failure.

The takeaway: sign for what the floor holds, write the difference down first, and let the honest document win the claim.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
A furniture warehouse in Kumasi handles about ten customer orders a day. Each order is large, often dozens of bulky and heavy items such as beds, wardrobes and sofas, all for a single customer who is waiting for a complete consignment.

Which statement best describes the picking approach best suited to this profile?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Discrete picking, one order picked in full by one picker or team per pass, because there are few orders, the items cannot practically be combined for different customers, and completeness is easiest to control one order at a time.
2. Batch picking, several orders combined into one pass, because combining ten heavy furniture orders in a single trolley run minimises the picker's walking distance above all other considerations.
3. Zone picking, because assigning pickers to fixed zones guarantees that no order will ever be picked incorrectly, whatever the size of the items.
4. Wave picking, because releasing all ten orders in one group at the start of the day removes the need for pick lists or item verification.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

The order profile decides the method, and this profile, few orders, many bulky lines each, one waiting customer per order, is the natural home of discrete picking. With only about ten passes a day, walking distance is not the binding constraint, while accuracy and completeness are; one order per picker against the pick list keeps both visible, and heavy furniture items cannot share a pass with another customer's goods without inviting chaos at pack, as the Kumasi worked example showed.

Answer 2 applies batch logic to the wrong profile; batching exists to cut walking when there are many small orders, and merging heavy items from different customers' orders creates separation problems that outweigh any travel saved. Answer 3 exaggerates what zones do; zone picking organises travel in large buildings with many lines per order, it does not guarantee accuracy, and it adds handoff coordination rather than removing the need for checks. Answer 4 misunderstands waves entirely; a wave is a timing policy for releasing groups of orders, and no picking method removes the pick list or the checks at pick.

The takeaway: read the order profile first, orders, lines, item size and urgency, then choose the method that fits it.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
At a busy warehouse, the receiving team unloads 200 cartons of household goods and leaves them stacked on the receiving area floor, planning to put them away whenever there is time. The put-away confirmations have not been made, and pickers are already asking where the goods are.

Which statement best explains the operational problem with leaving the cartons on the floor?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. No problem exists, because goods become available for picking the moment the delivery vehicle has been unloaded and counted.
2. The only issue is housekeeping, since the cartons remain fully usable and the stock records are unaffected until the next stock count.
3. Until put-away is completed and confirmed, the goods are invisible to the system and cannot be picked reliably, and while they sit on the floor they face damage and the stock records risk drifting from reality.
4. The cartons should remain on the floor permanently, because floor storage is safer than racking for cartoned household goods.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The chain only advances when each handoff is completed and confirmed. Put-away moves goods from the receiving area to a designated storage location, and the confirmation scan is what makes the goods visible as pickable stock in the system. Until then the warehouse owns 200 cartons it cannot promise, cannot list and cannot pick, which is precisely why pickers are asking in vain. The floor dwell carries the other penalties this lesson taught as well: floor-stacked goods are damage-prone and obstruct the next delivery, and dock-to-stock time is stretching with every hour, while the gap between what exists and what the system shows is where record drift begins.

Answer 1 confuses physical arrival with system availability; goods are available when put away and confirmed, not when the truck is empty. Answer 2 underestimates the problem as housekeeping; the record and availability effects are real, and the stock count, if one comes, will only surface the confusion later. Answer 4 is simply false; racking exists precisely to protect cartoned goods, and floor storage is the exception, not the rule.

The takeaway: put away immediately and confirm the move, because goods the system cannot see are goods the business cannot sell.
