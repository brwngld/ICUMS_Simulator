=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Inventory Control and Its Three Questions
BODY:
Every warehouse exists because a business holds goods it is not using yet. These goods are its inventory, meaning the goods and materials held by an organisation for production, operational use, distribution, or sale. A bakery's flour, a distributor's cartons of cooking oil, a hardware merchant's cement and a garage's spare parts are all inventory.

Two broader terms describe how this stock is run, and beginners often merge them. Inventory management is the planning, monitoring, and control of inventory to maintain appropriate stock availability and minimise unnecessary costs: the managerial questions of how much stock to hold, how much to buy, and when. Inventory control is the procedures and systems used to track, safeguard, and maintain accurate records of stock: the operational discipline of counting what arrives, recording what leaves, keeping storage orderly, and keeping the book quantity and the physical quantity in agreement. Management decides; control makes the records truthful enough for those decisions to stand on. A business with strong purchasing judgement but weak control is a business deciding on fiction, because its choices rest on records that do not match the shelf.

Strip away the paperwork and every stock item lives under three questions.

1. How much should we order? Order too little and the cost and effort of ordering are repeated again and again; order too much and capital sits on the shelf while goods age, expire, or go out of fashion. The classic answer, the economic order quantity, is taught later in this lesson.

2. When should we order? An order placed too late arrives after the shelf is empty; placed too early, it lands on top of stock that has not yet been used. The reorder point is the trigger mechanism for this question, built from two ideas defined later in this lesson: the lead time and the safety stock.

3. Which units should we issue? The shelf may hold identical units that arrived on different dates or carry different expiry dates. The rotation method, first in, first out or first expired, first out, answers which one is picked.

Every other topic in this lesson supports one of these three, and a useful habit is to ask of every inventory procedure which of the three questions it serves.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Categories of Inventory
BODY:
Businesses hold several kinds of inventory, and the category a good belongs to depends on what the holder does with it. The common categories are the following.

1. Raw materials. Inputs held awaiting production or processing. Flour at a bakery, timber at a furniture workshop, and empty bottles at a bottling plant are raw materials: their purpose is to be transformed, not resold as they are.

2. Work in progress. Goods part-way through production. A chair assembled but not yet varnished, or juice blended but not yet bottled, has left the raw material store but has not reached the finished goods shelf.

3. Finished goods. Completed products held awaiting sale or distribution. The bottled water stacked at a distributor's warehouse and the bagged cement at a hardware shop are finished goods to their holders.

4. Maintenance, repair, and operating supplies. Items that are not sold but keep the operation running: forklift spare parts, lubricants, cleaning materials, packaging stock, and office consumables. These are easily overlooked because they never appear on a customer's invoice, yet a production line can stop for want of a single inexpensive bearing.

The same physical good can sit in different categories in different hands. A bag of cement is a finished good at the plant that made it, a raw material at a block factory, and resale stock at the hardware shop. The category matters because each kind is planned differently: raw materials follow the production schedule, finished goods follow sales demand, and operating supplies follow the maintenance calendar. Inventory control keeps a separate, accurate record of each.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Stock Levels and the Inventory Record
BODY:
The stock level is the quantity of a particular inventory item available or recorded at a given time. A particular item: records are kept per item, not in one undifferentiated pile, which is why a storekeeper can state the cement position without counting anything. And available or recorded: the recorded level and the truly available level can drift apart, and keeping them together is the central discipline of this lesson.

The inventory record is where the stock level lives. A usable record carries, for each item, the following: an item code and description; the unit of measure, so that cartons are never silently confused with pieces; the quantity on hand; the storage location; the value; and, where relevant, the batch number and expiry date explained in the next concept. Each movement updates the record, so the quantity on hand is the sum of everything recorded in minus everything recorded out.

Why does record accuracy carry so much weight? Four reasons.

1. Orders are promised against the record. Sales teams confirm customer orders from the recorded stock level; if the record overstates the shelf, the business sells goods it does not have.

2. Reordering runs on the record. Reorder points trigger when recorded stock falls to a defined level; an inaccurate record triggers orders too late or too early.

3. Discrepancies stay invisible. If the record is loosely maintained, the gap between book and shelf grows silently until a stocktake exposes it, by which time the cause is hard to trace.

4. Rotation depends on the record. Choosing the first-in or the first-expired unit is only possible when arrival dates, batches, and expiry dates were captured correctly.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Stock Movements: Receipts, Issues, Transfers, and Adjustments
BODY:
Inventory balances move in only four ways, and each should leave a trace in the record.

1. Receipts. Goods entering stock, from a supplier, from the factory, or as customer returns. A receipt increases the balance of the item. Receiving is also where the physical work taught in Lesson 8.2, Receiving, Put-Away, Picking, and Dispatch, becomes an information event: the item, the quantity, the unit of measure and, for dated goods, the batch number and expiry date are checked against the supplier's documents and entered into the record. A batch number is an identifier assigned to a group of goods produced or processed under specified conditions; the expiry date is the date after which a product should no longer be used or distributed under its applicable requirements. Captured at the gate, these two facts power every rotation decision made afterwards.

2. Issues. Goods leaving stock, whether to a customer order, to production, or for internal use. An issue decreases the balance, and the record should name which batch and which location the goods came from.

3. Transfers. Goods moving between locations without leaving the business: from a warehouse in Accra to a branch in Tamale, or from reserve racking to the forward picking area. A transfer decreases one location's balance and increases another's, leaving the business total unchanged. Stock moved physically but never recorded is one of the commonest causes of discrepancy, because the shelf says one thing and the record another.

4. Adjustments. Corrections made when a count shows the record and the shelf disagree, or when goods are written off as damaged or expired. Adjustments are deliberate, documented acts taken after investigation, not a quiet way of making the numbers agree; Lesson 8.4, Warehouse Documentation and Stock Reconciliation, treats them fully.

The rule is symmetry: every physical movement appears in the record, and every record entry corresponds to a real movement.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: FIFO: First In, First Out
BODY:
First in, first out, abbreviated to FIFO, is an inventory rotation method in which goods received earlier are issued or used before goods received later. The first pallet onto the rack is the first pallet off it. The method exists to prevent stock from ageing in place: if the oldest units always leave first, nothing lingers long enough to deteriorate, fade, go out of fashion, or lose value in a corner.

FIFO works only when the physical arrangement supports it. New receipts are put away so that the older stock faces the picker: behind it, beneath it, or in the next reachable position. Where goods are stacked loose and new deliveries dumped in front of old ones, FIFO is silently abolished, however faithfully the computer system believes in it. The method also depends on the record: the arrival date or receipt sequence of each lot must have been captured at receiving, because the instruction to take the oldest lot comes from that data.

Why is FIFO the general default? Because almost everything deteriorates or dates eventually, even without an expiry date printed on it. Cement held for very long periods can absorb moisture and lose strength. Electronics, phone accessories, and printed packaging become obsolete. Even where these pressures are weak, issuing the oldest unit first keeps stock rotating, keeps the offer to customers fresh, and prevents the slow accumulation of unsellable stock that must eventually be written off. For any product with no expiry date, FIFO is the natural standing rule; and even for dated products it remains the foundation on which the stricter rule builds.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: FEFO: First Expired, First Out
BODY:
First expired, first out, abbreviated to FEFO, is an inventory rotation method in which goods with the earliest expiry dates are issued or used first. For any product whose fitness is governed by a date, medicines, food, cosmetics, and certain chemicals among them, the expiry date, not the arrival date, is the fact that governs the issue decision. The unit that leaves first is the unit that will expire first, whichever delivery it arrived with.

FEFO runs on the two facts captured at receiving: the batch number and the expiry date. When a customer order arrives, the record identifies the batch with the earliest expiry date that is still within its usable period, and that batch is picked, even if a newer delivery sits in front of it on the rack. In a system-driven warehouse the selection is automatic; in a manual storeroom it is a written rule backed by disciplined shelving and dated labels.

Because dates keep passing, a FEFO storeroom also runs an expiry sweep: a recurring walk of the shelves, commonly monthly, that finds every batch approaching its expiry and does something deliberate with it. The batch may be prioritised for issue, offered back to the supplier where an agreement allows, or removed for disposal under the applicable requirements. The sweep turns FEFO from a picking rule into a waste-prevention system: it catches short-dated stock that demand will not absorb in time.

An expired unit of medicine is not merely a missed sale. It is the cost of the goods written off, a disposal task, and a potential safety incident if it slips through. FEFO exists so that no unit expires on the shelf while a longer-dated unit leaves first.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Choosing Between FIFO and FEFO, and the Place of LIFO
BODY:
FIFO orders the shelf by arrival date; FEFO orders it by expiry date. The two methods give the same answer whenever the earliest arrival is also the earliest to expire, which is the natural situation when every delivery carries fresh production and a uniform shelf life. They diverge whenever expiry order and arrival order disagree, and real supply chains disagree often: a supplier clears short-dated stock at a discount; an imported consignment spends months in transit and arrives with much of its shelf life already spent; production runs differ in their date coding. In any of these cases the short-dated batch may be the newer arrival, and pure FIFO would place a long-dated batch in front of it, exactly the situation FEFO prevents. The choice rule is simple: goods with expiry dates run FEFO; goods without them run FIFO as the general default.

The third abbreviation, LIFO, last in, first out, belongs here so that it is not confused with the other two. As a physical rotation method, issuing the newest arrivals first, LIFO suits almost no goods: it strands the oldest stock at the back of the warehouse to age, deteriorate, and eventually expire or obsolesce there. LIFO is actually an accounting cost-flow convention: a bookkeeping assumption that the latest units purchased are the ones whose cost is charged against sales first. It is associated chiefly with United States accounting practice and is not permitted as a cost-flow method under international financial reporting standards. The accounting question of which cost to attach to a sale is different from the warehouse question of which physical unit to hand the picker, and a business may answer them differently without inconsistency. The accounts may run last in, first out; the shelves do not.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: ABC Analysis: Control Effort Follows Value
BODY:
No store can give every item the same attention, and ABC analysis is the tool that says it should not try. The method classifies inventory items by their value contribution and then matches the control effort to the class. It is an application of the Pareto principle, the pattern that a small share of causes accounts for a large share of results: in inventory terms, a small share of the items usually holds most of the value.

The classes are the following.

1. A items. The few items that carry most of the value. They receive the tightest control: frequent cycle counts, secure and well-organised storage, close monitoring of stock levels and reorder points, and management attention the moment anything looks wrong.

2. B items. The middle band: more numerous, worth less per item and in total. Control is regular but lighter; counting and review are less frequent.

3. C items. The many items that carry little value: washers, labels, low-priced consumables. Control is deliberately loose, with simple rules, larger and less frequent orders, and infrequent counts, because elaborate control over a cheap item costs more than the item is worth.

ABC analysis connects directly to stocktaking practice. Counting an entire store in one event is disruptive and leaves the rest of the year in silence. Cycle counting replaces the single annual event: a subset of items is counted on a continuous rotating schedule, with A items counted most often and C items least often, and each surprise investigated while the trail is fresh.

The class percentages vary from business to business, and none is a law. The worked example later in this lesson shows one spare-parts store in which a twelfth of the items carried more than three-quarters of the value, and allocated its counting and security effort accordingly.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: When to Order: Lead Time, Safety Stock, and the Reorder Point
BODY:
The second of the three questions, when to order, has a precise answer built from three ideas.

The first is lead time: the time between initiating a replenishment order and receiving the required goods. Lead time is not only transport. It includes order processing, the supplier's preparation and handling, transit, and, across borders, customs clearance. A retailer whose supplier needs two weeks has a two-week lead time.

The second is the demand during that lead time. Between placing an order and receiving it, the business keeps selling or consuming from the same shrinking shelf. Expected lead-time demand is the average usage multiplied by the lead time: two weeks of demand for a two-week lead.

The third is safety stock: additional inventory maintained to reduce the risk of shortages caused by unexpected demand or supply delays. It exists because averages lie about individual weeks. Heavy orders, a broken-down truck, a supplier hiccup, any of these can push demand or lead time past the average, and a business holding exactly the average stocks out the first time reality runs warm. Safety stock is the buffer for those weeks. How much to hold is a management choice about service level, the chosen probability of not stocking out. A higher service level requires disproportionately more safety stock: pushing protection from good to nearly perfect costs far more than the first layer did.

The three ideas combine in the reorder point: the inventory level at which replenishment should be initiated under a defined stock control policy. In its standard form:

Reorder point = expected demand during lead time + safety stock.

For steady demand this equals average weekly usage times the lead time in weeks, plus the safety stock. When the recorded stock level falls to the reorder point, the order is placed, so that the remaining stock, buffer included, covers demand until the delivery arrives. The next worked example applies this to a hardware retailer.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: How Much to Order: The Economic Order Quantity
BODY:
The reorder point answers when to order. The third question, how much, has a classic textbook answer in the economic order quantity, usually abbreviated to EOQ. The model weighs two costs that pull in opposite directions. Ordering cost is the administrative and delivery cost incurred each time an order is placed, roughly fixed per order: paperwork, receiving work, transport. Holding cost is the annual cost of keeping one unit in stock: the capital tied up in it, the storage space, insurance, and the risk of damage, expiry, or obsolescence. Order in large quantities and orders become rare, but the shelf fills and holding cost climbs. Order in small quantities and the shelf stays lean, but the orders crowd the calendar. Somewhere between lies the quantity that minimises the total of the two.

The classic formula gives that quantity as the square root of two times D times S, divided by H, where D is the annual demand in units, S is the fixed cost of placing one order, and H is the annual holding cost of one unit. The cement worked example computes it in full.

The formula's honesty lives in its assumptions. It assumes demand is constant and known. It assumes the full order arrives in one delivery, at the moment stock reaches zero. It assumes each order costs the same to place. And it assumes no quantity discounts and no stockouts. Real life complicates each: demand is seasonal, suppliers deliver in instalments, bulk discounts tempt larger orders. The EOQ is therefore taught as the classic model: a reasoned starting point that shows why order sizes have a logic, not a law that settles the question. Users treat the result as a reference point, adjusted for the realities of their trade and reviewed when those realities change.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Stockouts, Overstocking, Discrepancies, and the Health of Stock
BODY:
Three failure modes sit on either side of every inventory decision, and two health measures show which way the business is drifting.

1. Stockouts. A stockout is a situation in which an inventory item is unavailable when required. The visible cost is the missed sale or the stopped job; behind it follow emergency purchases at unfavourable prices, expedited transport, idle production lines, and customers who conclude the supplier cannot be relied upon. Because the reorder point and safety stock exist to prevent stockouts, every stockout is evidence of a control failure: a wrong record, an ignored trigger, or a buffer reality outmatched.

2. Overstocking. Overstocking is holding inventory quantities that exceed reasonable operational requirements. It feels safe and is quietly expensive: capital parked on racking, storage and insurance costs growing, goods ageing or passing expiry dates in storage, and discrepancies hiding longer in a crowded warehouse. For dated goods, overstocking converts directly into the expiry sweep's casualty list.

3. Inventory discrepancies. A discrepancy is a difference between the recorded stock level and the physical stock found by a count. Its causes are ordinary: recording errors, unrecorded movements and transfers, mis-picks, unit-of-measure confusion, damage nobody logged, and theft. The discipline is to investigate before adjusting the record, so the cause is corrected rather than papered over; Lesson 8.4, Warehouse Documentation and Stock Reconciliation, takes that discipline further.

Inventory turnover, a measure of how frequently inventory is sold or used and replaced during a specified period, is calculated as the cost of goods sold divided by the average inventory: the higher the figure, the faster capital cycles through the stock. Days of supply divides average inventory by average daily usage to show how many days the current stock would cover. High turnover means capital is working; pushed too high, it means shelves run thin and stockout risk rises, and the safety stock and service level decisions are where that balance is set deliberately.

This is the payoff: with accurate records, captured movements, protected quality and punctual triggers, the warehouse picks faster and promises reliably, and inventory control becomes the quiet engine of warehouse efficiency and customer service.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A Pharmacy Storeroom Runs FEFO
BODY:
Consider the medicine storeroom of a private clinic in Kumasi. The clinic dispenses about twenty cartons of a common antibiotic, amoxicillin, in a typical month. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

In January the storeroom receives Batch A: 100 cartons, expiring in March of the following year. In April it receives Batch B: 80 cartons of the same medicine at a good discount, because the supplier is clearing a short-dated lot, expiring at the end of September of the same year.

Step 1: Capture at receiving. The pharmacist checks each delivery against the supplier's documents and records, for each batch, the batch number, the quantity, and the expiry date. The record now shows two batches with different dates.

Step 2: Set the rule. The storeroom's standing rule is FEFO. Because the medicines carry expiry dates, the expiry date, not the arrival date, decides what is dispensed.

Step 3: Issue by earliest expiry. From April, every dispensing takes Batch B, even though Batch A arrived first. Batch B's 80 cartons cover the roughly four months from April to July, so the short-dated batch is exhausted by the end of July, two clear months before its September expiry. Batch A then resumes and runs comfortably towards its March expiry the following year. Nothing expires; the discount is fully earned.

Step 4: Sweep monthly. At each month-end the pharmacist walks the shelves, checking that no batch is drifting towards its date faster than demand is moving it. The sweep is a physical check of the labels, not just a reading of the screen.

What would FIFO alone have got wrong? A strict first-in, first-out rule would have continued dispensing Batch A until it ran out in May, and only then started Batch B, with exactly the four months from June to September to move 80 cartons. One quiet month, and several cartons of amoxicillin pass their dates on the shelf: medicine written off, disposal costs incurred, and a safety risk had any slipped out.

What does this example demonstrate?

When goods carry expiry dates, the date governs the issue order, and the record must hold honest batch and expiry data for the rule to act on. FEFO plus a recurring sweep is what stands between short-dated stock and the write-off bin.

Key lesson: for dated goods, first expired, first out; the arrival order is the wrong master.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: The Reorder Decision at a Cement Hardware Retailer
BODY:
A hardware shop in Takoradi sells about 120 bags of cement in a typical week, roughly steady through the year. Its supplier needs two weeks from the placing of an order to delivery. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

The owner has three numbers to find: when to order, how much to order, and what buffer to hold.

Step 1: Lead-time demand. Two weeks of demand at 120 bags a week means 240 bags will be sold between placing the order and the truck's arrival. Stock held when the order is placed must survive this period.

Step 2: Safety stock. Demand is not perfectly steady: site pours and month-end building pushes make some weeks heavier, and trucks run late. The owner chooses a buffer of 100 bags, judged enough to absorb an unusually heavy week or a delivery a few days late. This buffer size is a service-level choice: a shop tolerating almost no stockout holds more; one watching its capital holds less.

Step 3: The reorder point. Applying the formula:

Reorder point = lead-time demand + safety stock = 240 + 100 = 340 bags.

When the recorded stock of cement falls to 340 bags, the owner places the order. Above that level, ordering early would pile unsold stock on the racking; below it, the shop is gambling that no heavy week or late truck intervenes.

Step 4: How much to order. Each order costs the owner about 250 Ghana cedis in administration and delivery, and holding a bag for a year costs about 12 Ghana cedis in capital, space, and damage risk. Annual demand is roughly 6,000 bags. The economic order quantity is the square root of two times 6,000 times 250, divided by 12:

EOQ = square root of (2 x 6,000 x 250 / 12) = square root of 250,000 = 500 bags.

The owner therefore orders 500 bags about twelve times a year (6,000 divided by 500), each time when the shelf falls to 340 bags.

What does this example demonstrate?

The when and how much of inventory control are three numbers a small business can keep: a lead-time demand from its own sales, a safety stock matched to its appetite for risk, and an order quantity balancing ordering cost against holding cost. The EOQ's assumptions remind the owner that demand is not truly constant, so the 500 bags are a reference point, reviewed as sales and costs shift, not a permanent instruction.

Key lesson: find the lead-time demand, choose the buffer honestly, and let the reorder point fire the order.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: ABC Analysis in Action at a Spare-Parts Distribution Centre
BODY:
Consider a spare-parts distribution centre in Rotterdam supplying industrial customers across Europe. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

The centre holds about 4,000 stock items, and ranking them by annual value contribution produces a lopsided picture.

Step 1: Rank and classify. About 12 per cent of the items, some 480 of them, account for 78 per cent of the total value held. These become the A items: motors, drives, and high-value electronic units. A further 26 per cent of the items carry about 17 per cent of the value: the B items. The remaining 62 per cent of the items, the washers, seals, labels, and small fasteners, carry barely 5 per cent of the value: the C items.

Step 2: Match control effort to class. The A items live in a secured, well-organised section, are cycle counted monthly, and have stock levels and reorder points reviewed weekly by a senior buyer. The B items are counted quarterly under standard controls. The C items are counted once or twice a year and replenished by simple rules in larger, less frequent orders, because a complicated control routine over a cheap fastener would cost more than the fastener itself. One counterfeit or misplaced motor is a catastrophe; one missing washer is a Tuesday.

Step 3: Read the health of the stock. The centre's average inventory is worth about 2,000,000 US dollars, and the goods it sells in a year cost about 6,000,000 US dollars. Inventory turnover is therefore 6,000,000 divided by 2,000,000, which is three times a year. Days of supply is average inventory divided by average daily usage: about 2,000,000 divided by roughly 16,400 dollars a day, or about 122 days. Three turns a year prompts a question: how much of the 2,000,000 sits parked in slow-moving C items?

What does this example demonstrate?

Control effort follows value, not item count. The store does not spread its attention evenly across 4,000 items; it concentrates counting, security, and review on the few hundred that carry the money, and deliberately relaxes over the many that do not. Turnover and days of supply are how it checks whether the arrangement is working.

Key lesson: classify by value, protect the few, simplify over the many, and measure the balance.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: FEFO Is Only as Good as the Dates Captured at Receiving
BODY:
A rotation rule is an instruction that acts on data, and a mis-keyed expiry date defeats the whole system. If a batch that truly expires in September is recorded as expiring next year, the system will faithfully and confidently issue it last: every decision FEFO makes about that batch will be precisely wrong. The rule does not fail; the data did, and the rule cannot tell the difference.

This is where the receiving discipline of Lesson 8.2, Receiving, Put-Away, Picking, and Dispatch, matters. The batch number and expiry date are verified against the supplier's documents and the physical labels at the gate, not copied from memory or from the previous delivery. Where scanning is available, the label is scanned rather than keyed, removing the typing error.

Two further habits protect the system. First, the periodic expiry sweep should include a physical check of labels against the record, because the shelf is the truth and the screen is the claim. Second, any date found wrong is corrected immediately and the affected batch re-examined: a batch discovered to be shorter-dated than believed may already need priority issue. FEFO is an information system before it is a picking rule; starve it of honest dates and it issues confidently in the wrong order.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Formulas Are Servants, Not Masters
BODY:
The reorder point and the economic order quantity are computed from inputs: average demand, lead times, ordering costs, holding costs. Every one moves. Demand rises and falls with seasons and events: cement with the building season, food and drink with festivals. Lead times move with suppliers, routes, and border formalities. Costs move with fuel and currency. A reorder point calculated once and never revisited is a stale number, and it fails in exactly the direction its assumptions have drifted.

Three review habits keep the arithmetic honest.

1. Review after every stockout or near miss. A stockout despite a working trigger means the buffer or the lead-time assumption no longer matches reality; ask which input moved.

2. Review when the trade changes. A new supplier with a different lead time, a large new contract, or a seasonal swing all justify recalculating the reorder point and the order quantity.

3. Treat the EOQ as a reference point. Its assumptions, constant demand, one full delivery, no discounts, rarely hold exactly, so the calculated quantity is adjusted for bulk offers and delivery realities rather than obeyed to the bag.

Judgement sets the inputs, reviews the outputs, and owns the decision. The arithmetic serves that judgement; it does not replace it.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: A Stockout Costs More Than Its Figure Shows
BODY:
When a stockout happens, the accounting records show what it cost to recover: the emergency purchase, the extra transport, perhaps a penalty. The largest costs never appear as a line at all. The customer who found the shelf empty did not merely fail to buy; the customer bought from a competitor, found the rival adequate, and may never return. The production line that waited for a spare part billed nobody for its idle hours; the staff who spent the afternoon expediting and apologising did nothing else.

This is why the service level is chosen deliberately rather than left to chance. Safety stock is often criticised as idle capital, but the criticism is incomplete: it is the premium on an insurance policy against costs that are real, recurring, and mostly invisible. A business that holds no buffer has not saved money; it has taken an unpaid position that every hiccup in demand or supply will pay out against, and the payout lands on the outbound delivery relationship with customers described in Lesson 1.4, Types of Logistics: Inbound, Outbound, Reverse, and Third-Party.

The mirror image warns against overcorrecting: stock piled high against every conceivable disruption converts the invisible cost of the stockout into the visible costs of overstocking, capital parked, goods expiring, discrepancies hidden. The balance point is found where this lesson began: honest records, a lead time known from experience, a buffer sized to the service the business intends to give, and a review cycle that keeps all of it true.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A pharmaceutical distributor in Accra holds two batches of the same cough syrup. Batch A, received in November, expires in August of the following year. Batch B, received in February as part of a supplier's short-dated clearance offer, expires in May of the current year. A picker following the first-in, first-out arrival rule is about to issue Batch A.

Which statement best describes the correct issue decision?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Issue Batch A, because the first batch received must always be issued first regardless of any other consideration.
2. Issue Batch B first under the first-expired, first-out rule, because its May expiry is the earliest, even though it arrived later than Batch A.
3. Issue from whichever batch holds the greater quantity, because larger batches should always be cleared before smaller ones.
4. Issue from whichever batch the picker reaches first, because storage position rather than date should determine the issue order.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Cough syrup carries an expiry date, so the first-expired, first-out rule governs: the batch with the earliest expiry date is issued first, whichever delivery it arrived with. Here the arrival order and the expiry order disagree, which is precisely the situation in which the two rules diverge. Following arrival order would hold the May-dated batch behind the August-dated one, and the short-dated stock would drift towards its expiry on the shelf, risking write-off and waste, exactly as the Kumasi pharmacy example showed.

Answer 1 treats arrival order as an absolute, the misconception this lesson dismantles: arrival order governs only when no expiry date does. Answer 3 invents a quantity rule that no rotation discipline is based on; batch size says nothing about which units will spoil first. Answer 4 lets storage position decide, which is how warehouses drift into issuing by convenience while their records claim a rule is running; placement should serve the rule, not replace it.

The takeaway: when goods carry expiry dates, the expiry date is the master, and the earlier arrival must wait its turn.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
A hardware retailer in Takoradi sells about 120 bags of cement in a typical week. Its supplier needs two weeks from order to delivery. The retailer has chosen a safety stock of 100 bags to protect against heavy weeks and late deliveries. All figures are illustrative.

Which option best explains when the retailer should place its next order?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. When the stock reaches zero, because ordering while stock remains would hold unnecessary inventory in the warehouse.
2. When the stock reaches 240 bags, because that covers the demand expected during the two-week lead time.
3. When the stock reaches 340 bags, because that covers the 240 bags of expected lead-time demand plus the 100-bag safety stock.
4. When the stock reaches 440 bags, because the safety stock should be added twice to protect against every possible delay.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The reorder point is the expected demand during the lead time plus the safety stock. Two weeks of demand at 120 bags a week gives 240 bags of lead-time demand, and adding the chosen 100-bag buffer gives a trigger of 340 bags. Ordering at that level means the shelf, buffer included, should just carry the shop through the two weeks even if demand runs warm or the truck runs late.

Answer 1 triggers the order only when the shelf is already empty, guaranteeing that at least the whole lead time passes with nothing to sell. Answer 2 is the attractive half-answer: 240 bags covers only the average lead-time demand, leaving no buffer, so the very first heavy week or delayed delivery becomes a stockout. Answer 4 doubles the buffer with no stated reason, quietly tying up capital and racking space; the safety stock is chosen once, deliberately, to match the intended service level, and then added once.

The takeaway: the order fires when stock falls to lead-time demand plus the chosen buffer, not at zero, not at the average alone, and not at an arbitrary doubling.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
At a distribution centre, a supervisor proposes issuing the newest arrivals first, arguing that the company's accounts value its stock on a last-in, first-out basis, so the warehouse should physically follow the same order.

Which statement best evaluates the proposal?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The proposal is sound, because the accounting records and the physical stock must always follow the same order of movement.
2. The proposal confuses an accounting cost-flow convention with a physical rotation method; warehouses issue the oldest stock first so goods do not age on the shelf, and physically issuing newest first suits almost no goods.
3. The proposal is acceptable provided the warehouse holds no dated goods, because rotation rules matter only for products carrying expiry dates.
4. The proposal would reduce holding costs, because issuing the newest stock first shortens the time every unit spends in storage.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Last in, first out is an accounting cost-flow convention: a bookkeeping assumption about which purchase costs are charged against sales first, associated chiefly with United States accounting practice and not permitted as a cost-flow method under international financial reporting standards. It is not a physical rotation method. Physically issuing the newest arrivals first strands the oldest stock at the back of the warehouse to age, deteriorate, and eventually expire or obsolesce there, which suits almost no goods and inverts everything FIFO and FEFO exist to achieve.

Answer 1 accepts the false premise that accounts and shelves must move in the same order; the accounting question of attaching costs to sales is different from the warehouse question of which physical unit to hand the picker, and a business may answer them differently without inconsistency. Answer 3 wrongly confines rotation to dated goods; even undated stock absorbs moisture, fades, or goes out of fashion, which is why FIFO is the general default everywhere. Answer 4 reverses the arithmetic: issuing newest first lengthens, not shortens, the stay of the oldest units.

The takeaway: the accounts may run last in, first out; the shelves run first in, first out, or first expired, first out.
