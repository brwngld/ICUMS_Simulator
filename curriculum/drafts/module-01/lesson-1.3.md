DRAFT — Lesson 1.3: The Seven Rights of Logistics (for Theory Builder entry; owner to review)

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The Seven Rights as a Checklist for Logistics Quality
BODY:
The two previous lessons defined logistics as the planning, implementation, coordination, and control of the movement and storage of goods from their point of origin to the point of use, and distinguished logistics from the wider activity of supply chain management. This lesson builds on that foundation by answering a practical question: when a delivery has taken place, how do we decide whether the logistics operation behind it actually succeeded?

The most widely used answer in logistics training is a checklist known as the seven rights of logistics. According to this checklist, a delivery is fully successful only when the business supplies:

1. The right product
2. In the right quantity
3. In the right condition
4. To the right place
5. At the right time
6. To the right customer
7. At the right cost

Each right expresses one requirement that the customer genuinely cares about. The customer does not experience the delivery as a list of internal activities such as picking, loading, and transport planning. The customer experiences the outcome: was the product correct, was the amount complete, did it arrive intact, at the intended destination, on the required date, to the intended recipient, and at a cost that makes sense for the service received?

Every logistics activity introduced in the earlier lessons exists to help meet one or more of the seven rights. Order processing protects the right product and the right quantity. Packaging, careful handling, and cold storage protect the right condition. Route planning supports the right place and the right time. Accurate records confirm the right customer. Disciplined cost management supports the right cost.

The seven rights are widely taught in logistics training, but they are a professional convention rather than an official standard. Some books list five rights and some list eight, and some materials speak of the right price instead of the right cost. This course uses the seven-item list above consistently.

In practice, the list works as a checklist that is applied before dispatch, checked again at delivery, and reviewed afterwards. The remainder of this lesson examines each right in turn, shows how failing one creates operational problems, and explains how the rights are balanced against one another.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Right Product: Delivering the Goods That Were Ordered
BODY:
Right product means ensuring that the correct goods are supplied or delivered: the exact item, brand, grade, size, or specification that the customer ordered. The requirement sounds simple, and it is the first question every receiver asks: is this what I ordered?

Product errors arise surprisingly easily. A warehouse picker may select a lookalike carton. Two product codes may differ by a single digit. An order may be transcribed incorrectly at the point of sale. Similar goods in similar packaging, such as two grades of engine oil or two varieties of the same drink, are especially easy to confuse.

Consider a retailer in Kumasi that orders 100 cartons of washing powder. The warehouse accidentally picks 100 cartons of bathing soap supplied in cartons of a similar design. The truck, the driver, and the paperwork may all be in order, yet the delivery fails the very first right: the retailer has received goods it did not order and cannot sell as its planned stock.

The consequences reach beyond the immediate disappointment. The wrong goods must be collected or returned, which costs transport and handling. The correct goods must still be dispatched, which takes additional time. The retailer loses confidence in the supplier's operation, and shelf space may sit empty while the correction is made.

Protecting the right product depends on accuracy at the earliest stages of the process: capturing order details correctly, verifying that the specific item ordered is available, identifying goods clearly in storage, and checking items against the order during picking and before dispatch. A final check at the point of loading, confirming that each carton matches the delivery note, is one of the cheapest safeguards in logistics.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Right Quantity: Delivering the Amount That Was Requested
BODY:
Right quantity means ensuring that the required amount of goods is provided. A customer that orders 300 bags of cement needs 300 bags: a delivery of 270 leaves the customer short, and a delivery of 330 leaves the customer with goods it did not order and may have nowhere to store.

Short deliveries create immediate operational problems. A construction site that planned a concrete pour around 300 bags may have to postpone the work and stand down labour. A shop that expected a full delivery may run out of the product during a busy trading period and lose sales to a competitor across the street.

Over-deliveries are also a failure, although beginners often overlook this. Goods that arrive in excess of the order tie up the customer's money, occupy storage space that was never planned for, and may deteriorate or expire before they can be used. They also create disputes: should the customer pay for goods it never requested?

Quantity accuracy depends on disciplined counting at two points as a minimum: when goods are picked for dispatch and when they are received. The physical count must be compared against the delivery documents, and any difference investigated immediately, because a discrepancy accepted quietly today becomes an unexplained loss tomorrow.

Quantity also connects directly to inventory management. A business can only promise 300 bags if its records show that 300 bags are genuinely available, which is why accurate stock records form part of meeting the seven rights rather than a separate clerical matter.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Right Condition: Delivering Goods in a Usable State
BODY:
Right condition means ensuring that goods arrive in an acceptable and usable state. Goods that reach the correct place on time can still be worthless if they arrive broken, contaminated, spoiled, or otherwise unfit for use.

Condition is protected by packaging, careful handling, suitable storage, and, for certain goods, temperature control. Failures take many forms: tiles cracked by rough handling, cartons stained by water that leaked onto a truck bed, cans dented beyond what a retailer will accept, bags of cement that have hardened after exposure to moisture.

Some goods require an unbroken chain of temperature-controlled transport and storage, known as the cold chain. Frozen fish that thaws in transit, or medicines that are held outside their required temperature range, may become unusable even though nothing about the failure is visible from outside the packaging. A medicine delivery that travels without the required cooling is a wrong-condition delivery, even if it is complete and punctual. A worked example later in this lesson examines such a failure in detail.

For goods with a limited shelf life, condition also includes the usable life remaining: dated goods delivered with very little of their shelf life left may be physically intact yet commercially unacceptable to the buyer.

The right condition is planned, not hoped for. It requires identifying how the goods are vulnerable, choosing packaging and handling methods that suit them, verifying that storage and transport equipment can maintain what the goods require, and inspecting goods at receipt so that damage is detected and documented rather than discovered later.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Right Place: Delivering to the Intended Destination
BODY:
Right place means delivering goods to the intended destination: the exact address, site, gate, or branch for which they were ordered. A delivery made to the wrong location fails this right even when the goods themselves are perfect.

Wrong-place deliveries usually trace back to information problems: an address recorded incorrectly at the order stage, an old address left on the delivery note after a customer has moved, unclear directions, or two locations with similar names.

Consider a consignment of 300 bags of cement ordered for a construction site outside Wa. The delivery note carries an old address, and the driver offloads the cement at a farm gate in a nearby village. The cement may be undamaged and the journey otherwise well executed, but the delivery has failed: the bags are kilometres from where the work is happening. The error now costs twice. The cement must be reloaded, transported again, and rechecked, while workers and equipment at the site wait idle.

The right place can be a demanding requirement in practice. It may mean not just the correct town but the correct premises, the correct loading dock, or the correct gate on a large site. Delivery instructions should therefore state the destination precisely, and drivers should confirm the location against the delivery note before offloading. A few minutes of verification at the destination prevents the double cost of a misplaced delivery: the wasted journey and the delayed customer.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Right Time: Delivering When the Goods Are Required
BODY:
Right time means delivering goods when they are required, which is not the same thing as delivering them as quickly as possible. The distinction matters. Speed is one option among several; the actual requirement is that the delivery aligns with the customer's activities.

Late deliveries disrupt whatever the goods were meant to support. Roofing sheets that arrive after the carpenters have left the site delay the whole building programme. Stock that misses the start of a promotion loses the sales the promotion was designed to capture. Raw materials delivered late can hold up production and trigger extra costs throughout a customer's operation.

Earliness can also be a failure, which surprises many beginners. Fresh fish delivered a day before the customer's cold room is available begins to deteriorate. Goods that arrive before a site can receive them must be stored, guarded, or handled twice. Delivering early is only acceptable when the customer can receive and protect the goods.

The right time is therefore defined by the customer's need: the date and delivery window that the customer's production schedule, sales plan, or project programme requires. Meeting it depends on realistic scheduling, reliable transport, monitoring of the journey, and early communication when a delay becomes likely, so that the customer can adjust its plans instead of simply being failed at the gate.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Right Customer: Delivering to the Intended Recipient
BODY:
Right customer means ensuring that goods reach the intended recipient: the person or organisation that ordered them or is named to receive them. It is closely related to the right place but is not the same requirement. The place is the destination; the customer is who should take receipt there.

Confusion between recipients is a real operational risk, particularly where names or premises are similar. On a busy market street, a delivery intended for Adjoa's Provisions can easily be offloaded at the neighbouring boutique with a similar name. In a company with several branches, goods ordered by one branch are sometimes delivered to another branch that never ordered them, leaving one branch overstocked and the other short.

The consequences of a wrong-recipient delivery are serious. The goods are in the hands of an organisation that has no order for them and may use them, resell them, or simply be unable to account for them. Recovering the goods costs transport and goodwill, and the intended customer is left unserved.

The right customer is protected by verifying three things at delivery: that the consignee name on the delivery note matches the recipient, that the order reference matches, and that the person signing is authorised to receive on behalf of the customer. Drivers should not offload on the strength of a familiar-looking signboard alone. A signature from the wrong person is not proof of delivery; it is the beginning of a dispute.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Right Cost: Delivering at a Sustainable Cost
BODY:
Right cost means achieving the delivery at an appropriate and economically sustainable cost: a cost the business can afford to repeat on every order and that matches the level of service the customer has agreed to receive. Some training materials call this the right price; this course uses the term right cost.

A logistics operation consumes money at every stage: transport and fuel, warehousing, handling labour, packaging, insurance, and administration. If deliveries persistently cost more than the business planned, the operation is failing economically even when every other right is met, because the business cannot sustain the service it is providing.

The right cost does not mean the cheapest possible delivery. Cutting cost carelessly usually breaks other rights. An unreliable, cut-price carrier delivers late. A shared vehicle without proper protection delivers damaged goods. Skipping packaging to save weight ends in damaged cargo. The cost of correcting those failures, through replacements, returns, refunds, and lost customers, routinely exceeds the saving.

Equally, paying for service the customer does not need wastes money. Routing a non-urgent consignment by the fastest and most expensive option serves no one; the extra cost is absorbed either in the price the customer must be charged or in the supplier's margin.

The right cost is therefore judged together with the other six rights. The objective is to meet the agreed service level at a cost the operation can sustain, order after order. A worked example later in this lesson shows this balancing in a transport choice between speed and economy.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: How the Seven Rights Create Customer Satisfaction
BODY:
Customer satisfaction in logistics is not an abstract feeling. It is the customer's judgement that each delivery matched what was expected. The seven rights describe exactly what customers expect, so the checklist is also a direct model of satisfaction.

Meeting all seven rights consistently produces effects that compound over time. The customer learns that orders can be trusted: production can be scheduled, shelves can be stocked, and promises to end customers can be kept. Trust turns into repeat orders, and repeat orders into a stable business relationship. Reliable suppliers also gain reputation, because satisfied customers describe their experience to others.

Failing the rights produces the mirror image. A customer that receives the wrong product must return goods and reorder, losing selling time. A customer that is repeatedly let down on time stops planning around the supplier and starts looking for alternatives. Every failure carries a hidden cost beyond the immediate incident: the customer's confidence, once reduced, must be rebuilt through consistent performance.

Consider a bakery that depends on daily flour deliveries. When the flour arrives complete, fresh, and on schedule, the bakery bakes to plan and serves its own customers without interruption. When deliveries are repeatedly short or late, the bakery must buy emergency stock at higher cost, disappoint customers, and eventually question whether it has the right supplier.

Notice also that customers judge deliveries as a whole. A shipment that is perfect in every respect except arriving three days late is, in the customer's experience, a late delivery, not a mostly successful one. Customer satisfaction is therefore manufactured inside operations, one verified delivery at a time.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Balancing the Seven Rights: The Trade-Off Principle
BODY:
Achieving all seven rights simultaneously costs money, and achieving any of them at a higher level usually costs more. Faster transport costs more than slower transport. A dedicated vehicle protects condition and timing better than a shared one, but costs more. Holding extra stock makes goods available at any time, but raises storage costs and ties up capital. These tensions are called trade-offs: improvements on one right tend to raise the cost of achieving another.

This is why logistics management exists as a discipline. Its work is not to maximise every right regardless of expense, but to decide the level of each right the customer actually requires, and then to deliver that level at the most sustainable cost. The fastest option is not the cheapest, and the cheapest option is not automatically the best; the correct choice depends on the situation.

Two brief illustrations show the logic. A pharmaceutical business shipping temperature-sensitive medicines will pay for fast, carefully controlled transport, because a failure of condition would destroy the value of the entire consignment. A furniture importer replenishing a showroom may accept several weeks of sea freight, because the need is a full showroom next quarter, not next week. Neither manager is wrong; each balanced the rights against a different requirement.

Beginners often ask which of the seven rights is the most important. The professional answer is that they are jointly important: the required level of each right is set by the customer's need, and the operation's task is to meet all seven at a cost that can be sustained order after order. A worked example later in this lesson walks through one such balancing decision in detail.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Using the Seven Rights as a Daily Checklist
BODY:
The seven rights earn their place in logistics practice because they convert a vague ambition, good service, into specific, checkable questions. A logistics officer can apply them to any consignment at three stages.

1. Before dispatch
Confirm that the goods are the exact product ordered; count them against the order; check that packaging and storage requirements are understood and that the goods leave in the required condition; verify the delivery address and the consignee name; confirm the required delivery date; and confirm the transport arrangement and the agreed cost. A pre-dispatch check that works through these seven points removes most causes of delivery failure before the truck moves.

2. During delivery
The driver follows the agreed instructions: the correct destination, the correct recipient, the required handling. Any deviation, such as a closed gate, a damaged carton noticed in transit, or a delay on the road, is reported promptly so that the customer can be informed early rather than surprised at arrival.

3. On receipt and afterwards
The receiver counts the goods, inspects their condition, and checks the delivery note against the order before signing. Both parties then update their records. Any missed right, whether a shortage, a damaged carton, or a late arrival, is recorded and investigated so that the same failure is less likely to recur.

Applied this way, the checklist also becomes a shared language between trading partners. When a delivery fails, the two businesses can identify precisely which right failed and why, instead of arguing in general terms about poor service.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Walking a Rice Delivery Through the Seven Rights
BODY:
Consider a food distributor with a warehouse in Tema and a retail customer, Adampa Provisions Ltd, operating a store in Tamale. The retailer orders 400 bags of rice ahead of a festive period of high demand. The delivery can be checked against every one of the seven rights. All figures in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: Order confirmation. The order states the specific grade and brand of rice the retailer sells, the quantity of 400 bags, the store's delivery address in Tamale, the intended recipient, the required delivery date, and the agreed transport arrangement and cost.

Step 2: Inventory check and picking. Warehouse staff confirm that 400 bags of the ordered grade are available and count them as they are picked for dispatch. This protects the right product and the right quantity.

Step 3: Protection of condition. The bags are loaded onto a dry, covered truck, stacked carefully so the sacks are not torn or crushed, and sheeted against rain. This protects the right condition.

Step 4: Transportation. The truck makes the road journey from Tema to Tamale, several hundred kilometres, following the schedule agreed for the required delivery date.

Step 5: Delivery to the right place and the right customer. The driver delivers to the store's address in Tamale, checks the consignee name before offloading, and hands the goods to the store manager, who is authorised to receive them.

Step 6: Verification on receipt. The store manager counts the bags, confirms the total of 400, inspects them for tearing, dampness, or contamination, and checks the grade against the order before signing.

Step 7: Records and cost. Both businesses update their records, and the retailer is charged according to the agreed delivery arrangement.

Checking the delivery against each right shows why it succeeded. The right product was met: the bags matched the ordered grade and brand. The right quantity: 400 bags counted at dispatch and at receipt. The right condition: the bags arrived dry, sealed, and undamaged. The right place: the store's address in Tamale. The right time: the agreed date, before the period of high demand. The right customer: the store manager received and signed for the goods. The right cost: the agreed, planned arrangement was used.

What does this example demonstrate? A successful delivery is not the result of good fortune. Each of the seven rights was met because a specific, deliberate activity protected it.

Key lesson: treat the seven rights as a checklist on every consignment. Checking each right separately makes failures visible early, while they can still be corrected.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: A Cold-Chain Failure on an International Medicine Shipment
BODY:
A pharmaceutical distributor in Accra orders temperature-sensitive medicines from a manufacturer based in Mumbai, India, for onward supply to hospital pharmacies. The arrangements described are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: The order and its condition requirement. The order confirms the products, quantities, and destinations, and states a strict requirement: the medicines must remain within a specified temperature range from the moment they leave the manufacturer until they are delivered. Temperature-controlled transport and storage of this kind is known as the cold chain.

Step 2: Export preparation. The manufacturer packs the medicines in insulated packaging designed to hold the required temperature range throughout the journey.

Step 3: International transportation. The shipment travels by air from Mumbai to Accra under temperature-controlled handling, arriving within the planned schedule.

Step 4: The failure at the destination. The distributor has no functioning cold storage available when the shipment arrives, because its cold room is out of service. The medicines are therefore held for two days in an ordinary storeroom at ambient temperature before being sent on to the hospital pharmacy.

Step 5: Detection at receipt. The hospital pharmacist checks the temperature records that accompany the shipment and finds that the medicines have been held outside the required range. The medicines can no longer be confirmed as usable and cannot be supplied to patients.

Step 6: The consequences. The distributor must arrange replacement stock urgently at additional cost. It has paid for international transport that delivered goods which cannot be used, the hospital's confidence is damaged, and patients may experience delays in receiving treatment.

Checking the delivery against the seven rights reveals a decisive pattern. The right product was met, because the correct medicines were supplied. The right quantity was met, because the count was correct. The right place was met, because the medicines reached the hospital pharmacy. The right customer was met, because the pharmacist received them. The right time was met in the narrow sense that the delivery dates were as planned. But the right condition failed completely: medicines that had to be kept cold were stored warm, and the goods became unusable. The right cost also failed, because money was spent on a delivery that produced no usable goods.

What does this example demonstrate? A delivery can appear complete on paper, with correct items, correct count, correct address, and correct dates, and still be a service failure. Condition requirements must be planned across the entire journey, not just the main transport leg.

Key lesson: where goods have special handling requirements, every storage point, transit stop, and handover must be checked against those requirements. One unprotected stage is enough to destroy the value of the whole consignment.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Balancing Speed and Cost for an Imported Spare Part
BODY:
A water-processing company in Takoradi depends on an imported filling machine. One morning a key seal on the machine fails and production stops. The company's supplier in Dubai can provide the replacement seal in two ways. Air freight delivers within a few days at a comparatively high transport cost. Sea freight costs far less but takes several weeks, including port handling at both ends. The journey times in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: Establish the right time. The machine is stopped, and the company is losing output it has already promised to customers. The seal is therefore needed as quickly as it can possibly be obtained. In this scenario, the right time means immediately.

Step 2: Establish the true cost of each option. Air freight is expensive, but every additional week without the machine brings lost production and disappointed customers. Sea freight is cheap to pay for but expensive to wait for. The relevant comparison is not the freight invoice alone; it is the freight cost plus the cost of waiting.

Step 3: The decision. Because the cost of waiting is high, the manager chooses air freight. Paying more for transport is the economically sustainable choice here, because the faster delivery protects the business's other commitments. The right time and the right cost have been balanced, and in this situation the balance favours speed.

Step 4: The contrasting case. Three months later, the same company orders routine spare parts for maintenance planned well in advance. Here the right time is modest: the parts are needed before the planned maintenance date, not immediately. The manager chooses sea freight and a consolidated shipment, saving transport cost without risking the schedule. This time the balance favours economy.

What does this example demonstrate? The right time does not mean always fastest, and the right cost does not mean always cheapest. Both rights are defined by the customer's actual requirement. An urgent breakdown justifies paying for speed; routine replenishment justifies waiting in order to save cost.

Key lesson: logistics management is the balancing of the seven rights against one another. The professional question is not how fast or how cheap a delivery can be, but what level of service the situation genuinely requires and what it is worth paying for.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: The Seven Rights Are a Convention, Not an Official Standard
BODY:
Treat the seven rights as a professional checklist, not as a law or an international standard issued by an authority. No single organisation owns the list. It is a convention that has developed across logistics textbooks and training programmes, and versions of it differ.

You may meet the five rights of purchasing, which list quality, quantity, time, place, and price. You may meet lists that speak of the right price rather than the right cost, or lists that add further items such as right documentation or right way, meaning the right transport mode. Longer versions with eight or more rights also circulate.

The differences are smaller than they first appear. Every version expresses the same idea: a delivery must match what the customer ordered, in the amount and state required, at the destination and time required, for a recipient entitled to use it, at an economic cost. When you meet another list, identify which version is being used and adapt your checklist to it.

Within this course, one list is used consistently: product, quantity, condition, place, time, customer, and cost. Lesson questions and assessments use this version, so learn it in this form first and explore the variants afterwards.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: A Failure of Any One Right Is a Service Failure
BODY:
Because customers experience a delivery as a whole, a failure of any single right makes the whole delivery a failure in the customer's eyes, however well the other six went.

Consider a supplier that delivers the exact product ordered, in the full quantity, undamaged, to the right address, signed for by the right customer, at the planned cost, but three days after the date the promotion it was meant to stock was due to begin. Nothing about that delivery is a success for the customer. The goods arrived too late to do the job they were ordered for.

Beginners sometimes reason that a delivery meeting six of the seven rights is more than 80 per cent successful. Customers do not score this way. Strength in six rights does not compensate for weakness in the seventh, because each right protects a requirement the customer cannot simply do without.

This is also the practical reason the seven rights are checked one by one. A general impression that a delivery went well can hide a single missed requirement; a structured check against each right does not. Logistics teams therefore verify product, quantity, condition, place, time, customer, and cost separately on every consignment, and treat any single miss as a service failure to be investigated rather than a near miss to be forgotten.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Right Place and Right Customer Are Different Checks
BODY:
Newcomers to logistics often merge the right place and the right customer into a single idea of delivering to the right address. They are separate requirements, and each can fail while the other is satisfied.

Right place concerns the destination: the premises, site, gate, dock, or branch where the goods belong. Right customer concerns the recipient: the person or organisation entitled to take receipt. Two short illustrations show the difference.

Goods for a small provisions shop are offloaded at the neighbouring shop by mistake. The street location is essentially correct, but the recipient is wrong: the goods are now in the hands of a business that never ordered them. Conversely, goods ordered by a customer are delivered to the customer's old address after the business has relocated. The intended recipient is beyond doubt, but the place is wrong, and the goods sit where the customer can no longer receive them.

The protection is procedural. Delivery notes should state both the destination and the consignee name clearly. Drivers confirm both before offloading. The person signing must be authorised to receive for the named customer. When a dispute arises, records showing that both checks were made protect the logistics operation as well as the customer.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A pharmacy in Kumasi orders 20 cartons of temperature-sensitive medicines from a distributor in Accra. The delivery reaches the pharmacy on the agreed date, contains exactly the medicines ordered, and the count is correct. However, the medicines travelled in an ordinary vehicle without the required cooling and spent a night in a hot storeroom.

Which option best describes what went wrong?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The delivery failed the right condition, because the goods were not kept in the state required for them to remain usable.
2. The delivery failed the right quantity, because the medicines could no longer be confirmed as usable on arrival.
3. The delivery failed the right time, because medicines should always be delivered overnight.
4. The delivery failed the right customer, because the driver was not authorised to handle medicines.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

The right condition requires goods to arrive in an acceptable and usable state, and for temperature-sensitive medicines this includes remaining within the required temperature range throughout transport and storage. A delivery that travels without the required cooling has failed the right condition even when the product, quantity, destination, recipient, and delivery date are all correct.

Answer 2 misidentifies the failure. The count was correct, so the right quantity was met; the problem is the state of the goods, not the number delivered. Answer 3 is wrong because the delivery arrived on the agreed date, so the right time was met, and nothing in this lesson suggests that medicines must always move overnight. Answer 4 is wrong because the right customer concerns whether the goods reach the intended recipient, which they did; the driver's handling of the goods relates to condition, not to the recipient.

The takeaway: condition requirements are not only about visible damage. For goods that depend on temperature control, an invisible failure can make a complete, punctual delivery worthless.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
A company in Takoradi must import non-urgent spare parts needed for planned maintenance in two months. The manager is comparing air freight, which is fast but expensive, with sea freight, which is cheaper but slower.

Which statement best describes a sound application of the seven rights in this decision?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Air freight should always be chosen, because the right time means delivering goods as fast as possible in every situation.
2. Sea freight should always be chosen, because the right cost means selecting the cheapest option in every situation.
3. The manager should choose the option that meets the required maintenance date at the most sustainable cost, balancing the right time against the right cost.
4. Neither time nor cost matters here, because the seven rights apply only to urgent shipments.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The seven rights are balanced against one another, and both the right time and the right cost are defined by the customer's actual requirement. Here the parts are needed before a maintenance date two months away, so a slower and cheaper mode that still meets that date satisfies the right time while protecting the right cost. If the parts were instead needed for a breakdown that had stopped production, the same balance could justify paying for speed.

Answer 1 fails because the right time does not mean maximum speed regardless of need; it means delivery when the goods are required. Answer 2 fails because the right cost does not mean the cheapest option regardless of service; cutting cost carelessly can break other rights and cost more in corrections. Answer 4 fails because the seven rights apply to every delivery, urgent or routine; only the required balance differs.

The takeaway: the professional question is what level of service the situation genuinely requires, and what it is worth paying for.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
A dispatch officer in Tema is preparing a consignment of 150 cartons of cooking oil for delivery to a named customer's shop in Wa.

According to the seven rights of logistics, which option best describes what the officer should confirm before the truck departs?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. That the truck is available and fuelled, since transport arrangements are the only matter a dispatch officer controls.
2. That the goods are the cheapest brand in the warehouse, even if the customer ordered a different brand.
3. That the delivery documents are complete, since product, quantity, and condition can all be checked after the goods arrive.
4. That the goods are the exact products ordered, counted against the order and properly packed, and that the delivery note shows the correct destination, consignee, required date, and agreed cost arrangement.
CORRECT: 4
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 4.

Before dispatch, the seven rights work as a pre-departure checklist. The right product confirms the exact ordered goods. The right quantity requires counting against the order. The right condition requires suitable packing and an understanding of any handling requirements. The right place and the right customer require a clear destination and consignee on the delivery note. The right time requires the agreed delivery date. The right cost requires the planned transport arrangement. Answer 4 covers each of these checks.

Answer 1 fails because transport is only one part of the dispatch officer's responsibility; the goods, the delivery information, and the cost arrangement must also be confirmed. Answer 2 fails because substituting a cheaper brand breaks the right product, since the customer must receive what was ordered. Answer 3 fails because checking product, quantity, and condition only after arrival allows any failure to travel hundreds of kilometres before it is detected.

The takeaway: most delivery failures can be prevented before the truck moves, by checking each of the seven rights in turn at dispatch.
