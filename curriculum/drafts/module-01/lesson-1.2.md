=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: From a single business to a chain of businesses
BODY:
The previous lesson examined logistics within a single business: how one organisation plans, implements, and controls the movement and storage of goods so that products reach customers in the required quantity and condition. This lesson widens the view, because most products do not travel from their raw materials to the final customer inside one company.

Consider a bottle of cooking oil sold in a shop in Accra. The bottle begins on farms where oil palm is grown. A mill buys the fruit and crushes it into crude oil. A refinery purifies the oil, bottles it, and packs it into cartons. A distributor transports the cartons to a regional warehouse, from which shops receive their orders. Finally, a household buys one bottle and takes it home. Five or more different businesses took part, and each one depended on the others.

This connected sequence of businesses, activities, information, and resources is called a supply chain. A supply chain is the network of organisations, people, activities, information, and resources involved in producing and delivering a product, from the earliest suppliers of materials to the final customer.

The word chain is convenient but slightly misleading. Each stage is usually connected to many suppliers and many customers at the same time, so a supply chain in practice looks more like a web or network. One shop, for example, sells hundreds of different products, and each product arrives through its own supply chain.

The purpose of studying the supply chain is simple: the shop in the example cannot satisfy its customers on its own. Its ability to sell cooking oil depends on decisions and activities performed far beyond its own premises, by people it may never meet. Supply chain management is the discipline that studies and manages this wider network, and the remainder of this lesson explains what it involves and how it relates to logistics.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The definition and purpose of supply chain management
BODY:
Supply chain management is the planning and management of all the activities involved in sourcing and procurement, in production, and in logistics, together with the coordination and collaboration with the partners who make up the chain: suppliers, intermediaries, service providers, and customers. Its aim is to integrate supply and demand management within and across companies, an idea often called integration, so that all participants work toward one coherent result instead of a collection of separate efforts.

Note the breadth of that definition. Supply chain management is not only about moving goods. It reaches backward into what the business buys and forward into how the business serves its customers. Its major purposes include the following.

1. Securing the right inputs. Through sourcing and procurement, the chain obtains the materials, components, and services that production requires. Choosing suitable suppliers and buying from them on fair, reliable terms determines everything that happens afterwards.

2. Coordinating production. Conversion, meaning the processing or manufacturing that turns raw materials into finished products, must be planned so that quantities match what the market will actually need.

3. Directing logistics. Supply chain management relies on logistics management to move and store goods efficiently between the stages of the chain, from suppliers through production to customers.

4. Coordinating the participants. The businesses in a chain are independent organisations. Supply chain management builds the communication, agreements, and shared plans that allow them to act together.

5. Balancing supply with demand. The chain should produce and deliver what customers need, when they need it, without piling up unnecessary stock or starving the market.

The overall purpose is to make the required products available at the required time and place, at an acceptable total cost to the whole chain. Meeting that purpose requires every participant to contribute, which is why coordination sits at the centre of supply chain management.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The participants and tiers of a supply chain
BODY:
A supply chain contains several kinds of participants, each performing a distinct role.

1. Suppliers provide the raw materials, components, packaging, and services that production requires. A refinery depends on suppliers of crude oil, bottles, labels, and cartons.

2. Producers, such as mills, factories, and processing plants, convert inputs into finished or part-finished goods.

3. Distributors and wholesalers buy in bulk, store products, and supply retailers over a wide area.

4. Retailers sell to the final customer in shops, markets, or online.

5. The final customer, whether a household or another business, is the point for which the entire chain exists.

Alongside these, service providers support the chain without owning the goods: transport companies move them, warehouse operators store them and, in international trade, freight forwarders arrange shipments and prepare the necessary documentation.

Two directional terms describe positions in the chain. Upstream means toward the original sources of materials; for a retailer, the distributor, the producer, and the farms are all upstream. Downstream means toward the final customer; for a producer, the distributor and the shops are downstream.

Chains also have depth. A bottling plant buys cartons from a packaging manufacturer; that manufacturer in turn buys paper from a paper mill, which buys from pulp suppliers. From the bottling plant's point of view, the packaging manufacturer is a first-tier supplier and the paper mill is a second-tier supplier. In the downstream direction, a producer may sell to a distributor, who sells to shops, who sell to households: the customers of its customers. When managers speak of the whole supply chain, they mean this full stretch, from the suppliers of their suppliers to the customers of their customers.

Every participant in the chain is therefore both a customer and a supplier: it buys from the stage above it and supplies the stage below it.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The relationship between logistics and supply chain management
BODY:
The previous lesson defined logistics as the planning, implementation, coordination, and control of the movement and storage of goods, together with the related information and resources, from a point of origin to a point of consumption or use. Supply chain management, introduced in this lesson, covers that and more. The clearest way to state the relationship is this: logistics management is that part of supply chain management which plans, implements, and controls the efficient flow and storage of goods and related information between the point of origin and the point of consumption. Logistics sits inside supply chain management as one of its principal components.

The scope of each differs in three ways.

1. Range of activities. Logistics concentrates on the movement and storage of goods and the information needed to manage them. Supply chain management adds sourcing, procurement, production planning, and the coordination of participants, and then directs logistics within that wider plan.

2. Range of organisations. A company's logistics management chiefly organises its own flows: inbound from its suppliers and outbound to its customers. Supply chain management reaches further, linking several independent companies into one coordinated system.

3. Question asked. Logistics asks how to move and store particular goods efficiently: which method, which route, which warehouse, which schedule. Supply chain management asks what should be bought, from whom, what should be produced and in what quantity, and how all the participants should work together so that the whole chain performs.

The two are not rivals and not alternatives. A company does not choose between logistics and supply chain management; it manages logistics as part of its supply chain management. Both share the same underlying purpose, meeting customers' requirements efficiently, and the worked examples and important notes below develop the comparison further.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Activities associated with logistics
BODY:
The previous lesson introduced the major logistics activities: transportation, warehousing, inventory management, order processing, packaging and cargo handling, distribution, information management, and coordination. This lesson groups the same activities by direction of flow, because supply chains move goods in named directions.

1. Inbound logistics covers the flows of materials and goods coming into a business from its suppliers: crude oil arriving at a refinery, cartons arriving at a bottling plant, fresh produce arriving at a processing factory.

2. Outbound logistics covers the flows of finished goods leaving the business toward its customers: bottled oil leaving the refinery for the distributor's warehouse, cartons leaving the warehouse for shops.

3. Internal logistics covers movement and storage inside the organisation's own premises: transferring goods between receiving areas, storage, production, and dispatch.

4. Reverse logistics covers goods moving back up the chain: returns, repairs, recycling, and disposal. An important note near the end of this lesson returns to this flow.

In practice, companies draw the boundary of logistics differently. Around the core activities of transportation, warehousing, order fulfilment, inventory management, and network design, many organisations add related duties to a greater or lesser degree: sourcing decisions, procurement, production planning, packaging and assembly, and customer service may all sit wholly or partly within the logistics function. In other companies these duties belong to separate purchasing or production departments and are coordinated at supply chain level.

For a beginner, the practical point is this: some activities, such as transportation and warehousing, are always logistics; others, such as procurement and production planning, sit between logistics and the wider supply chain, and different organisations assign them differently. What never changes is that all of these activities must be managed somewhere, and their results must fit together.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The broader activities of supply chain management
BODY:
Where logistics manages flows of goods, supply chain management manages the whole set of activities that make those flows possible and worthwhile. The broader activities include the following.

1. Sourcing. Deciding where and from whom to obtain materials and services. This includes identifying potential suppliers, comparing their reliability, prices, and quality, and selecting those on whom the chain will depend.

2. Procurement. Carrying out the purchasing itself: preparing requisitions and purchase orders, agreeing terms, receiving the goods, checking them against the order, and processing the supplier's invoice for payment. Procurement turns sourcing decisions into actual supplies arriving at the factory gate.

3. Production planning. Deciding what to convert, when, and in what quantity, so that output matches expected demand rather than guesswork. A refinery plans how many thousands of litres to refine and bottle each week before the logistics team plans how to move them.

4. Demand and supply planning. Balancing forecasts of what customers will buy against what the chain can produce and move, so that the two sides of the chain pull in the same direction.

5. Coordination and collaboration with channel partners. Sharing plans, forecasts, stock positions, and schedules with suppliers, distributors, and customers, so that each stage prepares for what the next stage actually needs.

6. Customer service. Keeping commitments to customers and managing the information that tells them what to expect.

The contrast with the logistics activities can be stated in one sentence. Logistics asks how to move and store goods efficiently; supply chain management asks what to buy, from whom, what to make, how much, and how all participants should act together. Both sets of activities are essential, and each supports the other: poor sourcing decisions cannot be repaired by good trucking, and excellent sourcing is wasted if the goods never reach customers.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: The three flows of a supply chain: products, information, and finance
BODY:
Whatever the product, three flows move through every supply chain. Understanding them is essential, because logistics concentrates on the first flow while supply chain management coordinates all three. Beneath all three flows lie the resources introduced in the previous lesson: vehicles, warehouses, people, equipment, and systems.

1. Product flow. The physical movement of goods downstream, from suppliers through producers, distributors, and retailers to the final customer. This is the flow logistics handles daily: trucks, warehouses, loading, and delivery. Some goods also flow upstream as returns, refills, or recycling.

2. Information flow. The movement of data in both directions. Purchase orders and delivery instructions travel upstream from buyers to sellers; forecasts, stock levels, and sales results also travel upstream to inform production; shipment updates, delivery confirmations, and invoices travel downstream with the goods. Information is the chain's nervous system: when it is late or wrong, each participant plans on guesses.

3. Finance flow. Payments travel upstream. The household pays the shop; the shop pays the distributor; the distributor pays the refinery; the refinery pays the mill; the mill pays the farms. At each stage, payment terms decide when money actually moves, and stock held in warehouses ties up working capital until the goods are finally sold.

The three flows are inseparable in practice. Consider the bottle of cooking oil from earlier. The shop reorders cartons from the distributor (information), the distributor's truck delivers them (product), and the shop's payment follows (finance). If any one of the three fails, the other two eventually fail with it: goods delivered without accurate records cannot be paid for promptly, and payment claimed for goods never received leads to disputes rather than a working chain.

Supply chain management exists to keep all three flows moving smoothly among independent organisations, and logistics provides the physical execution that the other two flows describe and settle.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: How logistics supports supply chain performance
BODY:
A supply chain is judged by its results: products available when customers want them, deliveries that arrive reliably and in acceptable condition, and a total cost the market can bear. These results are produced, physically, by logistics activities performed at every link of the chain.

Transportation connects the stages: farms to mills, mills to refineries, refineries to warehouses, warehouses to shops. Warehousing and storage hold goods between stages so that production rhythms and shopping rhythms do not have to match moment by moment. Inventory management keeps availability without excessive stock. Order processing and accurate records ensure that what is promised is what is delivered. Each of these activities was introduced in the previous lesson; in the supply chain view their significance multiplies, because each participant's logistics becomes the next participant's supply.

Consider what that means. The refinery's outbound logistics determine the distributor's inbound supply. The distributor's warehouse accuracy determines whether the shop's order can be filled. The shop's shelf availability, the customer's actual experience, rests on logistics work performed by four or five different businesses upstream. A weak link does not stay contained: when the distributor's records are wrong, the refinery feels it as sudden extra orders, the shops experience it as empty shelves, and customers experience it as a reason to buy elsewhere.

This is why supply chain performance depends on logistics even though supply chain management is broader. Plans, supplier choices, and production schedules are only intentions until logistics turns them into movement and storage. A chain with excellent coordination but careless execution still fails its customers, and a chain with excellent execution but no coordination executes the wrong plan efficiently. The complementary pairing of the two is the subject that runs through the rest of this lesson.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: Coordination among supply chain participants
BODY:
Coordination is the deliberate alignment of plans and actions among the independent businesses that make up a chain. It matters because each participant sees only part of the picture: a shop knows its own sales, the distributor knows its own orders, the refinery knows its own production. When participants do not share what they know, each one plans on guesses, and guesses are usually late and exaggerated.

Without coordination, a familiar pattern appears. When shops fear a shortage, they place larger orders than their real sales require. The distributor sees the larger orders and increases its own order to the refinery. The refinery raises production. Each stage, acting sensibly on the information available to it, amplifies the original signal. When the fear proves groundless, orders collapse, stocks pile up, goods age or perish, and the chain swings from too much to too little. This amplification of orders up the chain is one of the best-documented problems in supply chain management, and it is caused not by bad luck but by participants deciding in isolation.

With coordination, the pattern changes. Participants share sales information and forecasts, so orders track real demand rather than fear. Replenishment happens on agreed schedules in smaller, more frequent quantities. A distributor that knows a shop's weekly sales pattern can deliver a steady quantity instead of reacting to sudden spikes. The benefits reach every participant: lower total inventory across the chain, fewer urgent shipments and stockouts, steadier production plans, less waste, and delivery promises that can be kept.

Coordination is achieved through practical means: regular communication, shared forecasts and stock data, agreed procedures, service agreements, and the trust that builds when participants keep their commitments. An important note later in this lesson explains why coordination, not command, is the only workable model between independent companies.

=== KEY CONCEPT ===
HEADING: How logistics and supply chain management complement each other
BODY:
Logistics and supply chain management are complementary: each supplies what the other lacks. Supply chain management decides and coordinates; logistics executes. A supply chain needs both, in the same way that a journey needs both a route plan and a vehicle.

Supply chain management without logistics is paper. Decisions about which farms supply a mill, how much oil a refinery should produce, and which regions a distributor should serve produce no value until goods physically move and are physically stored. The plan depends on trucks, warehouses, accurate records, and delivery schedules, all of which are logistics.

Logistics without supply chain management is wasted effort in the wrong direction. A distribution team can run its warehouse flawlessly and fill its trucks efficiently, yet still deliver products nobody needed, sourced from a supplier who should not have been chosen, at a time that misses the market. Movement and storage optimised in isolation can even work against the chain: a cheap but slow transport choice may suit the transport budget while damaging a production line that needed the material urgently.

Working together, the two produce results neither achieves alone. Consider the cooking-oil chain once more. Supply chain management decides to serve northern markets from a regional warehouse rather than shipping each shop's order from the coast. Logistics turns that decision into reality: vehicles, routes, storage capacity, stock records, and delivery rounds. When demand shifts, information shared upward allows the plan to be adjusted, and logistics implements the adjustment.

A simple summary completes the lesson. Logistics makes the chain run; supply chain management makes the parts of the chain run together. The next lesson turns to the criteria by which logistics work is judged: the seven rights of logistics.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: Mapping the supply chain of a bottle of cooking oil
BODY:
Follow one bottle of cooking oil from its raw material to a shelf in Accra, and identify the participants, their tiers, and the flows between them. All names, places, and quantities in this example are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Step 1: The farms. Smallholder farms in southern Ghana grow and harvest oil palm fruit. From the refinery's point of view the farms are second-tier suppliers, because they supply the mill, which is the refinery's first-tier supplier. Their logistics include collecting fruit from the groves and delivering it to the mill quickly, because harvested fruit deteriorates if it is left standing.

Step 2: The oil mill. The mill buys fruit from many farms, crushes it, and produces crude oil in bulk tanks. Its logistics include receiving fruit, storing crude oil, and dispatching tanker loads to the refinery.

Step 3: The refinery and bottling plant, located near Tema. The refinery refines the crude oil, bottles it, packs the bottles into cartons, and stores finished stock. Here supply chain management is visible inside one company: procurement buys crude oil, bottles, labels, and cartons; production planning decides how many litres to refine each week; logistics manages inbound supplies, warehousing, and outbound dispatch.

Step 4: The distributor. A distribution company buys cartons in bulk, stores them in a regional warehouse, and supplies retailers across a wide area. Its inbound logistics bring cartons from Tema; its outbound logistics deliver mixed orders to many shops.

Step 5: The retail shop. A shop in Accra orders cartons from the distributor, receives them, checks the quantities, and displays bottles for customers. The final customer buys one bottle and carries it home.

Step 6: Trace the flows. Product flowed downstream through five stages. Information travelled in both directions: the shop's order went upstream, while delivery notes and stock information followed the goods downstream. Finance flowed upstream: the customer's payment reached the shop, part of it passed to the distributor, and onward payments reached the refinery, the mill, and the farms.

What does this example demonstrate?

One inexpensive bottle depends on at least five businesses and dozens of activities. Logistics occurs at every boundary between the stages, and supply chain management is the view that connects them. No single participant could deliver the bottle to the customer alone.

Key lesson: every product on a shelf is the visible end of a chain of suppliers, producers, distributors, and retailers, held together by logistics and by information and money flowing in both directions.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: The same product through two lenses: a view from Rotterdam
BODY:
The difference between logistics and supply chain management becomes clearest when the same product is examined twice: once through a logistics lens and once through a supply chain lens. Consider an electric kitchen kettle sold by a supermarket chain in the Netherlands, displayed in a store near Rotterdam. The example is illustrative.

Step 1: The logistics view. Stand in the supermarket chain's national distribution centre. The logistics questions are concrete and immediate. How many kettles arrive from the importer, and when? How should they be stored? When stores in the region place orders, how are cartons picked, packed, loaded, and routed so that each store receives its allocation on schedule? Which vehicles and delivery rounds serve which stores? These are logistics management questions, and they belong to the part of the supply chain that plans, implements, and controls the flow and storage of goods.

Step 2: Widen to the supply chain view. Step back from the distribution centre and follow the kettle upstream. The importer buys finished kettles from an assembly plant overseas. That plant receives motors, thermostats, plastics, and switches from several component suppliers, and each component supplier has suppliers of its own, such as the producer of the plastic granules. Shipping lines carry the packed containers to a European port, where import formalities are completed before the goods move inland to the importer's warehouse. Each arrow in this picture is a decision as well as a movement: which assembly plant, which component suppliers, which shipping schedule, how many units to order for which season.

Step 3: Compare the two views. The logistics view asks how the retailer's own goods flow efficiently. The supply chain view asks who should make the kettle, where, from which bought components, and how all the companies from component suppliers to store shelves should coordinate their plans and information. The logistics view is contained within the supply chain view, roughly one stage of it, while supply chain management spans them all.

What does this example demonstrate?

Neither view is optional. The distribution centre cannot pick and ship reliably unless upstream decisions have placed the right kettles in the right country at the right time; and the best sourcing decisions in the world are worthless if the distribution centre fails to deliver to stores. Logistics and supply chain management answer different questions about the same product, and the answers only work together.

Key lesson: logistics is the part; supply chain management is the whole that the part serves.

=== WORKED EXAMPLE ===
HEADING: What coordination changes: two weeks of orders in Tamale
BODY:
This example compares the same distributor operating without and with coordination. The business distributes bottled cooking oil to small retail shops in Tamale, ordering cartons from the bottling plant. In a normal week the shops together sell about 100 cartons, and the distributor replenishes accordingly. All figures are illustrative for teaching purposes.

Scenario A: Without coordination.

Step 1: A rumour spreads in the market that a shortage of cooking oil is coming. No one checks whether it is true.

Step 2: Each shop orders more than usual, just in case. Together they order 250 cartons, two and a half times their real sales rate.

Step 3: The distributor sees orders jump, cannot see the shops' actual sales, and assumes demand has risen. To protect its own availability, it orders 400 cartons from the bottling plant and asks for urgent delivery.

Step 4: The bottling plant adds weekend shifts to meet the surge.

Step 5: The rumour proves false. Shop sales remain about 100 cartons a week. The shops stop reordering for weeks while they sell through the stock on their shelves; the distributor sits on a large surplus, its storage full and its money tied up in slow-moving stock; the plant, having raised output, is asked to cut back.

Scenario B: With coordination.

Step 1: The same rumour appears. Because the distributor and the shops have an established arrangement, the shops report their actual weekly sales, and the distributor can compare incoming orders against real demand.

Step 2: The distributor sees that sales are unchanged and shares this information with the shops and the plant, along with a request to keep to the normal replenishment schedule.

Step 3: Orders stay close to 100 cartons a week. No surplus is created, no shifts are added and cancelled, and availability is maintained.

What does this example demonstrate?

The difference between the scenarios is not transport, warehousing, or any single logistics activity; both scenarios involve the same physical operations. The difference is information and coordination. When participants decide in isolation, each sensible-looking decision inflates the next, and the chain ends up with too much, then too little. When participants share demand information and keep to agreed replenishment plans, orders track real sales, costs fall across the chain, and products remain available. This is the operational efficiency that coordination among supply chain participants produces.

Key lesson: in a supply chain, the quality of decisions made upstream and downstream together matters as much as the quality of any one company's logistics.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Supply chain management is not simply a larger name for logistics
BODY:
Because the two terms are related, beginners sometimes use them interchangeably, as if supply chain management were merely a modern word for logistics. The distinction matters in daily work.

Logistics management concerns the flow and storage of goods and the related information, from origin to consumption, to meet customers' requirements. Supply chain management includes all of that and adds sourcing, procurement, production planning, and coordination and collaboration with partners across company boundaries.

A quick test helps. If the question concerns where to buy, from whom, what to produce and in what quantity, or how several companies should align their plans, it is a supply chain management question. If the question concerns how particular goods should be moved, stored, handled, and delivered, it is a logistics question, even when it ranges across several countries.

The confusion is understandable, because the same person in a small company may do both, and because the terms overlap: logistics is a part of supply chain management, so every logistics activity is also part of the supply chain. But the reverse does not hold. Procurement, supplier selection, and production planning are supply chain activities that are not logistics activities, however the company organises them.

Remember: logistics is a component of supply chain management, not a synonym for it. Treating the two as identical leads companies to believe that efficient trucks and warehouses amount to a managed supply chain, which they do not.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: No single company controls the whole chain
BODY:
Learners sometimes picture a supply chain manager directing every participant: the farms, the mill, the refinery, the distributor, and the shops, like departments of one company. In reality, each stage is usually an independent business with its own owners, goals, and customers, and no participant owns the chain from end to end.

This has two practical consequences.

First, companies coordinate rather than command. A refinery cannot order a distributor to hold more stock; it can offer terms, share forecasts, agree service arrangements, and build the trust that makes cooperation worthwhile. Coordination between independent organisations rests on agreements, shared information, and mutual benefit, and that is why coordination skills are central to supply chain management.

Second, every participant runs its own logistics while depending on everyone else's. The distributor in Tamale manages its own warehouse and vehicles, but its ability to promise deliveries to shops depends on the bottling plant's production plan, and the plant depends in turn on the mill. A supply chain is therefore many companies' logistics operations joined end to end, with success shared rather than controlled.

One further consequence deserves note: a single business usually belongs to several supply chains at once. A shop in Accra sells cooking oil, soap, matches, and school notebooks, each arriving through a different chain of suppliers. Managing its logistics means serving all of these chains well at the same time.

=== IMPORTANT NOTE ===
HEADING: Goods also move back up the chain
BODY:
Everything described so far emphasises the downstream journey from raw material to customer, but goods also travel in the opposite direction, and a beginner should not mistake this for an exception.

Reverse logistics is the part of logistics management that plans and controls flows moving back toward their point of origin. Everyday examples appear at every stage of the cooking-oil chain: empty bottles and plastic crates returned by shops to the distributor for refilling; damaged cartons sent back for replacement or credit; used packaging collected for recycling and, at the end of a product's useful life, disposal or recovery.

Reverse flows are part of logistics, not outside it: descriptions of logistics management explicitly cover both forward and reverse flow and storage of goods. They are also part of supply chain management, because returns must be planned, transported, processed, and recorded just as outbound goods are, and the finance flow travels with them in the form of credits and refunds.

Two practical lessons follow. First, a supply chain is never strictly one-way: when you map a chain, look for what flows back as well as what flows forward. Second, reverse flows have costs and value: handling returns poorly damages customer confidence, while recovering bottles, crates, and materials can reduce waste and cost for every participant. Reverse logistics receives fuller treatment later in this course; at this stage it is enough to recognise that the chain carries traffic in both directions.

=== MCQ 1 ===
QUESTION:
A manager at a bottling company in Accra spends part of her week negotiating bottle prices with a packaging supplier, planning how many litres to produce, and coordinating delivery schedules with the company's distributors. The company's logistics team, meanwhile, manages the warehouses and the delivery vehicles.

Which statement best describes the difference between the manager's supply chain management work and the logistics team's work?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Supply chain management and logistics are two names for the same activities, and companies simply choose whichever term they prefer.
2. Supply chain management covers sourcing, procurement, production planning, and coordination across companies, while logistics focuses on the movement and storage of goods within that wider scope.
3. Logistics manages the activities of suppliers and customers, while supply chain management manages only the company's own warehouses and vehicles.
4. Supply chain management replaces the need for logistics once a company grows large enough to work with other businesses.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

Supply chain management is the broader discipline: it covers sourcing and procurement, such as negotiating with the packaging supplier; production planning, such as deciding how much to produce; logistics management; and coordination with partners such as the distributors. Logistics is that part of supply chain management which plans, implements, and controls the movement and storage of goods, which is exactly the warehousing and transport work the logistics team performs.

Answer 1 fails because the two terms are not interchangeable: logistics is a component of supply chain management, not an alternative name for it. Answer 3 reverses the relationship: logistics chiefly manages a company's own flows, while supply chain management is what reaches across companies to suppliers and customers. Answer 4 is wrong because supply chain management never replaces logistics at any stage of growth; it depends on logistics to execute its plans.

Remember: supply chain management decides and coordinates across the chain; logistics moves and stores the goods within that plan.

=== MCQ 2 ===
QUESTION:
Shops in Tamale normally sell a steady total of about 100 cartons of cooking oil each week. When a rumour of an approaching shortage spreads, every shop places a much larger order, and the distributor, seeing the larger orders, increases its own order to the bottling plant. The rumour soon proves false.

Which option best explains how coordination among the supply chain participants would have improved this situation?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Coordinated sharing of actual sales information and agreed replenishment schedules would have kept orders close to real demand, avoiding excess stock, wasted production, and a later swing to shortage.
2. Nothing needed improving, because larger orders prove that customers want more products and the chain should always follow them.
3. The problem was caused by slow transportation and could have been avoided by arranging faster vehicles.
4. Coordination among the participants could have prevented the rumour from ever arising in the market.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

The shops ordered 250 cartons against real sales of about 100, and the distributor magnified the swing further by ordering 400 from the bottling plant. Each participant acted on limited information and fear of shortage, so orders inflated as they moved upstream. Coordination, meaning shared sales information and replenishment on agreed schedules, keeps orders close to real demand and prevents the surplus, the wasted production, and the later swing to shortage.

Answer 2 fails because the larger orders did not reflect more customers; they reflected uncertainty, and following them blindly created surplus stock. Answer 3 mislocates the problem: the same vehicles and warehouses performed well enough in the coordinated scenario, because transportation speed was not the cause. Answer 4 overstates what coordination can do: sharing information exposes a rumour quickly, but no participant can stop rumours from arising.

The takeaway: when supply chain participants share demand information and coordinate replenishment, the whole chain avoids the costly swing between too much stock and too little.

=== MCQ 3 ===
QUESTION:
In the chain that moves a bottle of cooking oil from farms to a shop in Accra, the distributor keeps accurate stock records, plans deliveries carefully, and confirms each shop's order before dispatch.

Which statement best describes the role these logistics activities play in the wider supply chain?
POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. They matter only to the distributor and have no effect on the farms, the mill, or the bottling plant.
2. They are logistics activities, so they sit outside supply chain management.
3. They turn the supply chain's plans into reliable product availability, supporting the performance of every participant that depends on timely, complete deliveries.
4. They remove the need for coordination among the chain's participants, because good execution is sufficient on its own.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

Accurate stock records, careful delivery planning, and order confirmation are logistics activities, and in a supply chain they become the next participant's supply reliability. Because the distributor executes its logistics well, the shops can trust their replenishment, the shelves stay stocked, and the bottling plant and mill face steadier orders. This is how logistics supports supply chain performance: it turns the chain's plans into products that are actually available.

Answer 1 is wrong because the distributor's logistics shape the experience of every other participant: poor records upstream become empty shelves downstream and erratic orders at the plant. Answer 2 misunderstands the relationship: logistics sits inside supply chain management as one of its components, not outside it. The takeaway: a chain performs only as well as its links execute, and each participant's logistics becomes its neighbours' supply.

=== FACTS & SOURCES ===
- Logistics management is defined as that part of supply chain management which plans, implements, and controls the efficient forward and reverse flow and storage of goods, services, and related information between the point of origin and the point of consumption, in order to meet customers' requirements. — source: research sheet logistics-scm-frameworks.md, section 1 (CSCMP definition).
- Supply chain management encompasses the planning and management of all activities involved in sourcing and procurement, conversion, and all logistics management activities, including coordination and collaboration with channel partners (suppliers, intermediaries, third-party providers, customers), and integrates supply and demand management within and across companies. — source: research sheet logistics-scm-frameworks.md, section 1 (CSCMP definition).
- Logistics management sits within supply chain management; logistics is a subset of supply chain management. — source: research sheet logistics-scm-frameworks.md, section 1 (CSCMP framing).
- Typical logistics activities include inbound and outbound transportation, warehousing, materials handling, order fulfilment, network design, inventory management, and management of third-party logistics providers, with sourcing, procurement, production planning, packaging and assembly, and customer service added to varying degrees by different organisations. — source: research sheet logistics-scm-frameworks.md, section 1 (CSCMP list of logistics activities).
- Inbound logistics refers to supplier flows into a business, outbound logistics to finished-goods flows to customers, and reverse logistics to flows back toward origin (returns, repairs, recycling, disposal); reverse logistics sits inside logistics management because the definition explicitly covers forward and reverse flow. — source: research sheet logistics-scm-frameworks.md, section 1.
- A freight forwarder is an intermediary that arranges international shipment for shippers, booking carriage, consolidating cargo, and preparing documentation, without usually operating the vessels or aircraft. — source: research sheet logistics-scm-frameworks.md, section 1.
- The basic procurement flow runs from approved purchase requisition to purchase order, then goods receipt checked against the order, then supplier invoice and payment. — source: research sheet logistics-scm-frameworks.md, section 3.
- Order variability can amplify upstream through a supply chain when participants order on limited information, and countermeasures include information sharing and smaller, more frequent, coordinated replenishment. — source: research sheet logistics-scm-frameworks.md, section 8 (bullwhip effect; causes established by Lee, Padmanabhan and Whang, 1997).
- The descriptions of supply chain participants and tiers (suppliers of suppliers to customers of customers), upstream and downstream terminology, and the product, information, and finance flows are standard supply chain teaching concepts. — source: general business knowledge.

The following are proposed replacements, in "Term :: definition" format, drawn from the terms actually defined and used in this draft.)

Supply Chain :: The network of organisations, people, activities, information, and resources involved in producing and delivering a product, from the earliest suppliers of materials to the final customer.
Supply Chain Management :: The planning and management of all activities involved in sourcing, procurement, production, and logistics, together with coordination and collaboration with the partners that make up the supply chain, so that supply and demand are managed together within and across companies.
Procurement :: The process of obtaining the goods and services a business needs from suppliers, including preparing requisitions and purchase orders, receiving the goods, checking them against the order, and processing the supplier's invoice for payment.
Upstream :: The direction in a supply chain toward the original sources of materials and components; from any participant, its suppliers are upstream.
Downstream :: The direction in a supply chain toward the final customer; from any participant, its distributors, retailers, and end customers are downstream.
Information Flow :: The movement of orders, forecasts, stock records, delivery instructions, and shipment updates between supply chain participants, travelling in both directions and allowing each stage to plan on facts rather than guesses.
Integration :: Making separate activities and separate companies work to one coordinated plan across the supply chain, so that supply and demand are managed together rather than in isolation.
