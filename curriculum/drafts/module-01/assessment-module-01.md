=== ASSESSMENT Q1 | tests lesson 1.1 ===
QUESTION:
The owner of a transport business in Takoradi tells a new manager: "We already do logistics. We own twelve trucks and employ drivers, so nothing more is needed." In its first year the business delivers on time only occasionally: orders are misrecorded, the warehouse cannot find stock, and customers receive no confirmation of delivery.

Which option best evaluates the owner's reasoning?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The reasoning is correct, because owning suitable vehicles and employing drivers is the main requirement of a logistics operation.
2. The reasoning is correct for domestic deliveries, though international deliveries would also require ships and aircraft.
3. The reasoning is flawed, because transportation is only one activity within logistics, which also requires planning, accurate records, warehousing, order processing, and coordination; vehicles alone have not prevented the business's failures.
4. The reasoning is flawed only because twelve trucks are too few; a larger fleet would remove the recording and storage problems.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The lesson defines logistics as the planning, implementation, coordination, and control of the movement and storage of goods, together with the related information and resources. Transportation is one activity inside that system, not the whole of it. The business's actual failures — misrecorded orders, unusable inventory records, and missing delivery confirmation — are failures of order processing, information management, warehousing, and coordination, and no fleet size can fix them.

Answer 1 restates the exact misconception the lesson dismantles: a company may own several vehicles and still experience poor logistics performance if its inventory records are inaccurate, deliveries are poorly scheduled, or warehouse operations are disorganised. Answer 2 is wrong because domestic and international logistics both require the wider activities; the difference between them is the crossing of national borders with its extra documentation and coordination, not the presence or absence of planning. Answer 4 doubles down on the same error, treating recording and storage problems as fleet problems.

The takeaway: transportation moves goods, but logistics coordinates the planning, storage, information, and control that make movement and availability possible. Vehicles are a resource, not the discipline.

=== ASSESSMENT Q2 | tests lesson 1.1 ===
QUESTION:
A beverage distributor in Accra delivers 100 cartons of soft drinks to a retailer in Koforidua. The truck reaches the shop on the agreed date, but the retailer had ordered 120 cartons, and eight of the delivered cartons arrived crushed.

Which option best describes this delivery from the logistics point of view?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. A service failure, because goods that physically reach the customer have still not met the customer's requirements if the quantity is short or the goods are damaged; movement alone is not successful logistics.
2. A complete success, because the goods reached the correct town on the agreed date.
3. A partial success that should be recorded as satisfactory, because the majority of the order was delivered intact.
4. A warehouse problem only, because shortages and crushed cartons are counting matters that do not involve the logistics operation.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

The lesson is explicit that a shipment reaching its destination does not automatically mean the logistics operation was successful. Performance must be considered against the requirements of the transaction: product accuracy, quantity, condition, destination, delivery time, operational cost, and reliable information. Here the quantity is short (100 against 120) and the condition is unacceptable (eight crushed cartons), so the operation fell short even though movement took place on schedule.

Answer 2 commits the basic error of equating movement with success; the delivery missed requirements the customer genuinely cares about. Answer 3 reflects the habit of judging a delivery by impressions or percentages; the lesson's position is that customers experience a delivery as a whole, measured against their requirements, and a short, partly damaged order does not become acceptable because most units survived. Answer 4 treats quantity and condition as clerical matters isolated from logistics, but the lesson shows the opposite: these factors are connected parts of one organised process, and a discrepancy must be investigated rather than filed away.

The takeaway: successful logistics means meeting the required quantity, condition, destination, time, cost, and information — not simply moving goods to a destination.

=== ASSESSMENT Q3 | tests lesson 1.1 ===
QUESTION:
A building-materials merchant in Tamale has 300 bags of cement in stock, and the delivery documents for a construction site are complete and accurate. During the rainy season, however, no suitable truck can be hired for the next three days, so the delivery cannot be made as planned.

In terms of the coordination of goods, information, and resources, which option best explains the situation?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The delivery should proceed, because correct goods and accurate documents are sufficient; transport problems are outside logistics.
2. The delivery is delayed because a required resource is missing: even with the goods available and the information accurate, a delivery needs suitable transport, and all three elements must be in place together.
3. The delay is an information failure, because the documents should have guaranteed that a vehicle would be available.
4. The delay proves the merchant holds the wrong inventory, because cement should never be stored before it is sold.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The lesson teaches that effective logistics depends on coordinating three elements: the goods, the information about them, and the resources that move and handle them. The cement is the goods; the complete, accurate documents are the information; the missing suitable truck is the absent resource. The lesson's own example makes precisely this point: if the goods are available but no suitable truck can be arranged, delivery may fail. All three elements must be in place at the same time.

Answer 3 mislocates the problem. The information in this scenario is accurate; documents describe the delivery, they do not summon a vehicle, so the failure sits with resources, not information. Answer 1 removes transport from logistics altogether, contradicting the definition of logistics as the coordinated movement and storage of goods; transportation is one of the major logistics activities. Answer 4 confuses the issue: holding stock before sale is normal distribution practice, and the lesson presents inventory availability as something to be managed and coordinated, not as an error in itself.

The takeaway: goods, information, and resources form one system — a weakness in any single element can stop a delivery that is otherwise ready to go.

=== ASSESSMENT Q4 | tests lesson 1.2 ===
QUESTION:
A bottling plant buys cartons from a packaging manufacturer. That manufacturer, in turn, buys its paper from a paper mill, which buys from pulp suppliers.

From the bottling plant's point of view, how should the paper mill be classified?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. A first-tier supplier, because its paper ends up inside the final cartons.
2. A downstream customer, because paper moves in the direction of the final product.
3. A service provider, because it supports the chain without owning the bottled product.
4. A second-tier supplier, because it supplies the bottling plant's first-tier supplier, the packaging manufacturer.
CORRECT: 4
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 4.

The lesson defines tiers from the viewpoint of the buying company: the packaging manufacturer, which sells directly to the bottling plant, is the plant's first-tier supplier, and the paper mill, which supplies that first-tier supplier, is a second-tier supplier. When managers speak of the whole supply chain, they mean this full stretch, from the suppliers of their suppliers to the customers of their customers.

Answer 1 rests on the misconception that the tier is decided by whether the material ends up inside the product; tier is decided by the number of steps between the supplier and the buyer, not by where the material physically ends up. Answer 2 gets the direction wrong: the paper mill is upstream of the bottling plant, because upstream means toward the original sources of materials, while downstream means toward the final customer. Answer 3 confuses suppliers with service providers; the lesson reserves that term for businesses such as transport companies, warehouse operators, and freight forwarders, which support the chain without owning the goods. The paper mill owns and sells the paper it produces.

The takeaway: every participant is both a customer and a supplier, and tiers are counted in steps from your own position in the chain.

=== ASSESSMENT Q5 | tests lesson 1.2 ===
QUESTION:
A distributor delivers cartons of cooking oil to shops reliably every week, so the product flow works well. However, its invoices and delivery notes are repeatedly late or show wrong quantities. Shops delay payment because they cannot verify what they owe, and some begin to dispute the delivered quantities as well.

Using the three flows of a supply chain, which option best explains this situation?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The finance flow has failed, but the product and information flows remain healthy, so the chain's overall performance is unaffected.
2. The three flows are inseparable: the inaccurate information flow has disrupted the finance flow and is now eroding trust in the product flow as well.
3. Only the product flow matters in a supply chain, because goods that arrive on time define performance; paperwork failures are irrelevant.
4. Invoices belong to logistics while payments belong to the supply chain, so the two difficulties are unrelated and should be managed separately.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The lesson presents product, information, and finance flows moving through every supply chain, and states plainly that the three flows are inseparable in practice: if any one of the three fails, the other two eventually fail with it. Its own illustration is almost identical to this scenario — goods delivered without accurate records cannot be paid for promptly. Here the information flow, carried by invoices and delivery notes, is faulty, so the finance flow stalls, and the shops' growing disputes now threaten confidence in a product flow that is physically performing well.

Answer 1 treats the flows as independent pipes, which is the error the lesson corrects; a product flow cannot stay healthy for long while the records that justify payment remain wrong. Answer 3 dismisses two of the three flows, but the lesson calls information the chain's nervous system: when it is late or wrong, each participant plans on guesses. Answer 4 invents a dividing line between logistics paperwork and supply chain money; records and payments are part of one connected set of flows among the same participants, not separate worlds.

The takeaway: goods, information, and money must all keep moving accurately, because each flow eventually carries the other two with it.

=== ASSESSMENT Q6 | tests lesson 1.2 ===
QUESTION:
A bottling plant expects strong demand before a festive period and wants its independent distributors in Kumasi and Takoradi to hold more stock during the season.

Based on this module, which option best describes how the plant should pursue that goal?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. By sharing its demand forecast with the distributors, agreeing replenishment arrangements in writing, and offering terms that make holding extra stock worthwhile, because independent companies coordinate rather than command.
2. By instructing the distributors to raise their stock levels immediately, because a producer directs the other members of its supply chain like departments of one company.
3. By shipping double quantities to the distributors without notice, because the goods will be needed eventually and arriving early is always welcome.
4. By keeping all extra stock in the plant's own warehouse, because what independent distributors choose to hold is not the producer's concern.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

The lesson's important note is direct: no single company controls the whole chain, so companies coordinate rather than command. A refinery — or a bottling plant — cannot order an independent distributor to hold more stock; it can offer terms, share forecasts, agree service arrangements, and build the trust that makes cooperation worthwhile. Coordination between independent organisations rests on agreements, shared information, and mutual benefit, and those are exactly the means listed in Answer 1.

Answer 2 expresses the beginner's picture of a supply chain manager directing every participant like a department of one company; in reality each stage is an independent business with its own owners, goals, and customers. Answer 3 imposes stock by surprise, ignoring agreed schedules and creating the swings between too much and too little that uncoordinated chains suffer; the Tamale example shows how unilateral, fear-driven quantities destabilise a whole chain. Answer 4 is isolation rather than coordination: when participants decide separately, orders stop tracking real demand and every participant bears the cost.

The takeaway: between independent companies, alignment is built from forecasts, agreements, and trust — never from commands.

=== ASSESSMENT Q7 | tests lesson 1.3 ===
QUESTION:
Two deliveries go wrong in the same market. In the first, goods intended for a provisions shop are offloaded at the neighbouring boutique, whose name is similar, although the street and building on the delivery note were correct. In the second, goods ordered by a trader are delivered to the premises the trader occupied before relocating, although the documents named the correct recipient.

Which option correctly identifies which of the seven rights failed in each case?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The first failed the right place, and the second failed the right customer.
2. Both deliveries failed the right place only, because both involve addresses.
3. The first failed the right customer, and the second failed the right place.
4. Neither delivery is a service failure, because in each case the goods arrived in the customer's general area.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The lesson separates the two rights precisely: the right place concerns the destination — the premises, site, gate, dock, or branch where the goods belong — while the right customer concerns the recipient, the person or organisation entitled to take receipt. The lesson's two illustrations match this scenario exactly: goods offloaded at the neighbouring shop are in the hands of a business that never ordered them, so the recipient failed even though the location was essentially correct; goods sent to an old address have the right recipient but the wrong place, and sit where the customer can no longer receive them.

Answer 1 swaps the two, which is the exact confusion the note exists to dismantle. Answer 2 merges the rights into a single idea of delivering to the right address, and so cannot explain a case where one right passed and the other failed. Answer 4 applies a standard of the general area, but each right is a specific, checkable requirement: any single miss makes the whole delivery a service failure in the customer's eyes, however near the goods landed.

The takeaway: place and customer are separate checks — confirm the destination and the entitled recipient, and confirm both before offloading.

=== ASSESSMENT Q8 | tests lesson 1.3 ===
QUESTION:
A supplier delivers 60 boxes of fresh fish to a hotel in Accra one day before the agreed date. The hotel's cold room will not be free until the next day, so the fish spends the night in an ordinary storeroom and part of it deteriorates.

Against the seven rights, which option best identifies what failed?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The right condition failed, because the fish deteriorated; the delivery date has no bearing once the goods left the supplier in good state.
2. The right time failed, because the delivery did not match the customer's ability to receive and protect the goods; earliness is also a timing failure, and it caused the deterioration.
3. The right quantity failed, because the hotel could use only part of the fish delivered.
4. No right failed, because delivering ahead of the agreed date is always a service improvement.
CORRECT: 2
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 2.

The lesson teaches that the right time means delivering goods when they are required, which is not the same as delivering them as early as possible, and it warns that earliness can also be a failure. Its example is nearly identical to this scenario: fresh fish delivered a day before the customer's cold room is available begins to deteriorate, and goods that arrive before a site can receive them must be stored, guarded, or handled twice. Here the hotel could not receive and protect the fish, so the timing, not the transport, broke first.

Answer 1 names the visible symptom but misreads the root cause: the condition was protectable, and it failed because the delivery ignored the customer's readiness; the lesson insists that condition is planned across the entire journey, including the receiving point. Answer 3 is simply wrong on the facts — the quantity ordered was the quantity delivered. Answer 4 states the exact beginner assumption the lesson overturns: delivering early is acceptable only when the customer can receive and protect the goods.

The takeaway: the right time is defined by the customer's schedule and readiness, and a delivery can fail just as surely for being too early as for being late.

=== ASSESSMENT Q9 | tests lesson 1.3 ===
QUESTION:
A trainee reading an older textbook finds a list of five rights of purchasing — quality, quantity, time, place, and price — and becomes worried that the seven rights taught in this course must therefore be wrong.

Which option best resolves the trainee's concern?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. The textbook is wrong, because one official international standard fixes a single list of the rights that all training must follow.
2. The course is wrong, because only lists of exactly five rights are used in professional practice.
3. The two lists describe unrelated ideas, because the rights of purchasing never apply to logistics work.
4. Neither list is wrong, because the rights are a professional convention rather than an official standard, and versions differ; the trainee should identify which version a text uses, while this course uses product, quantity, condition, place, time, customer, and cost consistently.
CORRECT: 4
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 4.

The lesson's important note states that the seven rights are a convention that has developed across logistics textbooks and training programmes, not a law or an official standard issued by an authority, and that no single organisation owns the list. Versions differ: some materials list five rights of purchasing, some speak of the right price instead of the right cost, and some add items such as right documentation or right way. Every version expresses the same underlying idea — a delivery must match what the customer ordered, in the amount and state required, at the destination and time required, for a recipient entitled to use it, at an economic cost. The professional response is the one in Answer 4: recognise which version is in use, adapt the checklist to it, and learn this course's seven-item list first because lesson questions and assessments use it consistently.

Answer 1 invents an official standard that the lesson explicitly denies exists. Answer 2 makes the opposite error, treating one variant as the only valid form. Answer 3 wrongly severs the lists from each other; the lesson presents the variants as overlapping expressions of a single idea, not unrelated concepts.

The takeaway: the rights are a shared professional checklist, not a statute — learn this course's version, then read other versions with confidence.

=== ASSESSMENT Q10 | tests lesson 1.4 ===
QUESTION:
A furniture factory in Kumasi depends on regular deliveries of timber, nails, glue, and varnish from its suppliers. When a varnish delivery arrives a week late, the finishing line stands idle for three days, and completed furniture cannot be dispatched to customers on schedule.

Which option best analyses this failure?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Inbound logistics failed its purpose of continuity: materials that arrive late idle production, and the disruption then travels inward through the business and disturbs the outbound flow to customers.
2. Outbound logistics failed alone, because the missed customer deliveries are the only real problem; late supplier deliveries are not a logistics concern.
3. Reverse logistics failed, because the disruption moved backwards from customers toward the factory.
4. Third-party logistics failed, because the suppliers are external organisations rather than departments of the factory.
CORRECT: 1
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 1.

Inbound logistics is the management of goods and materials moving into an organisation, and the lesson defines its purpose as continuity: it is the discipline that prevents the bakery-without-flour situation. The lesson's own warning matches this scenario — materials that arrive late can idle a production line, and a weakness in inbound logistics does not stay contained; it travels inward and disturbs production, which in turn disturbs the outbound flow. The three-day idle line and the late customer dispatches are one connected failure, beginning at the factory's inbound boundary.

Answer 2 treats the visible end of the problem as its cause; outbound logistics could not dispatch on time precisely because the inbound supply failed, and calling supplier deliveries a matter outside logistics contradicts the lesson, which counts ordering, receiving, inspection, and put-away among inbound activities. Answer 3 misapplies reverse logistics, which describes goods physically flowing back through the chain for returns, repair, reuse, recycling, or disposal; a disruption spreading through a business is not a reverse flow of goods. Answer 4 confuses the categories: third-party logistics describes who performs logistics activities as an outsourced service, not the mere fact that a business buys materials from outside suppliers.

The takeaway: inbound logistics protects production continuity, and its failures always resurface as outbound failures.

=== ASSESSMENT Q11 | tests lesson 1.4 ===
QUESTION:
At a returns centre, four returned items are inspected and graded: an electric kettle graded as new and fit for resale; a fan graded as repairable; a batch of used packaging graded as usable for material recovery; and expired stock graded as fit only for documented disposal.

Which option best describes the principle that decides where each item goes next?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Every returned item is returned to sellable stock, because a return means the goods are by definition resalable.
2. Every returned item is sent for documented disposal, because goods that come back can no longer be trusted for any other purpose.
3. Each item follows its grade — resale, repair, material recovery, or documented disposal — so that the operation recovers the most value each item still holds, with disposal only as the last resort.
4. Each item is routed to whichever destination is cheapest to reach, because the condition of returned goods is not worth assessing.
CORRECT: 3
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 3.

The lesson's returns-management sequence ends in grading and disposition: items are graded at inspection — as new and fit for resale, repairable or refurbishable, usable for parts or material recovery, or fit only for documented disposal — and routed accordingly, while the customer receives the agreed outcome such as a replacement, a repair, or a refund. The stated purpose of reverse logistics is to recover value and meet obligations, and the important note adds the ordering of that recovery: resale first, then repair, then recovery of materials, and disposal only when nothing else is possible.

Answer 1 assumes every return is resalable, which is exactly what grading exists to test; a worn, damaged, or expired item resold as new would create a worse failure than the original return. Answer 2 reverses the value logic the lesson teaches, treating disposal — the last resort — as the default, and would waste the kettle, the fan, and the recoverable packaging. Answer 4 ignores condition entirely, but the whole point of receiving and inspection is that disposition depends on what each item actually is.

The takeaway: returns are managed, not merely accepted — grading routes each item to the outcome that recovers the most value.

=== ASSESSMENT Q12 | tests lesson 1.4 ===
QUESTION:
A company with a complicated supply chain spanning several countries engages a coordinator to run its logistics. The coordinator owns no trucks and operates no warehouses. It selects and supervises several transport companies and warehouse operators, connects their work through shared information systems, and answers for the client's logistics performance from end to end.

Which option best identifies this arrangement?

POINTS: 1
OPTIONS:
1. Third-party logistics, because any external logistics organisation a company engages counts as a third party.
2. In-house logistics, because the provider owns no vehicles or warehouses, so the company is effectively still running its own logistics.
3. Reverse logistics, because the engagement begins after the goods have already been delivered to customers.
4. Fourth-party logistics, which manages and integrates the client's entire logistics operation, including several third-party providers, without typically owning logistics assets itself.
CORRECT: 4
EXPLANATION:
The correct answer is Answer 4.

The lesson defines a fourth-party logistics provider, or 4PL, as a coordinator that manages an entire logistics operation on the client's behalf: it typically owns no vehicles or warehouses, but selects, engages, and supervises several third-party providers, integrates their work with technology and shared information, and takes responsibility for the client's logistics performance from end to end. The lesson's memory aid fits exactly: a 3PL operates logistics activities for you, while a 4PL integrates and manages the whole network, including the 3PLs working within it.

Answer 1 is the attractive trap: the provider is certainly external, but mere externality does not settle the category. A 3PL performs activities such as trucking, warehousing, or forwarding; this provider performs none of them — it manages the performers, which is precisely the 4PL pattern. Answer 2 treats asset ownership as the test of in-house logistics, but the lesson shows that a business can buy a complete logistics package and own no trucks or warehouse at all; the test is who performs and manages the work, not who owns the assets. Answer 3 misreads the word reverse: reverse logistics describes goods flowing backwards through the chain for returns, repair, reuse, recycling, or disposal, not a contract that begins later in time.

The takeaway: ask two separate questions — in which direction do the goods flow, and who performs or manages the work — and the categories sort themselves.
